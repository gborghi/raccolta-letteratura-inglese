One morning the old [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat put his head out of his hole. He had bright beady eyes and stiff grey whiskers and his tail was like a long bit of black india-rubber. The little ducks were swimming about in the pond, looking just like a lot of yellow canaries, and their mother, who was pure white with real red legs, was trying to teach them how to stand on their heads in the [[motifs/water|water]].
“You will never be in the best society unless you can stand on your heads,” she kept saying to them; and every now and then she showed them how it was done. But the little ducks paid no attention to her. They were so young that they did not know what an advantage it is to be in society at all.
“What disobedient children!” cried the old [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat; “they really deserve to be drowned.”
“Nothing of the kind,” answered the Duck, “every one must make a beginning, and parents cannot be too patient.”
“Ah! I know nothing about the feelings of parents,” said the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat; “I am not a family man. In fact, I have never been married, and I never intend to be. [[motifs/love|Love]] is all very [[motifs/well|well]] in its way, but [[concepts/friendship|friendship]] is much higher. Indeed, I know of nothing in the world that is either nobler or rarer than a devoted [[concepts/friendship|friendship]].”
“And what, pray, is your idea of the duties of a devoted friend?” asked a green Linnet, who was sitting in a willow-tree hard by, and had overheard the conversation.
“Yes, that is just what I want to know,” said the Duck; and she swam away to the end of the pond, and stood upon her head, in order to give her children a good example.
“What a silly question!” cried the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat. “I should expect my devoted friend to be devoted to me, of course.”
“And what would you do in return?” said the little bird, swinging upon a silver spray, and flapping his tiny wings.
“I don’t understand you,” answered the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“Let me tell you a story on the subject,” said the Linnet.
“Is the story about me?” asked the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.  “If so, I will listen to it, for I am extremely fond of fiction.”
“It is applicable to you,” answered the Linnet; and he flew down, and alighting upon the bank, he told the story of The Devoted Friend.
“Once upon a [[motifs/time|time]],” said the Linnet, “there was an honest little fellow named Hans.”
“Was he very distinguished?” asked the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“No,” answered the Linnet, “I don’t think he was distinguished at all, except for his kind heart, and his funny round good-humoured face. He lived in a tiny cottage all by himself, and every day he worked in his [[motifs/garden|garden]]. In all the country-side there was no [[motifs/garden|garden]] so lovely as his. Sweet-william grew there, and Gilly-[[motifs/flowers|flowers]], and Shepherds’-purses, and Fair-maids of France. There were damask Roses, and yellow Roses, lilac Crocuses and  gold, purple Violets and white. Columbine and Ladysmock, Marjoram and Wild Basil, the Cowslip and the Flower-de-luce, the Daffodil and the Clove-Pink bloomed or blossomed in their proper order as the months went by, one flower taking another flower’s place, so that there were always beautiful things to look at, and pleasant odours to smell.
THE GREEN LINNET
“[[characters/little-hans|Little Hans]] had a great many friends, but the most devoted friend of all was big [[characters/hugh-the-miller|Hugh the Miller]]. Indeed, so devoted was the rich Miller to [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], that he would never go by his [[motifs/garden|garden]] without leaning over the wall and plucking a large nosegay, or a handful of sweet herbs, or filling his pockets with plums and cherries if it was the fruit season.
“‘Real friends should have everything in common,’ the Miller used to say, and [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] nodded and smiled, and felt very proud of having a friend with such noble ideas.
“Sometimes, indeed, the neighbours thought it strange that the rich Miller never gave [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] anything in return, though he had a hundred sacks of flour stored away in his mill, and six milch cows, and a large flock of woolly sheep; but Hans never troubled his head about these things, and nothing gave him greater pleasure than to listen to all the wonderful things the Miller used to say about the unselfishness of true [[concepts/friendship|friendship]].
“So [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] worked away in his [[motifs/garden|garden]]. During the [[motifs/spring|spring]], the [[motifs/summer|summer]], and the [[motifs/autumn|autumn]] he was very happy, but when the [[motifs/winter|winter]] came, and he had no fruit or [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] to bring to the market, he suffered a good deal from cold and hunger, and often had to go to bed without any supper but a few dried pears or some hard nuts. In the [[motifs/winter|winter]], also, he was extremely lonely, as the Miller never came to see him then.
“‘There is no good in my going to see [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] as long as the [[motifs/snow|snow]] lasts,’ the Miller used to say to his wife, ‘for when people are in trouble they should be left alone and not be bothered by visitors. That at least is my idea about [[concepts/friendship|friendship]], and I am sure I am right. So I shall wait till the [[motifs/spring|spring]] comes, and then I shall pay him a visit, and he will be able to give me a large basket of primroses, and that will make him so happy.’
“‘You are certainly very thoughtful about others,’ answered the Wife, as she sat in her comfortable armchair by the big pinewood [[motifs/fire|fire]]; ‘very thoughtful indeed. It is quite a treat to hear you talk about [[concepts/friendship|friendship]]. I am sure the clergyman himself could not say such beautiful things as you do, though he does live in a three-storied house, and wear a gold ring on his little finger.’
“‘But could we not ask [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] up  here?’ said the Miller’s youngest son. ‘If poor Hans is in trouble I will give him half my porridge, and show him my white rabbits.’
“‘What a silly boy you are!’ cried the Miller; ‘I really don’t know what is the use of sending you to school. You seem not to learn anything. Why, if [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] came up here, and saw our warm [[motifs/fire|fire]], and our good supper, and our great cask of red wine, he might get envious, and envy is a most terrible thing, and would spoil anybody’s [[concepts/nature|nature]]. I certainly will not allow Hans’ [[concepts/nature|nature]] to be spoiled. I am his best friend, and I will always watch over him, and see that he is not led into any temptations. Besides, if Hans came here, he might ask me to let him have some flour on credit, and that I could not do. Flour is one thing and [[concepts/friendship|friendship]] is another, and they should not be confused. Why, the words are spelt differently, and mean  quite different things. Everybody can see that.’
“‘How [[motifs/well|well]] you talk!’ said the Miller’s Wife, pouring herself out a large glass of warm ale; ‘really I feel quite drowsy. It is just like being in church.’
“‘Lots of people act [[motifs/well|well]],’ answered the Miller; ‘but very few people talk [[motifs/well|well]], which shows that talking is much the more difficult thing of the two, and much the finer thing also’; and he looked sternly across the table at his little son, who felt so ashamed of himself that he hung his head down, and grew quite scarlet, and began to cry into his tea. However, he was so young that you must excuse him.”
“Is that the end of the story?” asked the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“Certainly not,” answered the Linnet, “that is the beginning.”
“Then you are quite behind the age,” said  the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat. “Every good story-teller nowadays starts with the end, and then goes on to the beginning, and concludes with the middle. That is the new method. I heard all about it the other day from a critic who was walking round the pond with a young man. He spoke of the matter at great length, and I am sure he must have been right, for he had blue spectacles and a bald head, and whenever the young man made any remark, he always answered ‘Pooh!’ But pray go on with your story. I like the Miller immensely. I have all kinds of beautiful sentiments myself, so there is a great sympathy between us.”
“[[motifs/well|Well]],” said the Linnet, hopping now on one leg and now on the other, “as soon as the [[motifs/winter|winter]] was over, and the primroses  began to open their pale yellow [[motifs/stars|stars]], the Miller said to his wife that he would go down and see [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]].
“‘Why, what a good heart you have!’ cried his Wife; ‘you are always thinking of others. And mind you take the big basket with you for the [[motifs/flowers|flowers]].’
“So the Miller tied the sails of the windmill together with a strong iron chain, and went down the [[motifs/hill|hill]] with the basket on his arm.
“‘Good morning, [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]],’ said the Miller.
“‘Good morning,’ said Hans, leaning on his spade, and smiling from ear to ear.
“‘And how have you been all the [[motifs/winter|winter]]?’ said the Miller.
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], really,’ cried Hans, ‘it is very good of you to ask, very good indeed. I am afraid I had rather a hard [[motifs/time|time]] of it, but now the [[motifs/spring|spring]] has come, and I am quite happy, and all my [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] are doing [[motifs/well|well]].’
“‘We often talked of you during the [[motifs/winter|winter]], Hans,’ said the Miller, ‘and wondered how you were getting on.’
“‘That was kind of you,’ said Hans; ‘I was half afraid you had forgotten me.’
“‘Hans, I am surprised at you,’ said the Miller; ‘[[concepts/friendship|friendship]] never forgets. That is the wonderful thing about it, but I am afraid you don’t understand the poetry of life. How lovely your primroses are looking, by-the-bye!’
“‘They are certainly very lovely,’ said Hans, ‘and it is a most lucky thing for me that I have so many. I am going to bring them into the market and sell them to the Burgomaster’s daughter, and buy back my wheelbarrow with the money.’
“‘Buy back your wheelbarrow? You don’t mean to say you have sold it? What a very stupid thing to do!’
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], the fact is,’ said Hans, ‘that I was obliged to. You see the [[motifs/winter|winter]] was a very bad [[motifs/time|time]] for me, and I really had no money at all to buy bread with. So I first sold the silver buttons off my [[characters/sunday|Sunday]] coat, and then I sold my silver chain, and then I sold my big pipe, and at last I sold my wheelbarrow. But I am going to buy them all back again now.’
“‘Hans,’ said the Miller, ‘I will give you my wheelbarrow. It is not in very good repair; indeed, one side is gone, and there is something wrong with the [[motifs/wheel|wheel]]-spokes; but in spite of that I will give it to you. I know it is very generous of me, and a great many people would think me extremely foolish for parting with it, but I am not like the rest of the world. I think that generosity is the essence of [[concepts/friendship|friendship]], and, besides, I have got a new wheelbarrow for myself. Yes, you may  set your mind at ease, I will give you my wheelbarrow.’
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], really, that is generous of you,’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], and his funny round face glowed all over with pleasure. ‘I can easily put it in repair, as I have a plank of wood in the house.’
“‘A plank of wood!’ said the Miller; ‘why, that is just what I want for the roof of my barn. There is a very large hole in it, and the corn will all get damp if I don’t stop it up. How lucky you mentioned it! It is quite remarkable how one good action always breeds another. I have given you my wheelbarrow, and now you are going to give me your plank. Of course, the wheelbarrow is worth far more than the plank, but true [[concepts/friendship|friendship]] never notices things like that. Pray get it at once, and I will set to work at my barn this very day.’
“‘Certainly,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], and he ran into the shed and dragged the plank out.
“‘It is not a very big plank,’ said the Miller, looking at it, ‘and I am afraid that after I have mended my barn-roof there won’t be any left for you to mend the wheelbarrow with; but, of course, that is not my fault. And now, as I have given you my wheelbarrow, I am sure you would like to give me some [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] in return. Here is the basket, and mind you fill it quite full.’
“‘Quite full?’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], rather sorrowfully, for it was really a very big basket, and he knew that if he filled it he would have no [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] left for the market, and he was very anxious to get his silver buttons back.
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], really,’ answered the Miller, ‘as I have given you my wheelbarrow, I don’t think that it is much to ask you for a few [[motifs/flowers|flowers]]. I may be wrong, but I should have thought  that [[concepts/friendship|friendship]], true [[concepts/friendship|friendship]], was quite free from selfishness of any kind.’
“‘My dear friend, my best friend,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], ‘you are welcome to all the [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] in my [[motifs/garden|garden]]. I would much sooner have your good opinion than my silver buttons, any day;’ and he ran and plucked all his pretty primroses, and filled the Miller’s basket.
“‘Good-bye, [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]],’ said the Miller, as he went up the [[motifs/hill|hill]] with the plank on his shoulder, and the big basket in his hand.
“‘Good-bye,’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], and he began to dig away quite merrily, he was so pleased about the wheelbarrow.
“The next day he was nailing up some honeysuckle against the porch, when he heard the Miller’s voice calling to him from the [[motifs/road|road]]. So he jumped off the ladder, and ran down the [[motifs/garden|garden]], and looked over the wall.
“There was the Miller with a large sack of flour on his back.
“‘Dear [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]],’ said the Miller, ‘would you mind carrying this sack of flour for me to market?’
“‘Oh, I am so sorry,’ said Hans, ‘but I am really very busy to-day. I have got all my creepers to nail up, and all my [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] to [[motifs/water|water]], and all my grass to roll.’
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], really,’ said the Miller, ‘I think that, considering that I am going to give you my wheelbarrow, it is rather unfriendly of you to refuse.’
“‘Oh, don’t say that,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], ‘I wouldn’t be unfriendly for the whole world;’ and he ran in for his cap, and trudged off with the big sack on his shoulders.
“It was a very hot day, and the [[motifs/road|road]] was terribly dusty, and before Hans had reached the sixth milestone he was so tired that he had  to sit down and rest. However, he went on bravely, and at last he reached the market. After he had waited there some [[motifs/time|time]], he sold the sack of flour for a very good price, and then he returned [[motifs/home|home]] at once, for he was afraid that if he stopped too late he might meet some robbers on the way.
“‘It has certainly been a hard day,’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] to himself as he was going to bed, ‘but I am glad I did not refuse the Miller, for he is my best friend, and, besides, he is going to give me his wheelbarrow.’
“Early the next morning the Miller came down to get the money for his sack of flour, but [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] was so tired that he was still in bed.
“‘Upon my word,’ said the Miller, ‘you are very lazy. Really, considering that I am going to give you my wheelbarrow, I think you might work harder. Idleness is a great  sin, and I certainly don’t like any of my friends to be idle or sluggish. You must not mind my speaking quite plainly to you. Of course I should not dream of doing so if I were not your friend. But what is the good of [[concepts/friendship|friendship]] if one cannot say exactly what one means? Anybody can say charming things and try to please and to flatter, but a true friend always says unpleasant things, and does not mind giving [[motifs/pain|pain]]. Indeed, if he is a really true friend he prefers it, for he knows that then he is doing good.’
“‘I am very sorry,’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], rubbing his eyes and pulling off his [[motifs/night|night]]-cap, ‘but I was so tired that I thought I would lie in bed for a little [[motifs/time|time]], and listen to the [[motifs/birds|birds]] singing. Do you know that I always work better after hearing the [[motifs/birds|birds]] sing?’
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], I am glad of that,’ said the Miller, clapping [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] on the back, ‘for I want  you to come up to the mill as soon as you are dressed and mend my barn-roof for me.’
“Poor [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] was very anxious to go and work in his [[motifs/garden|garden]], for his [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] had not been watered for two days, but he did not like to refuse the Miller as he was such a good friend to him.
“‘Do you think it would be unfriendly of me if I said I was busy?’ he inquired in a shy and timid voice.
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], really,’ answered the Miller, ‘I do not think it is much to ask of you, considering that I am going to give you my wheelbarrow; but of course if you refuse I will go and do it myself.’
“‘Oh! on no account,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]]; and he jumped out of bed, and dressed himself, and went up to the barn.
“He worked there all day long, till sunset, and at sunset the Miller came to see how he was getting on.
HANS IN HIS [[motifs/garden|GARDEN]]
“‘Have you mended the hole in the roof yet, [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]]?’ cried the Miller in a cheery voice.
“‘It is quite mended,’ answered [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], coming down the ladder.
“‘Ah!’ said the Miller, ‘there is no work so delightful as the work one does for others.’
“‘It is certainly a great privilege to hear you talk,’ answered [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], sitting down and wiping his forehead, ‘a very great privilege. But I am afraid I shall never have such beautiful ideas as you have.’
“‘Oh! they will come to you,’ said the Miller, ‘but you must take more pains. At present you have only the practice of [[concepts/friendship|friendship]]; some day you will have the theory also.’
“‘Do you really think I shall?’ asked [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]].
“‘I have no [[concepts/doubt|doubt]] of it,’ answered the Miller, ‘but now that you have mended the  roof, you had better go [[motifs/home|home]] and rest, for I want you to drive my sheep to the mountain to-morrow.’
“Poor [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] was afraid to say anything to this, and early the next morning the Miller brought his sheep round to the cottage, and Hans started off with them to the mountain. It took him the whole day to get there and back; and when he returned he was so tired that he went off to [[motifs/sleep|sleep]] in his chair, and did not wake up till it was broad daylight.
“‘What a delightful [[motifs/time|time]] I shall have in my [[motifs/garden|garden]]!’ he said, and he went to work at once.
“But somehow he was never able to look after his [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] at all, for his friend the Miller was always coming round and sending him off on long errands, or getting him to help at the mill. [[characters/little-hans|Little Hans]] was very much distressed at times, as he was afraid his [[motifs/flowers|flowers]]  would think he had forgotten them, but he consoled himself by the reflection that the Miller was his best friend. ‘Besides,’ he used to say, ‘he is going to give me his wheelbarrow, and that is an act of pure generosity.’
“So [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] worked away for the Miller, and the Miller said all kinds of beautiful things about [[concepts/friendship|friendship]], which Hans took down in a notebook, and used to read over at [[motifs/night|night]], for he was a very good scholar.
“Now it happened that one evening [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] was sitting by his fireside when a loud rap came at the door. It was a very wild [[motifs/night|night]], and the [[motifs/wind|wind]] was blowing and roaring round the house so terribly that at first he thought it was merely the [[motifs/storm|storm]]. But a second rap came, and then a third, louder than any of the others.
“‘It is some poor traveller,’ said [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] to himself, and he ran to the door.
“There stood the Miller with a lantern in one hand and a big stick in the other.
“‘Dear [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]],’ cried the Miller, ‘I am in great trouble. My little boy has fallen off a ladder and hurt himself, and I am going for the Doctor. But he lives so far away, and it is such a bad [[motifs/night|night]], that it has just occurred to me that it would be much better if you went instead of me. You know I am going to give you my wheelbarrow, and so it is only fair that you should do something for me in return.’
“‘Certainly,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], ‘I take it quite as a compliment your coming to me, and I will start off at once. But you must lend me your lantern, as the [[motifs/night|night]] is so dark that I am afraid I might fall into the ditch.’
“‘I am very sorry,’ answered the Miller, ‘but it is my new lantern and it would be a great loss to me if anything happened to it.’
“‘[[motifs/well|Well]], never mind, I will do without it,’ cried [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]], and he took down his great fur coat, and his warm scarlet cap, and tied a muffler round his throat, and started off.
“What a dreadful [[motifs/storm|storm]] it was! The [[motifs/night|night]] was so black that [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] could hardly see, and the [[motifs/wind|wind]] was so strong that he could scarcely stand. However, he was very courageous, and after he had been walking about three hours, he arrived at the Doctor’s house, and knocked at the door.
“‘Who is there?’ cried the Doctor, putting his head out of his bedroom [[motifs/window|window]].
“‘[[characters/little-hans|Little Hans]], Doctor.’
“‘What do you want, [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]]?’
“‘The Miller’s son has fallen from a ladder, and has hurt himself, and the Miller wants you to come at once.’
“‘All right!’ said the Doctor; and he ordered his [[motifs/horse|horse]], and his big boots, and his  lantern, and came downstairs, and rode off in the direction of the Miller’s house, [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] trudging behind him.
“But the [[motifs/storm|storm]] grew worse and worse, and the rain fell in torrents, and [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] could not see where he was going, or keep up with the [[motifs/horse|horse]]. At last he lost his way, and wandered off on the moor, which was a very dangerous place, as it was full of deep holes, and there poor [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]] was drowned. His [[concepts/body|body]] was found the next day by some goatherds, floating in a great pool of [[motifs/water|water]], and was brought back by them to the cottage.
“Everybody went to [[characters/little-hans|little Hans]]’ funeral, as he was so popular, and the Miller was the chief mourner.
“‘As I was his best friend,’ said the Miller, ‘it is only fair that I should have the best place;’ so he walked at the head of the procession in a long black cloak, and every now  and then he wiped his eyes with a big pocket-handkerchief.
“‘[[characters/little-hans|Little Hans]] is certainly a great loss to every one,’ said the Blacksmith, when the funeral was over, and they were all seated comfortably in the inn, drinking spiced wine and eating sweet cakes.
“‘A great loss to me at any rate,’ answered the Miller, ‘why, I had as good as given him my wheelbarrow, and now I really don’t know what to do with it. It is very much in my way at [[motifs/home|home]], and it is in such bad repair that I could not get anything for it if I sold it. I will certainly take care not to give away anything again. One always suffers for being generous.’”
“[[motifs/well|Well]]?” said the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat, after a long pause.
“[[motifs/well|Well]], that is the end,” said the Linnet.
“But what became of the Miller?” asked the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“Oh! I really don’t know,” replied the Linnet; “and I am sure that I don’t care.”
“It is quite evident then that you have no sympathy in your [[concepts/nature|nature]],” said the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“I am afraid you don’t quite see the moral of the story,” remarked the Linnet.
“The what?” screamed the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat.
“The moral.”
“Do you mean to say that the story has a moral?”
“Certainly,” said the Linnet.
“[[motifs/well|Well]], really,” said the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat, in a very angry manner, “I think you should have told me that before you began. If you had done so, I certainly would not have listened to you; in fact, I should have said ‘Pooh,’ like the critic. However, I can say it now;” so he shouted out “Pooh” at the top of his voice, gave a whisk with his tail, and went back into his hole.
“And how do you like the [[motifs/water|Water]]-rat?” asked the Duck, who came paddling up some minutes afterwards. “He has a great many good points, but for my own part I have a mother’s feelings, and I can never look at a confirmed bachelor without the [[motifs/tears|tears]] coming into my eyes.”
“I am rather afraid that I have annoyed him,” answered the Linnet. “The fact is, that I told him a story with a moral.”
“Ah! that is always a very dangerous thing to do,” said the Duck.

Una mattina il vecchio Topo d’[[motifs/water|acqua]] mise la testa fuori dalla sua tana. Aveva occhietti lucidi come perline e rigidi baffi grigi, e la coda gli pareva un lungo pezzo di caucciù nero. Le anatrelle nuotavano nello stagno, somigliando proprio a un mucchio di canarini gialli, e la loro madre, che era candida con zampe di un rosso autentico, cercava d’insegnar loro a stare a testa in giù nell’[[motifs/water|acqua]].
“Non sarete mai nella migliore società se non saprete stare a testa in giù,” continuava a dir loro; e ogni tanto mostrava come si facesse. Ma le anatrelle non le prestavano attenzione. Erano così giovani che non sapevano che vantaggio sia stare in società.
“Che bambini disobbedienti!” gridò il vecchio Topo d’[[motifs/water|acqua]]; “meriterebbero davvero d’essere annegati.”
“Niente affatto,” rispose l’Anatra, “tutti devono pur cominciare, e i genitori non saranno mai troppo pazienti.”
“Ah! io non so nulla dei sentimenti dei genitori,” disse il Topo d’[[motifs/water|acqua]]; “non sono un uomo di famiglia. Anzi, non mi sono mai sposato, e non intendo farlo mai. L’[[motifs/love|amore]] è una cosa [[motifs/well|bella]], a modo suo, ma l’[[concepts/friendship|amicizia]] è molto più alta. Davvero, non conosco al mondo nulla di più nobile o di più raro di un’[[concepts/friendship|amicizia]] devota.”
