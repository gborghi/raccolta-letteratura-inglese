High above the [[motifs/city|city]], on a tall column, stood the statue of the Happy Prince. He was gilded all over with thin [[motifs/leaves|leaves]] of fine gold, for eyes he had two bright sapphires, and a large red ruby glowed on his sword-hilt.
He was very much admired indeed. “He is as beautiful as a weathercock,” remarked  one of the Town Councillors who wished to gain a reputation for having artistic tastes; “only not quite so useful,” he added, fearing lest people should think him unpractical, which he really was not.
“Why can’t you be like the Happy Prince?” asked a sensible mother of her little boy who was crying for the [[motifs/moon|moon]]. “The Happy Prince never [[motifs/dreams|dreams]] of crying for anything.”
“I am glad there is some one in the world who is quite happy,” muttered a disappointed man as he gazed at the wonderful statue.
“He looks just like an angel,” said the [[concepts/charity|Charity]] Children as they came out of the [[settings/cathedral|cathedral]] in their bright scarlet cloaks and their clean white pinafores.
“How do you know?” said the Mathematical Master, “you have never seen one.”
“Ah! but we have, in our [[motifs/dreams|dreams]],” answered the children; and the Mathematical  Master frowned and looked very severe, for he did not approve of children dreaming.
One [[motifs/night|night]] there flew over the [[motifs/city|city]] a little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]. His friends had gone away to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]] six weeks before, but he had stayed behind, for he was in [[motifs/love|love]] with the most beautiful Reed. He had met her early in the [[motifs/spring|spring]] as he was flying down the [[motifs/river|river]] after a big yellow moth, and had been so attracted by her slender waist that he had stopped to talk to her.
“Shall I [[motifs/love|love]] you?” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], who liked to come to the point at once, and the Reed made him a low bow. So he flew round and round her, touching the [[motifs/water|water]] with his wings, and making silver ripples. This was his courtship, and it lasted all through the [[motifs/summer|summer]].
“It is a ridiculous attachment,” twittered the other Swallows; “she has no money, and  far too many relations;” and indeed the [[motifs/river|river]] was quite full of Reeds. Then, when the [[motifs/autumn|autumn]] came they all flew away.
After they had gone he felt lonely, and began to tire of his lady-[[motifs/love|love]]. “She has no conversation,” he said, “and I am afraid that she is a coquette, for she is always flirting with the [[motifs/wind|wind]].” And certainly, whenever the [[motifs/wind|wind]] blew, the Reed made the most graceful curtseys. “I admit that she is domestic,” he continued, “but I [[motifs/love|love]] travelling, and my wife, consequently, should [[motifs/love|love]] travelling also.”
“Will you come away with me?” he said finally to her; but the Reed shook her head, she was so attached to her [[motifs/home|home]].
“You have been trifling with me,” he cried. “I am off to the Pyramids. Good-bye!” and he flew away.
All day long he flew, and at [[motifs/night|night]]-[[motifs/time|time]] he arrived at the [[motifs/city|city]]. “Where shall I put up?”  he said; “I [[concepts/hope|hope]] the town has made preparations.”
Then he saw the statue on the tall column.
“I will put up there,” he cried; “it is a fine position, with plenty of fresh air.” So he alighted just between the feet of the Happy Prince.
“I have a golden bedroom,” he said softly to himself as he looked round, and he prepared to go to [[motifs/sleep|sleep]]; but just as he was putting his head under his wing a large drop of [[motifs/water|water]] fell on him. “What a curious thing!” he cried; “there is not a single cloud in the sky, the [[motifs/stars|stars]] are quite clear and bright, and yet it is raining. The climate in the north of Europe is really dreadful. The Reed used to like the rain, but that was merely her selfishness.”
Then another drop fell.
“What is the use of a statue if it cannot keep the rain off?” he said; “I must look for  a good chimney-pot,” and he determined to fly away.
But before he had opened his wings, a third drop fell, and he looked up, and saw — Ah! what did he see?
The eyes of the Happy Prince were filled with [[motifs/tears|tears]], and [[motifs/tears|tears]] were running down his golden cheeks. His face was so beautiful in the moonlight that the little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] was filled with pity.
“Who are you?” he said.
“I am the Happy Prince.”
“Why are you weeping then?” asked the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]; “you have quite drenched me.”

THE PALACE OF SANS-SOUCI
“When I was alive and had a human heart,” answered the statue, “I did not know what [[motifs/tears|tears]] were, for I lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci, where sorrow is not allowed to enter. In the daytime I played with my companions in the [[motifs/garden|garden]], and in the evening I led the  [[motifs/dance|dance]] in the Great Hall. Round the [[motifs/garden|garden]] ran a very lofty wall, but I never cared to ask what lay beyond it, everything about me was so beautiful. My courtiers called me the Happy Prince, and happy indeed I was, if pleasure be happiness. So I lived, and so I died. And now that I am dead they have set me up here so high that I can see all the ugliness and all the misery of my [[motifs/city|city]], and though my heart is made of lead yet I cannot choose but weep.”
“What! is he not solid gold?” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] to himself. He was too polite to make any personal remarks out loud.
“Far away,” continued the statue in a low musical voice, “far away in a little street there is a poor house. One of the windows is open, and through it I can see a woman seated at a table. Her face is thin and worn, and she has coarse, red hands, all pricked by the needle,  for she is a seamstress. She is embroidering passion-[[motifs/flowers|flowers]] on a satin gown for the loveliest of the [[characters/queen|Queen]]’s maids-of-[[concepts/honour|honour]] to wear at the next [[settings/court|Court]]-ball. In a bed in the corner of the room her little boy is lying ill. He has a fever, and is asking for oranges. His mother has nothing to give him but [[motifs/river|river]] [[motifs/water|water]], so he is crying. [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], will you not bring her the ruby out of my sword-hilt? My feet are fastened to this pedestal and I cannot move.”
“I am waited for in [[settings/egypt|Egypt]],” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]. “My friends are flying up and down the Nile, and talking to the large lotus-[[motifs/flowers|flowers]]. Soon they will go to [[motifs/sleep|sleep]] in the tomb of the great [[archetypes/king|King]]. The [[archetypes/king|King]] is there himself in his painted coffin. He is wrapped in yellow linen, and embalmed with spices. Round his neck is a chain of pale green jade, and his hands are like withered [[motifs/leaves|leaves]].”
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “will you not stay with me for one [[motifs/night|night]], and be my messenger? The boy is so thirsty, and the mother so sad.”
“I don’t think I like boys,” answered the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]. “Last [[motifs/summer|summer]], when I was staying on the [[motifs/river|river]], there were two rude boys, the miller’s sons, who were always throwing stones at me. They never hit me, of course; we swallows fly far too [[motifs/well|well]] for that, and besides, I come of a family famous for its agility; but still, it was a mark of disrespect.”
But the Happy Prince looked so sad that the little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] was sorry. “It is very cold here,” he said; “but I will stay with you for one [[motifs/night|night]], and be your messenger.”
“Thank you, little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince.
So the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] picked out the great ruby from the Prince’s sword, and flew away with it in his beak over the roofs of the town.
He passed by the [[settings/cathedral|cathedral]] tower, where the white marble angels were sculptured. He passed by the palace and heard the sound of dancing. A beautiful girl came out on the balcony with her [[archetypes/lover|lover]]. “How wonderful the [[motifs/stars|stars]] are,” he said to her, “and how wonderful is the power of [[motifs/love|love]]!”
“I [[concepts/hope|hope]] my dress will be ready in [[motifs/time|time]] for the State-ball,” she answered; “I have ordered passion-[[motifs/flowers|flowers]] to be embroidered on it; but the seamstresses are so lazy.”
He passed over the [[motifs/river|river]], and saw the lanterns hanging to the masts of the ships. He passed over the Ghetto, and saw the old Jews bargaining with each other, and weighing out money in copper scales. At last he came to the poor house and looked in. The boy was tossing feverishly on his bed, and the mother had fallen asleep, she was so tired. In he hopped, and laid the great ruby on the  table beside the woman’s thimble. Then he flew gently round the bed, fanning the boy’s forehead with his wings. “How cool I feel!” said the boy, “I must be getting better;” and he sank into a delicious slumber.
Then the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] flew back to the Happy Prince, and told him what he had done. “It is curious,” he remarked, “but I feel quite warm now, although it is so cold.”
“That is because you have done a good action,” said the Prince. And the little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] began to think, and then he fell asleep. Thinking always made him sleepy.
When day broke he flew down to the [[motifs/river|river]] and had a bath. “What a remarkable phenomenon,” said the Professor of Ornithology as he was passing over the bridge. “A [[motifs/swallow|swallow]] in [[motifs/winter|winter]]!” And he wrote a long letter about it to the local newspaper. Every one quoted it, it was  full of so many words that they could not understand.
“To-[[motifs/night|night]] I go to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]],” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], and he was in high spirits at the prospect. He visited all the public monuments, and sat a long [[motifs/time|time]] on top of the church steeple. Wherever he went the Sparrows chirruped, and said to each other, “What a distinguished stranger!” so he enjoyed himself very much.
When the [[motifs/moon|moon]] [[motifs/rose|rose]] he flew back to the Happy Prince. “Have you any commissions for [[settings/egypt|Egypt]]?” he cried; “I am just starting.”
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “will you not stay with me one [[motifs/night|night]] longer?”
THE LOVELIEST OF THE [[characters/queen|QUEEN]]’S MAIDS OF [[concepts/honour|HONOUR]]
“I am waited for in [[settings/egypt|Egypt]],” answered the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]. “To-morrow my friends will fly up to the Second Cataract. The [[motifs/river|river]]-[[motifs/horse|horse]] couches there among the bulrushes, and on a great granite throne sits the God Memnon.  All [[motifs/night|night]] long he watches the [[motifs/stars|stars]], and when the morning star shines he utters one cry of joy, and then he is silent. At [[motifs/noon|noon]] the yellow lions come down to the [[motifs/water|water]]’s edge to drink. They have eyes like green beryls, and their roar is louder than the roar of the cataract.”
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “far away across the [[motifs/city|city]] I see a young man in a garret. He is leaning over a desk covered with papers, and in a tumbler by his side there is a bunch of withered violets. His hair is brown and crisp, and his lips are red as a pomegranate, and he has large and dreamy eyes. He is trying to finish a play for the Director of the Theatre, but he is too cold to write any more. There is no [[motifs/fire|fire]] in the grate, and hunger has made him faint.”
“I will wait with you one [[motifs/night|night]] longer,” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], who really had a good heart. “Shall I take him another ruby?”
“Alas! I have no ruby now,” said the Prince; “my eyes are all that I have left. They are made of rare sapphires, which were brought out of India a thousand years ago. Pluck out one of them and take it to him. He will sell it to the jeweller, and buy food and firewood, and finish his play.”
“Dear Prince,” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], “I cannot do that”; and he began to weep.
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “do as I command you.”
So the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] plucked out the Prince’s eye, and flew away to the student’s garret. It was easy enough to get in, as there was a hole in the roof. Through this he darted, and came into the room. The young man had his head buried in his hands, so he did not hear the flutter of the bird’s wings, and when he looked up he found the beautiful sapphire lying on the withered violets.
“I am beginning to be appreciated,” he cried; “this is from some great admirer. Now I can finish my play,” and he looked quite happy.
The next day the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] flew down to the harbour. He sat on the mast of a large vessel and watched the sailors hauling big chests out of the hold with ropes. “Heave a-hoy!” they shouted as each chest came up. “I am going to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]]!” cried the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], but nobody minded, and when the [[motifs/moon|moon]] [[motifs/rose|rose]] he flew back to the Happy Prince.
“I am come to bid you good-bye,” he cried.
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “will you not stay with me one [[motifs/night|night]] longer?”
“It is [[motifs/winter|winter]],” answered the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], “and the chill [[motifs/snow|snow]] will soon be here. In [[settings/egypt|Egypt]] the sun is warm on the green palm-trees, and the crocodiles lie in the mud and look lazily  about them. My companions are building a nest in the Temple of Baalbec, and the pink and white doves are watching them, and cooing to each other. Dear Prince, I must leave you, but I will never forget you, and next [[motifs/spring|spring]] I will bring you back two beautiful jewels in place of those you have given away. The ruby shall be redder than a red [[motifs/rose|rose]], and the sapphire shall be as blue as the great sea.”
“In the square below,” said the Happy Prince, “there stands a little match-girl. She has let her matches fall in the gutter, and they are all spoiled. Her father will beat her if she does not bring [[motifs/home|home]] some money, and she is crying. She has no shoes or stockings, and her little head is bare. Pluck out my other eye and give it to her, and her father will not beat her.”
“I will stay with you one [[motifs/night|night]] longer,” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], “but I cannot pluck out  your eye. You would be quite blind then.”
“[[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “do as I command you.”
So he plucked out the Prince’s other eye, and darted down with it. He swooped past the match-girl, and slipped the [[motifs/jewel|jewel]] into the palm of her hand. “What a lovely bit of glass!” cried the little girl; and she ran [[motifs/home|home]], laughing.
Then the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] came back to the Prince. “You are blind now,” he said, “so I will stay with you always.”
“No, little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the poor Prince, “you must go away to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]].”
“I will stay with you always,” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], and he slept at the Prince’s feet.
All the next day he sat on the Prince’s shoulder, and told him stories of what he had seen in strange lands. He told him of the red ibises, who stand in long rows on the  banks of the Nile, and catch gold-fish in their beaks; of the Sphinx, who is as old as the world itself, and lives in the [[motifs/desert|desert]], and knows everything; of the merchants, who walk slowly by the side of their camels and carry amber beads in their hands; of the [[archetypes/king|King]] of the [[settings/mountains|Mountains]] of the [[motifs/moon|Moon]], who is as black as ebony, and worships a large crystal; of the great green snake that sleeps in a palm tree, and has twenty priests to feed it with honey-cakes; and of the pygmies who sail over a big lake on large flat [[motifs/leaves|leaves]], and are always at war with the butterflies.
“Dear little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “you tell me of marvellous things, but more marvellous than anything is the [[concepts/suffering|suffering]] of men and of women. There is no Mystery so great as Misery. Fly over my [[motifs/city|city]], little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]], and tell me what you see there.”
THE RICH MAKING MERRY IN THEIR BEAUTIFUL HOUSES, WHILE THE BEGGARS WERE SITTING AT THE GATES
So the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] flew over the great [[motifs/city|city]], and  saw the rich making merry in their beautiful houses, while the beggars were sitting at the gates. He flew into dark lanes, and saw the white faces of starving children looking out listlessly at the black streets. Under the archway of a bridge two little boys were lying in one another’s arms to try and keep themselves warm. “How hungry we are!” they said. “You must not lie here,” shouted the Watchman, and they wandered out into the rain.
Then he flew back and told the Prince what he had seen.
“I am covered with fine gold,” said the Prince, “you must take it off, leaf by leaf, and give it to my poor; the living always think that gold can make them happy.”
Leaf after leaf of the fine gold the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] picked off, till the Happy Prince looked quite dull and grey. Leaf after leaf of the fine gold he brought to the poor, and the children’s  faces grew rosier, and they laughed and played games in the street. “We have bread now!” they cried.
Then the [[motifs/snow|snow]] came, and after the [[motifs/snow|snow]] came the [[motifs/frost|frost]]. The streets looked as if they were made of silver, they were so bright and glistening; long icicles like crystal daggers hung down from the eaves of the houses, everybody went about in furs, and the little boys wore scarlet caps and skated on the ice.
The poor little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] grew colder and colder, but he would not leave the Prince, he loved him too [[motifs/well|well]]. He picked up crumbs outside the baker’s door when the baker was not looking, and tried to keep himself warm by flapping his wings.
But at last he knew that he was going to die. He had just strength to fly up to the Prince’s shoulder once more. “Good-bye,  dear Prince!” he murmured, “will you let me kiss your hand?”
“I am glad that you are going to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]] at last, little [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]],” said the Prince, “you have stayed too long here; but you must kiss me on the lips, for I [[motifs/love|love]] you.”
“It is not to [[settings/egypt|Egypt]] that I am going,” said the [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]]. “I am going to the House of [[motifs/death|Death]]. [[motifs/death|Death]] is the brother of [[motifs/sleep|Sleep]], is he not?”
And he kissed the Happy Prince on the lips, and fell down dead at his feet.
At that moment a curious crack sounded inside the statue, as if something had broken. The fact is that the leaden heart had snapped right in two. It certainly was a dreadfully hard [[motifs/frost|frost]].
Early the next morning the Mayor was walking in the square below in company with the Town Councillors. As they passed the column he looked up at the statue: “Dear  me! how shabby the Happy Prince looks!” he said.
“How shabby, indeed!” cried the Town Councillors, who always agreed with the Mayor; and they went up to look at it.
“The ruby has fallen out of his sword, his eyes are gone, and he is golden no longer,” said the Mayor; “in fact, he is little better than a beggar!”
“Little better than a beggar,” said the Town Councillors.
“And here is actually a dead bird at his feet!” continued the Mayor. “We must really issue a proclamation that [[motifs/birds|birds]] are not to be allowed to die here.” And the Town Clerk made a note of the suggestion.
So they pulled down the statue of the Happy Prince. “As he is no longer beautiful he is no longer useful,” said the Art Professor at the University.
Then they melted the statue in a furnace, and the Mayor held a meeting of the Corporation to decide what was to be done with the metal. “We must have another statue, of course,” he said, “and it shall be a statue of myself.”
“Of myself,” said each of the Town Councillors, and they quarrelled. When I last heard of them they were quarrelling still.
“What a strange thing!” said the overseer of the workmen at the foundry. “This broken lead heart will not melt in the furnace. We must throw it away.” So they threw it on a [[motifs/dust|dust]]-heap where the dead [[motifs/swallow|Swallow]] was also lying.
“Bring me the two most precious things in the [[motifs/city|city]],” said God to one of His Angels; and the Angel brought Him the leaden heart and the dead bird.
“You have rightly chosen,” said God, “for in my [[motifs/garden|garden]] of Paradise this little bird shall sing for evermore, and in my [[motifs/city|city]] of gold the Happy Prince shall praise me.”

In alto sopra la [[motifs/city|città]], su un’alta colonna, sorgeva la statua del Principe Felice. Era tutta dorata di sottili [[motifs/leaves|foglie]] d’oro fino, per occhi aveva due lucenti zaffiri, e un grande rubino rosso ardeva sull’elsa della sua spada.
Era davvero molto ammirato. “È bello come una banderuola,” osservò uno dei Consiglieri Comunali che desiderava farsi una reputazione di gusti artistici; “solo, non altrettanto utile,” aggiunse, temendo che la gente lo credesse poco pratico, il che davvero non era.
“Perché non sai essere come il Principe Felice?” chiese una madre assennata al suo bambino che piangeva per avere la [[motifs/moon|luna]]. “Il Principe Felice non si [[motifs/dreams|sogna]] mai di piangere per qualcosa.”
“Sono contento che ci sia qualcuno al mondo che è del tutto felice,” mormorò un uomo deluso mentre contemplava la statua meravigliosa.
“Sembra proprio un angelo,” dissero i Bambini della [[concepts/charity|Carità]] uscendo dalla [[settings/cathedral|cattedrale]] nei loro vivaci mantelli scarlatti e nei loro grembiulini bianchi e puliti.
“E come lo sapete?” disse il Maestro di Matematica, “non ne avete mai visto uno.”
“Ah! ma sì, nei nostri [[motifs/dreams|sogni]],” risposero i bambini; e il Maestro di Matematica aggrottò le sopracciglia e si fece molto severo, perché non approvava che i bambini sognassero.
Una [[motifs/night|notte]] volò sopra la [[motifs/city|città]] una piccola [[motifs/swallow|Rondine]]. I suoi amici erano partiti per l’[[settings/egypt|Egitto]] sei settimane prima, ma lei era rimasta indietro, perché era [[motifs/love|innamorata]] della più bella delle Canne. L’aveva incontrata all’inizio della [[motifs/spring|primavera]] mentre volava lungo il [[motifs/river|fiume]] dietro a una grande falena gialla, ed era stata così attratta dalla sua vita sottile che si era fermata a parlarle.
“Devo [[motifs/love|amarti]]?” disse la [[motifs/swallow|Rondine]], che amava andare subito al punto, e la Canna le fece un profondo inchino. Così le volò intorno e intorno, sfiorando l’[[motifs/water|acqua]] con le ali e facendo increspature d’argento. Questo fu il suo corteggiamento, e durò per tutta l’[[motifs/summer|estate]].
“È un attaccamento ridicolo,” cinguettavano le altre Rondini; “non ha denaro, e ha fin troppi parenti;” e in effetti il [[motifs/river|fiume]] era pieno zeppo di Canne. Poi, quando venne l’[[motifs/autumn|autunno]], volarono via tutte.
Dopo che se ne furono andate, si sentì sola e cominciò a stancarsi del suo [[motifs/love|amore]]. “Non ha conversazione,” disse, “e temo che sia una civetta, perché flirta sempre col [[motifs/wind|vento]].” E certamente, ogni volta che il [[motifs/wind|vento]] soffiava, la Canna faceva le riverenze più aggraziate. “Ammetto che sia casalinga,” continuò, “ma io [[motifs/love|amo]] viaggiare, e mio marito, di conseguenza, dovrebbe [[motifs/love|amare]] viaggiare anche lui.”
“Verrai via con me?” le disse infine; ma la Canna scosse il capo: era così attaccata alla sua [[motifs/home|casa]].
“Ti sei presa gioco di me,” gridò. “Io parto per le Piramidi. Addio!” e volò via.
Volò tutto il giorno, e nel [[motifs/time|tempo]] della [[motifs/night|notte]] giunse alla [[motifs/city|città]]. “Dove alloggerò?” disse; “[[concepts/hope|spero]] che la città abbia fatto i preparativi.”
Poi vide la statua sull’alta colonna.
“Alloggerò lassù,” gridò; “è una bella posizione, con molta aria fresca.” Così si posò proprio fra i piedi del Principe Felice.
“Ho una camera da letto d’oro,” disse piano tra sé guardandosi intorno, e si preparò a [[motifs/sleep|dormire]]; ma proprio mentre metteva la testa sotto l’ala, una grossa goccia d’[[motifs/water|acqua]] le cadde addosso. “Che cosa curiosa!” gridò; “non c’è una sola nuvola in cielo, le [[motifs/stars|stelle]] sono limpide e luminose, eppure piove. Il clima del nord dell’Europa è davvero terribile. Alla Canna piaceva la pioggia, ma quello era soltanto il suo egoismo.”
Poi cadde un’altra goccia.
“A che serve una statua se non ripara dalla pioggia?” disse; “devo cercarmi un buon comignolo,” e decise di volare via.
Ma prima che avesse aperto le ali, cadde una terza goccia, e alzò lo sguardo, e vide — Ah! che cosa vide?
Gli occhi del Principe Felice erano pieni di [[motifs/tears|lacrime]], e le [[motifs/tears|lacrime]] gli scorrevano giù per le guance dorate. Il suo viso era così bello al chiaro di luna che la piccola [[motifs/swallow|Rondine]] si riempì di pietà.
“Chi sei?” disse.
“Sono il Principe Felice.”
“E allora perché piangi?” chiese la [[motifs/swallow|Rondine]]; “mi hai inzuppata tutta.”

IL PALAZZO DI SANS-SOUCI
“Quando ero vivo e avevo un cuore umano,” rispose la statua, “non sapevo che cosa fossero le [[motifs/tears|lacrime]], perché vivevo nel Palazzo di Sans-Souci, dove al dolore non è permesso entrare.
