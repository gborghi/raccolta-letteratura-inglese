[TO H.S.H. ALICE, PRINCESS OF MONACO]
Every evening the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] went out upon the sea, and threw his nets into the [[motifs/water|water]].
When the [[motifs/wind|wind]] blew from the land he caught nothing, or but little at best, for it was a bitter and black-winged [[motifs/wind|wind]], and rough waves [[motifs/rose|rose]] up to meet it.  But when the [[motifs/wind|wind]] blew to the shore, the fish came in from the deep, and swam into the meshes of his nets, and he took them to the market-place and sold them.
Every evening he went out upon the sea, and one evening the net was so heavy that hardly could he draw it into the boat.  And he laughed, and said to himself, ‘Surely I have caught all the fish that swim, or snared some dull [[archetypes/monster|monster]] that will be a marvel to men, or some thing of horror that the great [[characters/queen|Queen]] will [[motifs/desire|desire]],’ and putting forth all his strength, he tugged at the coarse ropes till, like lines of blue enamel round a vase of bronze, the long veins [[motifs/rose|rose]] up on his arms.  He tugged at the thin ropes, and nearer and nearer came the circle of flat corks, and the net [[motifs/rose|rose]] at last to the top of the [[motifs/water|water]].
But no fish at all was in it, nor any [[archetypes/monster|monster]] or thing of horror, but only a little [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]] lying fast asleep.
Her hair was as a wet fleece of gold, and each separate hair as a thread of fine gold in a cup of glass.  Her [[concepts/body|body]] was as white ivory, and her tail was of silver and pearl.  Silver and pearl was her tail, and the green weeds of the sea coiled round it; and like sea-shells were her ears, and her lips were like sea-coral.  The cold waves dashed over her cold breasts, and the salt glistened upon her eyelids.
So beautiful was she that when the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] saw her he was filled with [[concepts/wonder|wonder]], and he put out his hand and drew the net close to him, and leaning over the side he clasped her in his arms.  And when he touched her, she gave a cry like a startled sea-gull, and woke, and looked at him in terror with her mauve-amethyst eyes, and struggled that she might escape.  But he held her tightly to him, and would not suffer her to depart.
And when she saw that she could in no way escape from him, she began to weep, and said, ‘I pray thee let me go, for I am the only daughter of a [[archetypes/king|King]], and my father is aged and alone.’
But the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] answered, ‘I will not let thee go save thou makest me a promise that whenever I call thee, thou wilt come and sing to me, for the fish delight to listen to the song of the Sea-folk, and so shall my nets be full.’
‘Wilt thou in very [[concepts/truth|truth]] let me go, if I promise thee this?’ cried the [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]].
‘In very [[concepts/truth|truth]] I will let thee go,’ said the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]].
So she made him the promise he desired, and sware it by the oath of the Sea-folk.  And he loosened his arms from about her, and she sank down into the [[motifs/water|water]], trembling with a strange [[concepts/fear|fear]].
Every evening the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] went out upon the sea, and called to the [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]], and she [[motifs/rose|rose]] out of the [[motifs/water|water]] and sang to him.  Round and round her swam the dolphins, and the wild gulls wheeled above her head.
And she sang a marvellous song.  For she sang of the Sea-folk who drive their flocks from [[motifs/cave|cave]] to [[motifs/cave|cave]], and carry the little calves on their shoulders; of the Tritons who have long green beards, and hairy breasts, and blow through twisted conchs when the [[archetypes/king|King]] passes by; of the palace of the [[archetypes/king|King]] which is all of amber, with a roof of clear emerald, and a pavement of bright pearl; and of the gardens of the sea where the great filigrane fans of coral wave all day long, and the fish dart about like silver [[motifs/birds|birds]], and the anemones cling to the rocks, and the pinks bourgeon in the ribbed yellow sand.  She sang of the big whales that come down from the north seas and have sharp icicles hanging to their fins; of the Sirens who tell of such wonderful things that the merchants have to stop their ears with wax lest they should hear them, and leap into the [[motifs/water|water]] and be drowned; of the sunken galleys with their tall masts, and the frozen sailors clinging to the rigging, and the mackerel swimming in and out of the open portholes; of the little barnacles who are great travellers, and cling to the keels of the ships and go round and round the world; and of the cuttlefish who live in the sides of the cliffs and stretch out their long black arms, and can make [[motifs/night|night]] come when they will it.  She sang of the nautilus who has a boat of her own that is carved out of an opal and steered with a silken sail; of the happy Mermen who play upon harps and can charm the great Kraken to [[motifs/sleep|sleep]]; of the little children who catch hold of the slippery porpoises and ride laughing upon their backs; of the Mermaids who lie in the white foam and hold out their arms to the mariners; and of the sea-lions with their curved tusks, and the sea-horses with their floating manes.
And as she sang, all the tunny-fish came in from the deep to listen to her, and the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] threw his nets round them and caught them, and others he took with a spear.  And when his boat was [[motifs/well|well]]-laden, the [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]] would sink down into the sea, smiling at him.
Yet would she never come near him that he might touch her.  Oftentimes he called to her and prayed of her, but she would not; and when he sought to seize her she dived into the [[motifs/water|water]] as a seal might dive, nor did he see her again that day.  And each day the sound of her voice became sweeter to his ears.  So sweet was her voice that he forgot his nets and his cunning, and had no care of his craft.  Vermilion-finned and with eyes of bossy gold, the tunnies went by in shoals, but he heeded them not.  His spear lay by his side unused, and his baskets of plaited osier were empty.  With lips parted, and eyes dim with [[concepts/wonder|wonder]], he sat idle in his boat and listened, listening till the sea-mists crept round him, and the wandering [[motifs/moon|moon]] stained his brown limbs with silver.
And one evening he called to her, and said: ‘Little [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]], little [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]], I [[motifs/love|love]] thee.  Take me for thy bridegroom, for I [[motifs/love|love]] thee.’
But the [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]] shook her head.  ‘Thou hast a human [[motifs/soul|soul]],’ she answered.  ‘If only thou wouldst send away thy [[motifs/soul|soul]], then could I [[motifs/love|love]] thee.’
And the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] said to himself, ‘Of what use is my [[motifs/soul|soul]] to me?  I cannot see it.  I may not touch it.  I do not know it.  Surely I will send it away from me, and much gladness shall be mine.’  And a cry of joy broke from his lips, and standing up in the painted boat, he held out his arms to the [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]].  ‘I will send my [[motifs/soul|soul]] away,’ he cried, ‘and you shall be my bride, and I will be thy bridegroom, and in the depth of the sea we will dwell together, and all that thou hast sung of thou shalt show me, and all that thou desirest I will do, nor shall our lives be divided.’
And the little [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]] laughed for pleasure and hid her face in her hands.
‘But how shall I send my [[motifs/soul|soul]] from me?’ cried the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]].  ‘Tell me how I may do it, and lo! it shall be done.’
‘Alas!  I know not,’ said the little [[characters/mermaid|Mermaid]]: ‘the Sea-folk have no souls.’  And she sank down into the deep, looking wistfully at him.
Now early on the next morning, before the sun was the span of a man’s hand above the [[motifs/hill|hill]], the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] went to the house of the Priest and knocked three times at the door.
The novice looked out through the wicket, and when he saw who it was, he drew back the latch and said to him, ‘Enter.’
And the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] passed in, and knelt down on the sweet-smelling rushes of the floor, and cried to the Priest who was reading out of the Holy Book and said to him, ‘Father, I am in [[motifs/love|love]] with one of the Sea-folk, and my [[motifs/soul|soul]] hindereth me from having my [[motifs/desire|desire]].  Tell me how I can send my [[motifs/soul|soul]] away from me, for in [[concepts/truth|truth]] I have no need of it.  Of what value is my [[motifs/soul|soul]] to me?  I cannot see it.  I may not touch it.  I do not know it.’
And the Priest beat his breast, and answered, ‘Alack, alack, thou art mad, or hast eaten of some poisonous herb, for the [[motifs/soul|soul]] is the noblest part of man, and was given to us by God that we should nobly use it.  There is no thing more precious than a human [[motifs/soul|soul]], nor any earthly thing that can be weighed with it.  It is worth all the gold that is in the world, and is more precious than the rubies of the kings.  Therefore, my son, think not any more of this matter, for it is a sin that may not be forgiven.  And as for the Sea-folk, they are lost, and they who would traffic with them are lost also.  They are as the beasts of the field that know not good from evil, and for them the Lord has not died.’
The [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]]’s eyes filled with [[motifs/tears|tears]] when he heard the bitter words of the Priest, and he [[motifs/rose|rose]] up from his knees and said to him, ‘Father, the Fauns live in the forest and are glad, and on the rocks sit the Mermen with their harps of red gold.  Let me be as they are, I beseech thee, for their days are as the days of [[motifs/flowers|flowers]].  And as for my [[motifs/soul|soul]], what doth my [[motifs/soul|soul]] profit me, if it stand between me and the thing that I [[motifs/love|love]]?’
‘The [[motifs/love|love]] of the [[concepts/body|body]] is vile,’ cried the Priest, knitting his brows, ‘and vile and evil are the pagan things God suffers to wander through His world.  Accursed be the Fauns of the woodland, and accursed be the singers of the sea!  I have heard them at [[motifs/night|night]]-[[motifs/time|time]], and they have sought to lure me from my beads.  They tap at the [[motifs/window|window]], and laugh.  They whisper into my ears the tale of their perilous joys.  They tempt me with temptations, and when I would pray they make mouths at me.  They are lost, I tell thee, they are lost.  For them there is no heaven nor hell, and in neither shall they praise God’s name.’
‘Father,’ cried the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]], ‘thou knowest not what thou sayest.  Once in my net I snared the daughter of a [[archetypes/king|King]].  She is fairer than the morning star, and whiter than the [[motifs/moon|moon]].  For her [[concepts/body|body]] I would give my [[motifs/soul|soul]], and for her [[motifs/love|love]] I would surrender heaven.  Tell me what I ask of thee, and let me go in peace.’
‘Away!  Away!’ cried the Priest: ‘thy leman is lost, and thou shalt be lost with her.’
And he gave him no blessing, but drove him from his door.
And the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] went down into the market-place, and he walked slowly, and with bowed head, as one who is in sorrow.
And when the merchants saw him coming, they began to whisper to each other, and one of them came forth to meet him, and called him by name, and said to him, ‘What hast thou to sell?’
‘I will sell thee my [[motifs/soul|soul]],’ he answered.  ‘I pray thee buy it of me, for I am weary of it.  Of what use is my [[motifs/soul|soul]] to me?  I cannot see it.  I may not touch it.  I do not know it.’
But the merchants mocked at him, and said, ‘Of what use is a man’s [[motifs/soul|soul]] to us?  It is not worth a clipped piece of silver.  Sell us thy [[concepts/body|body]] for a slave, and we will clothe thee in sea-purple, and put a ring upon thy finger, and make thee the minion of the great [[characters/queen|Queen]].  But talk not of the [[motifs/soul|soul]], for to us it is nought, nor has it any value for our service.’
And the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] said to himself: ‘How strange a thing this is!  The Priest telleth me that the [[motifs/soul|soul]] is worth all the gold in the world, and the merchants say that it is not worth a clipped piece of silver.’  And he passed out of the market-place, and went down to the shore of the sea, and began to ponder on what he should do.
And at [[motifs/noon|noon]] he remembered how one of his companions, who was a gatherer of samphire, had told him of a certain young [[characters/witch|Witch]] who dwelt in a [[motifs/cave|cave]] at the head of the bay and was very cunning in her witcheries.  And he set to and ran, so eager was he to get rid of his [[motifs/soul|soul]], and a cloud of [[motifs/dust|dust]] followed him as he sped round the sand of the shore.  By the itching of her palm the young [[characters/witch|Witch]] knew his coming, and she laughed and let down her red hair.  With her red hair falling around her, she stood at the opening of the [[motifs/cave|cave]], and in her hand she had a spray of wild hemlock that was blossoming.
‘What d’ye lack?  What d’ye lack?’ she cried, as he came panting up the steep, and bent down before her.  ‘Fish for thy net, when the [[motifs/wind|wind]] is foul?  I have a little reed-pipe, and when I blow on it the mullet come sailing into the bay.  But it has a price, pretty boy, it has a price.  What d’ye lack?  What d’ye lack?  A [[motifs/storm|storm]] to wreck the ships, and wash the chests of rich treasure ashore?  I have more storms than the [[motifs/wind|wind]] has, for I serve one who is stronger than the [[motifs/wind|wind]], and with a sieve and a pail of [[motifs/water|water]] I can send the great galleys to the [[characters/bottom|bottom]] of the sea.  But I have a price, pretty boy, I have a price.  What d’ye lack?  What d’ye lack?  I know a flower that grows in the valley, none knows it but I.  It has purple [[motifs/leaves|leaves]], and a star in its heart, and its juice is as white as milk.  Shouldst thou touch with this flower the hard lips of the [[characters/queen|Queen]], she would follow thee all over the world.  Out of the bed of the [[archetypes/king|King]] she would rise, and over the whole world she would follow thee.  And it has a price, pretty boy, it has a price.  What d’ye lack?  What d’ye lack?  I can pound a toad in a mortar, and make broth of it, and stir the broth with a dead man’s hand.  Sprinkle it on thine enemy while he sleeps, and he will turn into a black viper, and his own mother will slay him.  With a [[motifs/wheel|wheel]] I can draw the [[motifs/moon|Moon]] from heaven, and in a crystal I can show thee [[motifs/death|Death]].  What d’ye lack?  What d’ye lack?  Tell me thy [[motifs/desire|desire]], and I will give it thee, and thou shalt pay me a price, pretty boy, thou shalt pay me a price.’
‘My [[motifs/desire|desire]] is but for a little thing,’ said the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]], ‘yet hath the Priest been wroth with me, and driven me forth.  It is but for a little thing, and the merchants have mocked at me, and denied me.  Therefore am I come to thee, though men call thee evil, and whatever be thy price I shall pay it.’
‘What wouldst thou?’ asked the [[characters/witch|Witch]], coming near to him.
‘I would send my [[motifs/soul|soul]] away from me,’ answered the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]].
The [[characters/witch|Witch]] grew pale, and shuddered, and hid her face in her blue mantle.  ‘Pretty boy, pretty boy,’ she muttered, ‘that is a terrible thing to do.’
He tossed his brown curls and laughed.  ‘My [[motifs/soul|soul]] is nought to me,’ he answered.  ‘I cannot see it.  I may not touch it.  I do not know it.’
‘What wilt thou give me if I tell thee?’ asked the [[characters/witch|Witch]], looking down at him with her beautiful eyes.
‘Five pieces of gold,’ he said, ‘and my nets, and the wattled house where I live, and the painted boat in which I sail.  Only tell me how to get rid of my [[motifs/soul|soul]], and I will give thee all that I possess.’
She laughed mockingly at him, and struck him with the spray of hemlock.  ‘I can turn the [[motifs/autumn|autumn]] [[motifs/leaves|leaves]] into gold,’ she answered, ‘and I can weave the pale moonbeams into silver if I will it.  He whom I serve is richer than all the kings of this world, and has their dominions.’
‘What then shall I give thee,’ he cried, ‘if thy price be neither gold nor silver?’
The [[characters/witch|Witch]] stroked his hair with her thin white hand.  ‘Thou must [[motifs/dance|dance]] with me, pretty boy,’ she murmured, and she smiled at him as she spoke.
‘Nought but that?’ cried the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] in [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] and he [[motifs/rose|rose]] to his feet.
‘Nought but that,’ she answered, and she smiled at him again.
‘Then at sunset in some secret place we shall [[motifs/dance|dance]] together,’ he said, ‘and after that we have danced thou shalt tell me the thing which I [[motifs/desire|desire]] to know.’
She shook her head.  ‘When the [[motifs/moon|moon]] is full, when the [[motifs/moon|moon]] is full,’ she muttered.  Then she peered all round, and listened.  A blue bird [[motifs/rose|rose]] screaming from its nest and circled over the dunes, and three spotted [[motifs/birds|birds]] rustled through the coarse grey grass and whistled to each other.  There was no other sound save the sound of a wave fretting the smooth pebbles below.  So she reached out her hand, and drew him near to her and put her dry lips close to his ear.
‘To-[[motifs/night|night]] thou must come to the top of the mountain,’ she whispered.  ‘It is a Sabbath, and He will be there.’
The [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] started and looked at her, and she showed her white teeth and laughed.  ‘Who is He of whom thou speakest?’ he asked.
‘It matters not,’ she answered.  ‘Go thou to-[[motifs/night|night]], and stand under the branches of the hornbeam, and wait for my coming.  If a black dog run towards thee, strike it with a rod of willow, and it will go away.  If an owl speak to thee, make it no answer.  When the [[motifs/moon|moon]] is full I shall be with thee, and we will [[motifs/dance|dance]] together on the grass.’
‘But wilt thou swear to me to tell me how I may send my [[motifs/soul|soul]] from me?’ he made question.
She moved out into the sunlight, and through her red hair rippled the [[motifs/wind|wind]].  ‘By the hoofs of the goat I swear it,’ she made answer.
‘Thou art the best of the witches,’ cried the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]], ‘and I will surely [[motifs/dance|dance]] with thee to-[[motifs/night|night]] on the top of the mountain.  I would indeed that thou hadst asked of me either gold or silver.  But such as thy price is thou shalt have it, for it is but a little thing.’  And he doffed his cap to her, and bent his head low, and ran back to the town filled with a great joy.
And the [[characters/witch|Witch]] watched him as he went, and when he had passed from her sight she entered her [[motifs/cave|cave]], and having taken a [[motifs/mirror|mirror]] from a box of carved cedarwood, she set it up on a frame, and burned vervain on lighted charcoal before it, and peered through the coils of the smoke.  And after a [[motifs/time|time]] she clenched her hands in anger.  ‘He should have been mine,’ she muttered, ‘I am as fair as she is.’
And that evening, when the [[motifs/moon|moon]] had risen, the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] climbed up to the top of the mountain, and stood under the branches of the hornbeam.  Like a targe of polished metal the round sea lay at his feet, and the shadows of the fishing-boats moved in the little bay.  A great owl, with yellow sulphurous eyes, called to him by his name, but he made it no answer.  A black dog ran towards him and snarled.  He struck it with a rod of willow, and it went away whining.
At midnight the witches came flying through the air like bats.  ‘Phew!’ they cried, as they lit upon the ground, ‘there is some one here we know not!’ and they sniffed about, and chattered to each other, and made signs.  Last of all came the young [[characters/witch|Witch]], with her red hair streaming in the [[motifs/wind|wind]].  She wore a dress of gold tissue embroidered with peacocks’ eyes, and a little cap of green velvet was on her head.
‘Where is he, where is he?’ shrieked the witches when they saw her, but she only laughed, and ran to the hornbeam, and taking the Fisherman by the hand she led him out into the moonlight and began to [[motifs/dance|dance]].
Round and round they whirled, and the young [[characters/witch|Witch]] jumped so high that he could see the scarlet heels of her shoes.  Then right across the dancers came the sound of the galloping of a [[motifs/horse|horse]], but no [[motifs/horse|horse]] was to be seen, and he felt afraid.
‘Faster,’ cried the [[characters/witch|Witch]], and she threw her arms about his neck, and her breath was hot upon his face.  ‘Faster, faster!’ she cried, and the earth seemed to spin beneath his feet, and his brain grew troubled, and a great terror fell on him, as of some evil thing that was watching him, and at last he became aware that under the [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of a rock there was a figure that had not been there before.
It was a man dressed in a suit of black velvet, cut in the Spanish fashion.  His face was strangely pale, but his lips were like a proud red flower.  He seemed weary, and was leaning back toying in a listless manner with the pommel of his dagger.  On the grass beside him lay a plumed hat, and a pair of riding-gloves gauntleted with gilt lace, and sewn with seed-pearls wrought into a curious device.  A short cloak lined with sables hang from his shoulder, and his delicate white hands were gemmed with rings.  Heavy eyelids drooped over his eyes.
The [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] watched him, as one snared in a spell.  At last their eyes met, and wherever he danced it seemed to him that the eyes of the man were upon him.  He heard the [[characters/witch|Witch]] laugh, and caught her by the waist, and whirled her madly round and round.
Suddenly a dog bayed in the wood, and the dancers stopped, and going up two by two, knelt down, and kissed the man’s hands.  As they did so, a little smile touched his proud lips, as a bird’s wing touches the [[motifs/water|water]] and makes it laugh.  But there was disdain in it.  He kept looking at the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]].
‘Come! let us worship,’ whispered the [[characters/witch|Witch]], and she led him up, and a great [[motifs/desire|desire]] to do as she besought him seized on him, and he followed her.  But when he came close, and without knowing why he did it, he made on his breast the sign of the [[motifs/cross|Cross]], and called upon the holy name.
No sooner had he done so than the witches screamed like hawks and flew away, and the pallid face that had been watching him twitched with a spasm of [[motifs/pain|pain]].  The man went over to a little wood, and whistled.  A jennet with silver trappings came running to meet him.  As he leapt upon the saddle he turned round, and looked at the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] sadly.
And the [[characters/witch|Witch]] with the red hair tried to fly away also, but the Fisherman caught her by her wrists, and held her fast.
‘Loose me,’ she cried, ‘and let me go.  For thou hast named what should not be named, and shown the sign that may not be looked at.’
‘Nay,’ he answered, ‘but I will not let thee go till thou hast told me the secret.’
‘What secret?’ said the [[characters/witch|Witch]], wrestling with him like a wild cat, and biting her foam-flecked lips.
‘Thou knowest,’ he made answer.
Her grass-green eyes grew dim with [[motifs/tears|tears]], and she said to the Fisherman, ‘Ask me anything but that!’
He laughed, and held her all the more tightly.
And when she saw that she could not free herself, she whispered to him, ‘Surely I am as fair as the daughters of the sea, and as comely as those that dwell in the blue waters,’ and she fawned on him and put her face close to his.
But he thrust her back frowning, and said to her, ‘If thou keepest not the promise that thou madest to me I will slay thee for a false [[characters/witch|witch]].’
 She grew grey as a blossom of the Judas tree, and shuddered.  ‘Be it so,’ she muttered.  ‘It is thy [[motifs/soul|soul]] and not mine.  Do with it as thou wilt.’  And she took from her girdle a little knife that had a handle of green viper’s skin, and gave it to him.
‘What shall this serve me?’ he asked of her, wondering.
She was silent for a few moments, and a look of terror came over her face.  Then she brushed her hair back from her forehead, and smiling strangely she said to him, ‘What men call the [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the [[concepts/body|body]] is not the [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the [[concepts/body|body]], but is the [[concepts/body|body]] of the [[motifs/soul|soul]].  Stand on the sea-shore with thy back to the [[motifs/moon|moon]], and cut away from around thy feet thy [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]], which is thy [[motifs/soul|soul]]’s [[concepts/body|body]], and bid thy [[motifs/soul|soul]] leave thee, and it will do so.’
The [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] trembled.  ‘Is this true?’ he murmured.
‘It is true, and I would that I had not told thee of it,’ she cried, and she clung to his knees weeping.
He put her from him and left her in the rank grass, and going to the edge of the mountain he placed the knife in his belt and began to climb down.
And his [[motifs/soul|Soul]] that was within him called out to him and said, ‘Lo!  I have dwelt with thee for all these years, and have been thy servant.  Send me not away from thee now, for what evil have I done thee?’
And the [[characters/young-fisherman|young Fisherman]] laughed.  ‘Thou hast done me no evil, but I have no need of thee,’ he answered.  ‘The world is wide, and there is Heaven also, and Hell, and that dim twilight house that lies between.  Go wherever thou wilt, but trouble me not, for my [[motifs/love|love]] is calling to me.’
And his [[motifs/soul|Soul]] besought him piteously, but he heeded it not, but leapt from crag to crag, being sure-footed as a wild goat, and at last he reached the level ground and the yellow shore of the sea.
Bronze-limbed and [[motifs/well|well]]-knit, like a statue wrought by a Grecian, he stood on the sand with his back to the [[motifs/moon|moon]], and out of the foam came white arms that beckoned to him, and out of the waves [[motifs/rose|rose]] dim forms that did him homage.  Before him lay his [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]], which was the [[concepts/body|body]] of his [[motifs/soul|soul]], and behind him hung the [[motifs/moon|moon]] in the honey-coloured air.
And his [[motifs/soul|Soul]] said to him, ‘If indeed thou must drive me from thee, send me not forth without a heart.  The world is cruel, give me thy heart to take with me.’

Poiché ti ho lasciata in modo malvagio e sono andato per la mia rovina, il tuo amore è sempre rimasto con me, forte e invincibile, anche di fronte al male e al bene. Ora che sei morta, anch’io morirò con te.” La sua anima gli chiese di lasciarla andare, ma lui non volle, tanto era grande il suo amore. Il mare si avvicinò per sommergerlo; quando capì che la fine era vicina, baciò con labbra folli le fredde labbra della sirena. E il suo cuore si spezzò. Ma grazie all’intensità del suo amore, l’anima poté rientrare in lui e unirsi a lui come prima. Il mare sommerse il giovane pescatore. La mattina seguente, il sacerdote uscì per benedire il mare, poiché era stato turbato; con lui c’erano monaci, musicisti, portatori di candele e molti altri. Quando arrivò sulla riva, vide il giovane pescatore annegato tra le onde, con il corpo della piccola sirena stretto tra le braccia. Il sacerdote fece il segno della croce e gridò: “Non benedirò il mare né nulla che vi sia dentro! Maledetti siano i popoli del mare e tutti coloro che commerciano con loro. Quanto a colui che, per amore, ha abbandonato Dio e ora giace qui con la sua amata uccisa dalla volontà di Dio. Prendete i suoi corpi e seppelliteli nell’angolo del Campo dei Lavandai; non lasciate alcun segno sopra di loro, affinché nessuno conosca il luogo della loro sepoltura. Poiché maledetti sono stati in vita, maledetti saranno anche nella morte.” La gente fece come gli era stato ordinato. Nel Campo dei Lavandai, dove non crescevano erbe profumate, scavarono una fossa profonda e vi seppellirono i corpi. Al termine del terzo anno, in un giorno sacro, il sacerdote andò nella cappella per mostrare alla gente le ferite del Signore e parlare loro della collera di Dio.
E quando si fu vestito con le sue vesti sacre e entrò nel tempio per inchinarsi davanti all’altare, vide che l’altare era coperto di strane [[motifs/flowers|fiori]] mai viste prima. Erano strani da vedere, di una bellezza sorprendente; la loro bellezza lo turbava profondamente, e il loro profumo era dolce per lui. Si sentì felice, ma non capiva perché.  
Dopo aver aperto l’arca sacra, accese l’incensiere al suo interno, mostrò alla gente la bella ostia e poi la nascose di nuovo dietro il velo. Iniziò quindi a parlare alla folla, desideroso di parlarle della collera di Dio. Ma la bellezza di quei fiori bianchi lo turbava ancora; il loro profumo era dolce per lui, e dalle sue labbra uscirono altre parole: non parlò della collera di Dio, ma del Dio il cui nome è [[motifs/love|Amore]]. E non sapeva perché avesse detto così.  
Quando finì di parlare, la gente pianse; il sacerdote tornò nella sacrestia, con gli occhi pieni di lacrime. I diaconi entrarono e iniziarono a spogliarlo delle sue vesti sacre: l’alba, il cinturone, il maniple. Lui stava lì, come se fosse in un sogno.  
Dopo che lo ebbero spogliato, lui li guardò e chiese: “Quali fiori sono quelli sull’altare? Da dove vengono?”  
Gli risposero: “Non sappiamo di che tipo siano. Ma vengono dall’angolo del campo dei lavandai.” Il sacerdote tremò, tornò nella sua casa e pregò.  
La mattina seguente, ancora prima dell’alba, uscì con i monaci, i musicisti, i portatori di candele. E andò sulla riva del mare; benedisse il mare e tutte le creature che vi abitavano. Benedisse anche i fauni, quelle piccole creature che danzavano nei boschi, quelle dallo sguardo luminoso che si nascondevano tra le foglie. Benedisse tutto ciò che esisteva nel mondo di Dio, e la gente fu piena di gioia e meraviglia.  
Tuttavia, da allora non crescerono più fiori di alcun tipo nell’angolo del campo dei lavandai; quel campo rimase sterile, proprio come prima. E la gente del mare non venne più nella baia, perché andò in un’altra parte del mare.
