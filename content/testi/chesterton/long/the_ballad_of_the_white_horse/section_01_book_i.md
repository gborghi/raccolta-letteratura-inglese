BOOK I  
THE VISION OF THE [[King|KING]]  

THE BALLAD OF THE  
WHITE [[Horse|HORSE]]  
EFORE the gods that made the gods  
       Had seen their sunrise pass,  
     The White [[Horse]] of the White [[Horse]] Vale  
       Was cut out of the grass.  
     Before the gods that made the gods [5]  
       Had drunk at [[Dawn|dawn]] their fill,  
     The White [[Horse]] of the White [[Horse]] Vale  
       Was hoary on the [[Hill|hill]].  
     Age beyond age on British land,  
       Aeons on aeons gone, [10]  
     Was peace and war in western hills,  
       And the White [[Horse]] looked on.  
     For the White [[Horse]] knew England  
       When there was none to know;  
     He saw the first oar break or bend, is  
     He saw heaven fall and the world end,  
       O God, how long ago.  
     For the end of the world was long ago—  
       And all we dwell to-day  
     As children of some second birth, [20]  
     Like a strange people left on earth  
       After a judgment day.  
     For the end of the world was long ago,  
       When the ends of the world waxed free,  
     When Rome was sunk in a waste of slaves, [25]  
       And the sun drowned in the sea.  
     When Caesar’s sun fell out of the sky  
       And whoso hearkened right  
     Could only hear the plunging  
       Of the nations in the [[Night|night]]. [30]  
     When the ends of the earth came marching in  
       To torch and cresset gleam,  
     And the roads of the world that lead to Rome  
     Were filled with faces that moved like foam,  
       Like faces in [[A Dream|a dream]]. [35]  
     And men rode out of the eastern lands,  
       Broad [[River|river]] and burning plain;  
     Trees that are Titan [[Flowers|flowers]] to see,  
     And tiger skies, striped horribly,  
       With tints of tropic rain. [40]  
     Where Ind’s enamelled peaks arise  
       Around that inmost one,  
     Where ancient eagles on its brink,  
     Vast as archangels, gather and drink  
       The sacrament of the sun. [45]  
     And men brake out of the northern lands,  
       Enormous lands [[Alone|alone]],  
     Where a spell is laid upon life and lust  
     And the rain is changed to a silver [[Dust|dust]]  
       And the sea to a great green stone. [50]  
     And a Shape that moveth murkily  
       In mirrors of ice and [[Night|night]],  
     Hath blanched with [[Fear|fear]] all beasts and [[Birds|birds]],  
     As [[Death|death]] and a shock of evil words  
       Blast a man’s hair with white. [55]  
     And the cry of the palms and the purple moons,  
       Or the cry of the [[Frost|frost]] and foam,  
     Swept ever around an inmost place,  
     And the din of distant race on race  
       Cried and replied round Rome. [60]  
     And there was [[Death|death]] on the Emperor  
       And [[Night|night]] upon the Pope;  
     And Alfred, hiding in deep grass,  
       Hardened his heart with [[Hope|hope]].  
     A sea-folk blinder than the sea [65]  
       Broke all about his land,  
     But Alfred up against them bare  
     And gripped the ground and grasped the air,  
       Staggered, and strove to stand.  
     He bent them back with spear and spade, [70]  
         With desperate dyke and wall,  
     With foemen leaning on his shield  
     And roaring on him when he reeled  
       And no help came at all.  
     He broke them with a broken [[Sword|sword]] [75]  
       A little towards the sea,  
     And for one hour of panting peace,  
     Ringed with a roar that would not cease,  
     With golden [[Crown|crown]] and girded fleece  
       Made laws under a tree. [80]  
• • • • • • •  
     The Northmen came about our land  
       A Christless chivalry:  
     Who knew not of the arch or pen,  
     Great, beautiful half-witted men  
       From the sunrise and the sea. [85]  
     Misshapen ships stood on the deep  
       Full of strange gold and [[Fire|fire]],  
     And hairy men, as huge as sin,  
     With horned heads, came wading in  
       Through the long, low sea-mire. [90]  
     Our towns were shaken of tall kings  
       With scarlet beards like [[Blood|blood]];  
     The world turned empty where they trod,  
     They took the kindly [[Cross|cross]] of God  
       And cut it up for wood. [95]  
     Their souls were drifting as the sea,  
       And all good towns and lands  
     They only saw with heavy eyes,  
       And broke with heavy hands.  
     Their gods were sadder than the sea, [100]  
       Gods of a wandering will,  
     Who cried for [[Blood|blood]] like beasts at [[Night|night]],  
       Sadly, from [[Hill|hill]] to [[Hill|hill]].  
     They seemed as trees walking the earth,  
       As witless and as tall, [105]  
     Yet they took hold upon the heavens  
       And no help came at all.  
     They bred like [[Birds|birds]] in English woods,  
       They rooted like the [[Rose|rose]],  
     When Alfred came to Athelney [110]  
       To hide him from their bows.  
     There was not English armour left,  
       Nor any English thing,  
     When Alfred came to Athelney  
       To be an English [[King|king]]. [115]  
     For earthquake swallowing earthquake  
       Uprent the Wessex tree;  
     The whirlpool of the pagan sway  
     Had swirled his sires as sticks away  
       When a flood smites the sea. [120]  
     And the great kings of Wessex  
       Wearied and sank in gore,  
     And even their [[Ghosts|ghosts]] in that great stress  
     Grew greyer and greyer, less and less,  
     With the lords that died in Lyonesse [125]  
       And the [[King|king]] that comes no more.  
     And the God of the Golden Dragon  
       Was dumb upon his throne,  
     And the lord of the Golden Dragon  
       Ran in the woods [[Alone|alone]]. [130]  
     And if ever he climbed the crest of luck  
       And set the [[Flag|flag]] before,  
     Returning as a [[Wheel|wheel]] returns,  
     Came ruin and the rain that burns,  
       And all began once more. [135]  
     And naught was left [[King Alfred]]  
       But shameful [[Tears|tears]] of rage,  
     In the island in the [[River|river]]  
       In the end of all his age.  
     In the island in the [[River|river]] [140]  
       He was broken to his knee;  
     And he read, writ with an iron pen,  
     That God had wearied of Wessex men  
     And given their country, field and fen,  
       To the devils of the sea. [145]  
     And he saw in a little picture,  
       Tiny and far away,  
     His mother sitting in Egbert’s hall,  
     And a book she showed him, very small,  
     Where a sapphire Mary sat in stall [150]  
       With a golden [[Christ]] at play.  
     It was wrought in the monk’s slow manner,  
       From silver and sanguine shell,  
     Where the scenes are little and terrible,  
       Keyholes of heaven and hell. [155]  
     In the [[River|river]] island of Athelney,  
       With the [[River|river]] running past,  
     In colours of such simple creed  
     All things sprang at him, sun and weed,  
     Till the grass grew to be grass indeed [160]  
       And the tree was a tree at last.  
     Fearfully plain the [[Flowers|flowers]] grew,  
       Like the [[Child|child]]’s book to read,  
     Or like a friend’s face seen in a glass;  
     He looked; and there Our Lady was, [165]  
     She stood and stroked the tall live grass  
       As a man strokes his steed.  
     Her face was like an open word  
       When brave men speak and choose,  
     The very colours of her coat  
       Were better than good news. [170]  
     She spoke not, nor, turned not,  
       Nor any sign she cast,  
     Only she stood up straight and free,  
     Between the [[Flowers|flowers]] in Athelney, [175]  
       And the [[River|river]] running past.  
     One dim ancestral [[Jewel|jewel]] hung  
       On his ruined armour grey,  
     He rent and cast it at her feet:  
     Where, after centuries, with slow feet, [180]  
     Men came from hall and school and street  
       And found it where it lay.  
     “Mother of God,” the wanderer said,  
       “I am but a common [[King|king]],  
     Nor will I ask what saints may ask, [185]  
       To see a secret thing.  
     “The gates of heaven are fearful gates,  
       Worse than the gates of hell;  
     Not I would break the splendours barred  
     Or seek to know the thing they guard, [190]  
       Which is too good to tell.  
     “But for this earth most pitiful,  
       This little land I know,  
     If that which is for ever is,  
     Or if our hearts shall break with bliss, [195]  
       Seeing the stranger go?  
     “When our last bow is broken, [[Queen]],  
       And our last javelin cast,  
     Under some sad, green evening sky,  
     Holding a ruined [[Cross|cross]] on high, [200]  
     Under warm westland grass to lie,  
       Shall we come [[Home|home]] at last?”  
     And a voice came human but high up,  
       Like a cottage climbed among  
     The clouds; or a serf of hut and croft [205]  
     That sits by his hovel [[Fire|fire]] as oft,  
     But hears on his old bare roof aloft  
       A belfry burst in [[Song|song]].  
     “The gates of heaven are lightly locked,  
       We do not guard our gain, [210]  
     The heaviest hind may easily  
     Come silently and suddenly  
       Upon me in a lane,  
     “And any little maid that walks  
       In good thoughts apart, [215]  
     May break the guard of the Three Kings  
     And see the dear and dreadful things  
       I hid within my heart.  
     “The meanest man in grey fields gone  
       Behind the set of sun, [220]  
     Heareth between star and other star,  
     Through the door of the darkness fallen ajar,  
     The council, eldest of things that are,  
       The talk of the Three in One.  
     “The gates of heaven are lightly locked, [225]  
       We do not guard our gold,  
     Men may uproot where worlds begin,  
     Or read the name of the nameless sin;  
     But if he fail or if he win  
       To no good man is told. [230]  
     “The men of the East may spell the [[Stars|stars]],  
       And times and triumphs mark,  
     But the men signed of the [[Cross|cross]] of [[Christ]]  
       Go gaily in the dark.  
     “The men of the East may search the scrolls, [235]  
       For sure fates and fame,  
     But the men that drink the [[Blood|blood]] of God  
       Go singing to their shame.  
     “The wise men know what wicked things  
       Are written on the sky, [240]  
     They trim sad Lamps, they touch sad strings,  
     Hearing the heavy purple wings,  
     Where the forgotten seraph kings  
       Still plot how God shall die.  
     “The wise men know all evil things [245]  
       Under the twisted trees,  
     Where the perverse in pleasure pine  
     And men are weary of green wine  
       And sick of crimson seas.  
     “But you and all the kind of [[Christ]] [250]  
       Are ignorant and brave,  
     And you have wars you hardly win  
       And souls you hardly save.  
     “I tell you naught for your comfort,  
       Yea, naught for your [[Desire|desire]], [255]  
     Save that the sky grows darker yet  
       And the sea rises higher.  
     “[[Night]] shall be thrice [[Night|night]] over you,  
       And heaven an iron cope.  
     Do you have joy without a cause, [260]  
       Yea, [[Faith|faith]] without a [[Hope|hope]]?”  
     Even as she spoke she was not,  
       Nor any word said he,  
     He only heard, still as he stood  
     Under the old [[Night|night]]’s nodding hood, [265]  
     The sea-folk breaking down the wood  
       Like a high tide from sea.  
     He only heard the heathen men,  
       Whose eyes are blue and bleak,  
     Singing about some cruel thing [270]  
     Done by a great and smiling [[King|king]]  
       In daylight on a deck.  
     He only heard the heathen men,  
       Whose eyes are blue and blind,  
     Singing what shameful things are done [275]  
     Between the sunlit sea and the sun  
       When the land is left behind.
