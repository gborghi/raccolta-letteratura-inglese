A Midsummer [[Night]]'s Dream

The greatest of Shakespeare's comedies is also, from a certain point
of view, the greatest of his plays.  No one would maintain that it
occupied this position in the matter of psychological study if by
psychological study we mean the study of individual characters in a play.
No one would maintain that [[Puck]] was a character in the sense
that [[Falstaff]] is a character, or that the critic stood awed before
the psychology of Peaseblossom.  But there is a sense in which the play
is perhaps a greater triumph of psychology than <Hamlet> itself.
It may [[Well|well]] be questioned whether in any other literary work in
the world is so vividly rendered a social and spiritual atmosphere.
There is an atmosphere in <Hamlet>, for instance, a somewhat
murky and even melodramatic one, but it is subordinate to
the great character, and morally inferior to him; the darkness
is only a background for the isolated star of intellect.
But <A Midsummer Night's Dream> is a psychological study,
not of a solitary man, but of a spirit that unites mankind.
The six men may sit talking in an inn; they may not know each other's
names or see each other's faces before or after, but '[[Night|night]] or wine
or great stories, or some rich and branching discussion may make
them all at one, if not absolutely with each other, at least
with that invisible seventh man who is the harmony of all of them.
That seventh man is the [[Hero|hero]] of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.

A study of the play from a literary or philosophical point of view
must therefore be founded upon some serious realisation of what this
atmosphere is.  In a lecture upon <As You Like It>, Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]]
made a suggestion which is an admirable example of his amazing
ingenuity and of his one most interesting limitation.  In maintaining
that the light sentiment and optimism of the [[Comedy|comedy]] were regarded
by Shakespeare merely as the characteristics of a more or less
cynical pot-boiler, he actually suggested that the title "As You Like It"
was a taunting address to the public in disparagement of their taste
and the dramatist's own work.  If Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] had conceived
of Shakespeare as insisting that Ben Jonson should wear Jaeger
underclothing or join the Blue Ribbon Army, or distribute little
pamphlets for the non-payment of rates, he could scarcely have conceived
anything more violently opposed to the whole spirit of Elizabethan
[[Comedy|comedy]] than the spiteful and priggish modernism of such a taunt.
Shakespeare might make the fastidious and cultivated [[Hamlet]],
moving in his own [[Melancholy|melancholy]] and purely mental world,
warn players against an over-indulgence towards the rabble.
But the very [[Soul|soul]] and meaning of the great comedies is that of an
uproarious communion between the public and the play, a communion
so chaotic that whole scenes of silliness and violence lead us
almost to think that some of the "rowdies" from the pit have climbed
over the footlights.  The title "As you Like It", is, of course,
an expression of utter carelessness, but it is not the bitter
carelessness which Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] fantastically reads into it;
it is the god-like and inexhaustible carelessness of a happy man.
And the simple proof of this is that there are scores of these genially
taunting titles scattered through the whole of Elizabethan [[Comedy|comedy]].
Is "As You Like It" a title demanding a dark and ironic
explanation in a school of [[Comedy|comedy]] which called its plays
"What You Will", "A Mad World, My Masters", "If It Be Not Good,
the Devil Is In It", "The Devil is an Ass", "An Humorous Day's Mirth",
and "A Midsummer [[Night]]'s Dream"?  Every one of these titles is flung
at the head of the public as a drunken lord might fling a purse
at his footman.  Would Mr. [[Shaw]] maintain that "If It Be Not Good,
the Devil Is In It", was the opposite of "As You Like It",
and was a solemn invocation of the [[Supernatural|supernatural]] powers to testify
to the care and perfection of the literary workmanship?
The one explanation is as Elizabethan as the other.

Now in the reason for this modern and pedantic error lies the whole
secret and difficulty of such plays as <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.
The sentiment of such a play, so far as it can be summed up at all,
can be summed up in one sentence.  It is the mysticism of happiness.
That is to say, it is the conception that as man lives upon
a borderland he may find himself in the spiritual or [[Supernatural|supernatural]]
atmosphere, not only through being profoundly sad or meditative,
but by being extravagantly happy.  The [[Soul|soul]] might be rapt
out of the [[Body|body]] in an agony of sorrow, or a trance of ecstasy;
but it might also be rapt out of the [[Body|body]] in a paroxysm of laughter.
Sorrow we know can go beyond itself; so, according to Shakespeare,
can pleasure go beyond itself and become something dangerous and unknown.
And the reason that the logical and destructive modern school,
of which Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] is an example, does not grasp this
purely exuberant [[Nature|nature]] of the comedies is simply 'that their
logical and destructive attitude have rendered impossible the very
experience of this preternatural exuberance.  We cannot realise
<As You Like It> if we are always considering it as we understand it.
We cannot have <A Midsummer's Night Dream> if our one object in life
is to keep ourselves awake with the black coffee of criticism.
The whole question which is balanced, and balanced nobly and fairly,
in <A Midsummer Night's Dream>, is whether the life of waking,
or the life of the vision, is the real life, the <sine quâ non> of man.
But it is difficult to see what superiority for the purpose
of judging is possessed by people whose [[Pride|pride]] it is not to live
the life of vision at all.  At least it is questionable whether
the Elizabethan did not know more about both worlds than the modern
intellectual it is not altogether improbably that Shakespeare
would not only have had a clearer vision of the fairies, but would
have shot very much straighter at a deer and netted much more money
for his performances than a member of the Stage Society.

In pure poetry and the intoxication of words, Shakespeare never [[Rose|rose]]
higher than he rises in this play.  But in spite of this fact,
the supreme literary merit of <A Midsummer Night's Dream> is
a merit of design.  The amazing symmetry, the amazing artistic
and moral [[Beauty|beauty]] of that design, can be stated very briefly.
The story opens in the sane and common world with the pleasant
seriousness of very young [[lovers]] and very young friends.
Then, as the figures advance into the tangled wood of young troubles
and stolen happiness, a change and bewilderment begins to fall on them.
They lose their way and their wits for they are in the heart
of fairyland.  Their words, their hungers, their very figures
grow more and more dim and fantastic, like [[Dreams|dreams]] within [[Dreams|dreams]],
in the [[Supernatural|supernatural]] mist of [[Puck]].  Then the dream-fumes begin
to clear, and characters and spectators begin to awaken together
to the noise of horns and [[dogs]] and the clean and bracing morning.
[[Theseus]], the incarnation of a happy and generous rationalism,
expounds in hackneyed and superb lines the sane view of such
psychic experiences, pointing out with a reverent and sympathetic
scepticism that all these fairies and spells are themselves
but the emanations, the unconscious masterpieces, of man himself.
The whole company falls back into a splendid human laughter.
There is a rush for banqueting and private theatricals, and over all
these things ripples one of those frivolous and inspired conversations
in which every good saying seems to die in giving birth to another.
If ever the son of a man in his wanderings was at [[Home|home]] and
drinking by the fireside, he is at [[Home|home]] in the [[house]] of [[Theseus]].
All the [[Dreams|dreams]] have been forgotten, as a [[Melancholy|melancholy]] dream remembered
throughout the morning might be forgotten in the human certainty
of any other triumphant evening party; and so the play seems
naturally ended.  It began on the earth and it ends on the earth.
Thus to round off the whole midsummer [[Night|night]]'s dream in an eclipse
of daylight is an effect of genius.  But of this [[Comedy|comedy]],
as I have said, the mark is that genius goes beyond itself;
and one touch is added which makes the play colossal.
[[Theseus]] and his train retire with a crashing finale, full of [[Humour|humour]]
and wisdom and things set right, and [[Silence|silence]] falls on the [[house]].
Then there comes a faint sound of little feet, and for a moment,
as it were, the' elves look into the [[house]], asking which is the reality.
"Suppose we are the realities and they the shadows."  If that ending
were acted properly any modern man would feel shaken to his marrow
if he had to walk [[Home|home]] from the theatre through a country lane.

It is a trite matter, of course, though in a general criticism
a more or less indispensable one to comment upon another point
of artistic perfection, the extraordinarily human and accurate
manner in which the play catches the atmosphere of [[A Dream|a dream]].
The chase and tangle and frustration of the incidents and
personalities are [[Well|well]] known to every one who has dreamt of
perpetually falling over precipices or perpetually missing trains.
While following out clearly and legally the necessary narrative
of the drama, the author contrives to include every one of the main
peculiarities of the exasperating dream.  Here is the pursuit
of the man we cannot catch, the flight from the man we cannot see;
here is the perpetual returning to the same place, here is the crazy
alteration in the very objects of our [[Desire|desire]], the substitution
of one face for another face, the putting of the wrong souls
in the wrong bodies, the fantastic disloyalties of the [[Night|night]],
all this is as obvious as it is important.  It is perhaps somewhat
more -worth remarking that there is about this confusion of [[Comedy|comedy]]
yet another essential characteristic of [[Dreams|dreams]].  [[A Dream|A dream]] can commonly
be described as possessing an utter discordance of incident combined
with a curious [[unity]] of mood; everything changes but the dreamer.
It may begin with anything and end with anything, but if the dreamer
is sad at the end he will be sad as if by prescience at the beginning;
if he is cheerful at the beginning he will be cheerful if the [[Stars|stars]] fall.
<A Midsummer Night's Dream> has in a most singular degree effected
this difficult, this almost desperate subtlety.  The events in the
wandering wood are in themselves, and regarded as in broad daylight,
not merely [[Melancholy|melancholy]] but bitterly cruel and ignominious.
But yet by the spreading of an atmosphere as [[Magic|magic]] as the fog of [[Puck]],

Shakespeare contrives to make the whole matter mysteriously hilarious
while it is palpably tragic, and mysteriously charitable, while it is
in itself cynical.  He contrives somehow to rob [[Tragedy|tragedy]] and treachery
of their full sharpness, just as a toothache or a deadly danger from
a tiger, or a precipice, is robbed of its sharpness in a pleasant dream.
The creation of a brooding sentiment like this, a sentiment not merely
independent of but actually opposed to the events, is a much greater
triumph of art than the creation of the character of [[Othello]].

It is difficult to approach critically so great a figure as that of
[[Bottom]] the Weaver.  He is greater and more mysterious than [[Hamlet]],
because the interest of such men as [[Bottom]] consists of a rich
subconsciousness, and that of [[Hamlet]] in the comparatively superficial
matter of a rich consciousness.  And it is especially difficult in
the present age which has become hag-ridden with the mere intellect.
We are the victims of a curious confusion whereby being great is
supposed to have something to do with being clever, as if there
were the smallest reason to suppose that [[Achilles]] was clever,
as if there were not on the contrary a great deal of internal
evidence to indicate that he was next door to a [[Fool|fool]].  [[Greatness]] is
a certain indescribable but perfectly familiar and palpable quality
of size in the personality, of steadfastness, of strong flavour,
of easy and natural [[Self|self]]-expression. Such a man is as firm as a tree
and as unique as a rhinoceros, and he might quite easily be as
stupid as either of them.  Fully as much as the great poet towers
above the small poet the great [[Fool|fool]] towers above the small [[Fool|fool]].
We have all of us known rustics like [[Bottom]] the Weaver, men whose
faces would be blank with idiocy if we tried for -ten days to explain
the meaning of the National Debt, but who are yet great men,
akin to Sigurd and Hercules, heroes of the morning of the earth,
because their words were their own words, their memories their
own memories, and their [[Vanity|vanity]] as large and simple as a great [[Hill|hill]].
We have all of us known friends in our own circle, men whom the
intellectuals might justly describe as brainless, but whose presence
in a room was like a [[Fire|fire]] roaring in the grate changing everything,
lights and shadows and the air, whose entrances and exits were in some
strange fashion events, whose point of view once expressed haunts
and persuades the mind and almost intimidates it, whose manifest
absurdity clings to the fancy like the [[Beauty|beauty]] of first-[[Love|love]],
and whose follies are recounted like the legends of a paladin.
These ate great men, there are millions of them in the world,
though very few perhaps in the [[house|House]] of Commons.  It is not in
the cold halls of cleverness where celebrities seem to be important
that we should look for the great.  An intellectual salon is merely
a training-ground for one faculty, and is akin to a fencing class
or a rifle corps.  It is in our own homes and environments,
from Croydon to St. John's Wood, in old nurses, and gentlemen
with hobbies, and talkative spinisters and vast incomparable butlers,
that we may feel the presence of that [[Blood|blood]] of the gods.
And this creature so hard to describe, so easy to remember,
the august and memorable [[Fool|fool]], has never been so sumptuously painted
as in the [[Bottom]] of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.

[[Bottom]] has the supreme mark of this real [[Greatness|greatness]] in that like
the true saint or the true [[Hero|hero]] he only differs from humanity
in being as it were more human than humanity.  It is not true,
as the idle materialists of today suggest, that compared to the majority
of men the [[Hero|hero]] appears cold and dehumanised; it is the majority
who appear cold and dehumanised in the presence of [[Greatness|greatness]].
[[Bottom]], like [[Don Quixote]] and Uncle Toby and Mr. [[Richard]] Swiveller
and the rest of the Titans, has a huge and unfathomable weakness,
his silliness is on a great scale, and when he blows his own trumpet
it is like the trumpet of the [[Resurrection]].  The other rustics in
the play accept his leadership not merely naturally but exuberantly;
they have to the full that primary and savage unselfishness,
that uproarious abnegation which makes simple men take pleasure
in falling short of a [[Hero|hero]], that unquestionable element of basic
human [[Nature|nature]] which has never been expressed, outside this play,
so perfectly as in the incomparable chapter at the beginning
of <Evan Harrington> in which the praises of The Great Mel are
sung with a [[Lyric|lyric]] energy by the tradesmen whom he has cheated.
Twopenny sceptics write of the egoism of primal human [[Nature|nature]];
it is reserved for great men like Shakespeare and Meredith to detect
and make vivid this rude and subconscious unselfishness which is
older than [[Self|self]].  They [[Alone|alone]] with their insatiable tolerance can
perceive all the spiritual devotion in the [[Soul|soul]] of a snob.

And it is this natural play between the rich simplicity of [[Bottom]]
and the simple simplicity of his comrades which constitutes
the unapproachable excellence of the farcical scenes in this play.
[[Bottom]]'s sensibility to literature is perfectly fiery and genuine,
a great deal more genuine than that of a great many cultivated
critics of literature - "the raging rocks, and shivering
shocks shall break the locks of [[Prison|prison]] gates, and Phibbus'
car shall shine from far, and make and mar the foolish fates",
is exceedingly good poetical diction with a real throb and swell
in it, and if it is slightly and almost imperceptibly deficient
in the matter of sense, it is certainly every bit as sensible
as a good many other rhetorical speeches in Shakespeare put into
the mouths of kings and [[lovers]] and even the [[Spirits of the Dead|spirits of the dead]].
If [[Bottom]] liked cant for its own sake the fact only constitutes
another point of sympathy between him and his literary creator.
But the style of the thing, though deliberately bombastic and ludicrous,
is quite literary, the alliteration falls like wave upon wave,
and the whole verse, like a billow mounts higher and higher
before it crashes.  There is nothing mean about this folly;
nor is there in the whole realm of literature a figure so free
from vulgarity.  The man vitally base and foolish sings
"The Honeysuckle and the Bee"; he does not rant about "raging rocks"
and "the car of Phibbus".  Dickens, who more perhaps than any
modern man had the mental hospitality and the thoughtless wisdom
of Shakespeare, perceived and expressed admirably the same [[Truth|truth]].
He perceived, that is to say, that quite indefensible idiots
have very often a real sense of, and enthusiasm for letters.
Mr. Micawber loved eloquence and poetry with his whole immortal [[Soul|soul]];
words and visionary pictures kept him alive in the absence of food
and money, as they might have kept a saint fasting in a [[Desert|desert]].
[[Dick Swiveller]] did not make his inimitable quotations from Moore and
Byron merely as flippant digressions.  He made them because he loved
a great school of poetry.  The sincere [[Love|love]] of books has nothing to do
with cleverness or stupidity any more than any other sincere [[Love|love]].
It is a quality of character, a freshness, a power of pleasure,
a power of [[Faith|faith]].  A silly person may delight in reading
masterpieces just as a silly person may delight in picking [[Flowers|flowers]].
A [[Fool|fool]] may be in [[Love|love]] with a poet as he may be in [[Love|love]] with a woman.
And the triumph of [[Bottom]] is that he loves rhetoric and his own
taste in the arts, and this is all that can be achieved by [[Theseus]],
or for the matter of that by Cosimo di Medici.  It is worth remarking
as an extremely fine touch in the picture of [[Bottom]] that his literary
taste is almost everywhere concerned with sound rather than sense.
He begins the rehearsal with a boisterous readiness, "Thisby,
the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete."  "Odours, odours," says Quince,
in remonstrance, and the word is accepted in accordance with
the cold and heavy rules which require an element of meaning in a
poetical passage.  But "Thisby, the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete",
[[Bottom]]'s version, is an immeasurably finer and more resonant line.
The "i" which he inserts is an inspiration of metricism.

There is another aspect of this great play which ought to be
kept familiarly in the mind.  Extravagant as is the masquerade
of the story, it is a very perfect aesthetic harmony down to such
<coup-de-maître> as the name of [[Bottom]], or the flower called
[[Love]] in Idleness.  In the whole matter it may be said that there
is one accidental discord; that is in the name of [[Theseus]],
and the whole [[City|city]] of Athens in which the events take place.
Shakespeare's description of Athens in <A Midsummer Night's Dream>
is the best description of England that he or any one else ever wrote.
[[Theseus]] is quite obviously only an English squire, fond of hunting,
kindly to his tenants, hospitable with a certain flamboyant [[Vanity|vanity]].
The mechanics are English mechanics, talking to each other with
the queer formality of the poor.  Above all, the fairies are English;
to compare them with the beautiful patrician spirits of Irish legend,
for instance, is suddenly to discover that we have, after all,
a folk-lore and a mythology, or had it at least in Shakespeare's day.
Robin Goodfellow, upsetting the old women's ale, or pulling
the stool from under them, has nothing of the poignant
Celtic [[Beauty|beauty]]; his is the [[Horse|horse]]-play of the invisible world.
Perhaps it is some debased inheritance of English life which makes
American [[Ghosts|ghosts]] so fond of quite undignified practical jokes.
But this [[Union|union]] of mystery with farce is a note of the medieval English.
The play is the last glimpse of Merrie England, that distant
but shining and quite indubitable country.  It would be difficult
indeed to define wherein lay the peculiar [[Truth|truth]] of the phrase
"merrie England", though some conception of it is quite necessary
to the comprehension of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>. In some
cases at least, it may be said to lie in this, that the English
of the Middle Ages and the [[Renaissance]], unlike the England
of today, could conceive of the idea of a merry supernaturalism.
Amid all the great work of [[Puritanism]] the damning indictment
of it consists in one fact, that there was one only of the fables
of Christendom that it retained and renewed, and that was
the belief in witchcraft.  It cast away the generous and wholesome
superstition, it approved only of the morbid and the dangerous.
In their treatment of the great national fairy-tale of good
and evil, the Puritans killed St. George but carefully preserved
the Dragon, And this seventeenth-century tradition of dealing
with the psychic life still lies like a great [[Shadow|shadow]] over England
and [[America]], so that if we glance at a [[Novel|novel]] about occultism we
may be perfectly certain that it deals with sad or evil destiny.
Whatever else we expect we certainly should never expect to find
in it spirits such as those in <Aylwin> as inspirers of a tale of
tomfoolery like the <Wrong Box> or <The Londoners>. That impossibility
is the disappearance of "merrie England" and Robin Goodfellow.
It was a land to us incredible, the land of a jolly occultism where
the peasant cracked jokes with his patron saint, and only cursed
the fairies good-humouredly, as he might curse a lazy servant.
Shakespeare is English in everything, above all in his weaknesses.
just as [[London]], one of the greatest cities in the world, shows more
slums and hides more beauties than any other, so Shakespeare [[Alone|alone]]
among the four giants of poetry is a careless writer, and lets us
come upon his splendours by accident, as we come upon an old [[City]]
church in the twist of a [[City|city]] street.  He is English in nothing
so much as in that noble cosmopolitan unconsciousness which makes him
look eastward with the eyes of a [[Child|child]] towards Athens or [[Verona]].
He loved to talk of the glory of foreign lands, but he talked
of them with the tongue and unquenchable spirit of England.
It is too much the custom of a later patriotism to reverse this
method and talk of England from morning till [[Night|night]], but to talk
of her in a manner totally un-English. Casualness, incongruities,
and a certain fine absence of mind are in the temper of England;
the unconscious man with the ass's head is no bad type of the people.
Materialistic philosophers and mechanical politicians have certainly
succeeded in some cases in giving him a greater [[unity]].  The only
question is, to which animal has he been thus successfully conformed?

A Midsummer [[Night]]'s Dream

The greatest of Shakespeare's comedies is also, from a certain point
of view, the greatest of his plays.  No one would maintain that it
occupied this position in the matter of psychological study if by
psychological study we mean the study of individual characters in a play.
No one would maintain that [[Puck]] was a character in the sense
that [[Falstaff]] is a character, or that the critic stood awed before
the psychology of Peaseblossom.  But there is a sense in which the play
is perhaps a greater triumph of psychology than <Hamlet> itself.
It may [[Well|well]] be questioned whether in any other literary work in
the world is so vividly rendered a social and spiritual atmosphere.
There is an atmosphere in <Hamlet>, for instance, a somewhat
murky and even melodramatic one, but it is subordinate to
the great character, and morally inferior to him; the darkness
is only a background for the isolated star of intellect.
But <A Midsummer Night's Dream> is a psychological study,
not of a solitary man, but of a spirit that unites mankind.
The six men may sit talking in an inn; they may not know each other's
names or see each other's faces before or after, but '[[Night|night]] or wine
or great stories, or some rich and branching discussion may make
them all at one, if not absolutely with each other, at least
with that invisible seventh man who is the harmony of all of them.
That seventh man is the [[Hero|hero]] of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.

A study of the play from a literary or philosophical point of view
must therefore be founded upon some serious realisation of what this
atmosphere is.  In a lecture upon <As You Like It>, Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]]
made a suggestion which is an admirable example of his amazing
ingenuity and of his one most interesting limitation.  In maintaining
that the light sentiment and optimism of the [[Comedy|comedy]] were regarded
by Shakespeare merely as the characteristics of a more or less
cynical pot-boiler, he actually suggested that the title "As You Like It"
was a taunting address to the public in disparagement of their taste
and the dramatist's own work.  If Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] had conceived
of Shakespeare as insisting that Ben Jonson should wear Jaeger
underclothing or join the Blue Ribbon Army, or distribute little
pamphlets for the non-payment of rates, he could scarcely have conceived
anything more violently opposed to the whole spirit of Elizabethan
[[Comedy|comedy]] than the spiteful and priggish modernism of such a taunt.
Shakespeare might make the fastidious and cultivated [[Hamlet]],
moving in his own [[Melancholy|melancholy]] and purely mental world,
warn players against an over-indulgence towards the rabble.
But the very [[Soul|soul]] and meaning of the great comedies is that of an
uproarious communion between the public and the play, a communion
so chaotic that whole scenes of silliness and violence lead us
almost to think that some of the "rowdies" from the pit have climbed
over the footlights.  The title "As you Like It", is, of course,
an expression of utter carelessness, but it is not the bitter
carelessness which Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] fantastically reads into it;
it is the god-like and inexhaustible carelessness of a happy man.
And the simple proof of this is that there are scores of these genially
taunting titles scattered through the whole of Elizabethan [[Comedy|comedy]].
Is "As You Like It" a title demanding a dark and ironic
explanation in a school of [[Comedy|comedy]] which called its plays
"What You Will", "A Mad World, My Masters", "If It Be Not Good,
the Devil Is In It", "The Devil is an Ass", "An Humorous Day's Mirth",
and "A Midsummer [[Night]]'s Dream"?  Every one of these titles is flung
at the head of the public as a drunken lord might fling a purse
at his footman.  Would Mr. [[Shaw]] maintain that "If It Be Not Good,
the Devil Is In It", was the opposite of "As You Like It",
and was a solemn invocation of the [[Supernatural|supernatural]] powers to testify
to the care and perfection of the literary workmanship?
The one explanation is as Elizabethan as the other.

Now in the reason for this modern and pedantic error lies the whole
secret and difficulty of such plays as <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.
The sentiment of such a play, so far as it can be summed up at all,
can be summed up in one sentence.  It is the mysticism of happiness.
That is to say, it is the conception that as man lives upon
a borderland he may find himself in the spiritual or [[Supernatural|supernatural]]
atmosphere, not only through being profoundly sad or meditative,
but by being extravagantly happy.  The [[Soul|soul]] might be rapt
out of the [[Body|body]] in an agony of sorrow, or a trance of ecstasy;
but it might also be rapt out of the [[Body|body]] in a paroxysm of laughter.
Sorrow we know can go beyond itself; so, according to Shakespeare,
can pleasure go beyond itself and become something dangerous and unknown.
And the reason that the logical and destructive modern school,
of which Mr. Bernard [[Shaw]] is an example, does not grasp this
purely exuberant [[Nature|nature]] of the comedies is simply 'that their
logical and destructive attitude have rendered impossible the very
experience of this preternatural exuberance.  We cannot realise
<As You Like It> if we are always considering it as we understand it.
We cannot have <A Midsummer's Night Dream> if our one object in life
is to keep ourselves awake with the black coffee of criticism.
The whole question which is balanced, and balanced nobly and fairly,
in <A Midsummer Night's Dream>, is whether the life of waking,
or the life of the vision, is the real life, the <sine quâ non> of man.
But it is difficult to see what superiority for the purpose
of judging is possessed by people whose [[Pride|pride]] it is not to live
the life of vision at all.  At least it is questionable whether
the Elizabethan did not know more about both worlds than the modern
intellectual it is not altogether improbably that Shakespeare
would not only have had a clearer vision of the fairies, but would
have shot very much straighter at a deer and netted much more money
for his performances than a member of the Stage Society.

Sogno di una [[Night|notte]] di mezza estate

La più grande delle commedie di Shakespeare è anche, da un certo punto di vista, la più grande delle sue opere. Nessuno sosterrebbe che essa occupi tale posizione in fatto di studio psicologico, se per studio psicologico intendiamo lo studio dei singoli personaggi di un dramma. Nessuno sosterrebbe che [[Puck]] sia un personaggio nel senso in cui lo è [[Falstaff]], o che il critico si arresti reverente dinanzi alla psicologia di Fiordipisello. Ma vi è un senso in cui l'opera è forse un trionfo psicologico più grande dello stesso <Hamlet>. Ci si può [[Well|ben]] chiedere se in qualsiasi altra opera letteraria del mondo sia resa con tanta vividezza un'atmosfera sociale e spirituale. In <Hamlet>, per esempio, vi è un'atmosfera, alquanto cupa e persino melodrammatica, ma essa è subordinata al grande personaggio, e moralmente inferiore a lui; l'oscurità non è che uno sfondo per l'astro isolato dell'intelletto. Ma <A Midsummer Night's Dream> è uno studio psicologico non di un uomo solitario, bensì di uno spirito che unisce l'umanità. I sei uomini possono sedere a conversare in una locanda; possono non conoscere i nomi l'uno dell'altro né vedersi in volto prima o dopo, ma la [[Night|notte]], o il vino, o le grandi storie, o qualche ricca e ramificata discussione possono renderli tutti una cosa sola, se non proprio l'uno con l'altro, almeno con quell'invisibile settimo uomo che è l'armonia di tutti loro. Quel settimo uomo è l'[[Hero|eroe]] di <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.

Uno studio dell'opera da un punto di vista letterario o filosofico deve dunque fondarsi su una seria comprensione di che cosa sia questa atmosfera. In una conferenza su <As You Like It>, il signor Bernard [[Shaw]] avanzò un'osservazione che è un mirabile esempio della sua straordinaria arguzia e del suo unico, interessantissimo limite. Sostenendo che il sentimento lieve e l'ottimismo della [[Comedy|commedia]] fossero considerati da Shakespeare come mere caratteristiche di un lavoro alimentare più o meno cinico, egli giunse a suggerire che il titolo «As You Like It» fosse un'apostrofe beffarda rivolta al pubblico, in spregio al suo gusto e all'opera stessa del drammaturgo. Se il signor Bernard [[Shaw]] avesse immaginato uno Shakespeare intento a pretendere che Ben Jonson indossasse biancheria di lana Jaeger, o si arruolasse nell'Esercito del Nastro Azzurro, o distribuisse volantini contro il pagamento delle tasse, difficilmente avrebbe potuto concepire qualcosa di più violentemente contrario all'intero spirito della [[Comedy|commedia]] elisabettiana della modernità astiosa e pedante di una simile beffa. Shakespeare poteva ben far sì che il raffinato e colto [[Hamlet]], muovendosi nel proprio mondo [[Melancholy|malinconico]] e puramente mentale, mettesse in guardia gli attori dall'eccessiva indulgenza verso la plebaglia. Ma l'[[Soul|anima]] stessa e il significato delle grandi commedie è quello di una fragorosa comunione tra il pubblico e la scena, una comunione così caotica che intere scene di follia e violenza ci inducono quasi a credere che qualcuno dei «facinorosi» della platea si sia arrampicato oltre le luci della ribalta. Il titolo «As You Like It» è, naturalmente, espressione di una noncuranza assoluta, ma non della noncuranza amara che il signor Bernard [[Shaw]] vi legge fantasiosamente; è la noncuranza divina e inesauribile di un uomo felice. E la semplice prova di ciò è che vi sono decine di questi titoli genialmente beffardi disseminati per tutta la [[Comedy|commedia]] elisabettiana. È forse «As You Like It» un titolo che reclama una spiegazione oscura e ironica in una scuola di [[Comedy|commedia]] che intitolava i suoi drammi «What You Will», «A Mad World, My Masters», «If It Be Not Good, the Devil Is In It», «The Devil is an Ass», «An Humorous Day's Mirth» e «A Midsummer [[Night]]'s Dream»? Ognuno di questi titoli è scagliato in faccia al pubblico come un lord ubriaco potrebbe scagliare una borsa al suo lacchè. Sosterrebbe forse il signor [[Shaw]] che «If It Be Not Good, the Devil Is In It» fosse l'opposto di «As You Like It», e fosse una solenne invocazione alle potenze [[Supernatural|soprannaturali]] perché testimoniassero la cura e la perfezione della fattura letteraria? L'una spiegazione è tanto elisabettiana quanto l'altra.

Ora, nella ragione di questo errore moderno e pedante risiede tutto il segreto e la difficoltà di opere come <A Midsummer Night's Dream>. Il sentimento di un simile dramma, per quel poco che lo si possa riassumere, si può riassumere in una sola frase. È il misticismo della felicità. Vale a dire, è la concezione secondo cui l'uomo, vivendo su una terra di confine, può ritrovarsi nell'atmosfera spirituale o [[Supernatural|soprannaturale]] non solo per l'essere profondamente triste o meditativo, ma per l'essere smisuratamente felice. L'[[Soul|anima]] può essere rapita fuori dal [[Body|corpo]] in un'agonia di dolore o in una trance di estasi; ma può anche essere rapita fuori dal [[Body|corpo]] in un parossismo di riso. Il dolore, lo sappiamo, può oltrepassare sé stesso; così, secondo Shakespeare, anche il piacere può oltrepassare sé stesso e divenire qualcosa di pericoloso e ignoto. E la ragione per cui la scuola moderna, logica e distruttrice, di cui il signor Bernard [[Shaw]] è un esempio, non coglie questa [[Nature|natura]] puramente esuberante delle commedie è semplicemente che il suo atteggiamento logico e distruttore ha reso impossibile l'esperienza stessa di questa esuberanza prodigiosa. Non possiamo cogliere <As You Like It> se lo consideriamo sempre così come lo comprendiamo. Non possiamo avere <A Midsummer's Night Dream> se il nostro unico scopo nella vita è tenerci desti con il caffè nero della critica. L'intera questione che viene soppesata, e soppesata con nobiltà ed equità, in <A Midsummer Night's Dream>, è se sia la vita della veglia o la vita della visione la vita vera, il <sine quâ non> dell'uomo. Ma è difficile vedere quale superiorità nel giudicare posseggano coloro il cui [[Pride|orgoglio]] è di non vivere affatto la vita della visione. Perlomeno è dubbio che l'elisabettiano non conoscesse entrambi i mondi meglio dell'intellettuale moderno; e non è del tutto improbabile che Shakespeare non solo avesse una visione più chiara delle fate, ma mirasse assai più dritto a un cervo e ricavasse dalle sue rappresentazioni assai più denaro di quanto non farebbe un membro della Stage Society.

In pure poetry and the intoxication of words, Shakespeare never [[Rose|rose]]
higher than he rises in this play.  But in spite of this fact,
the supreme literary merit of <A Midsummer Night's Dream> is
a merit of design.  The amazing symmetry, the amazing artistic
and moral [[Beauty|beauty]] of that design, can be stated very briefly.
The story opens in the sane and common world with the pleasant
seriousness of very young [[lovers]] and very young friends.
Then, as the figures advance into the tangled wood of young troubles
and stolen happiness, a change and bewilderment begins to fall on them.
They lose their way and their wits for they are in the heart
of fairyland.  Their words, their hungers, their very figures
grow more and more dim and fantastic, like [[Dreams|dreams]] within [[Dreams|dreams]],
in the [[Supernatural|supernatural]] mist of [[Puck]].  Then the dream-fumes begin
to clear, and characters and spectators begin to awaken together
to the noise of horns and [[dogs]] and the clean and bracing morning.
[[Theseus]], the incarnation of a happy and generous rationalism,
expounds in hackneyed and superb lines the sane view of such
psychic experiences, pointing out with a reverent and sympathetic
scepticism that all these fairies and spells are themselves
but the emanations, the unconscious masterpieces, of man himself.
The whole company falls back into a splendid human laughter.
There is a rush for banqueting and private theatricals, and over all
these things ripples one of those frivolous and inspired conversations
in which every good saying seems to die in giving birth to another.
If ever the son of a man in his wanderings was at [[Home|home]] and
drinking by the fireside, he is at [[Home|home]] in the [[house]] of [[Theseus]].
All the [[Dreams|dreams]] have been forgotten, as a [[Melancholy|melancholy]] dream remembered
throughout the morning might be forgotten in the human certainty
of any other triumphant evening party; and so the play seems
naturally ended.  It began on the earth and it ends on the earth.
Thus to round off the whole midsummer [[Night|night]]'s dream in an eclipse
of daylight is an effect of genius.  But of this [[Comedy|comedy]],
as I have said, the mark is that genius goes beyond itself;
and one touch is added which makes the play colossal.
[[Theseus]] and his train retire with a crashing finale, full of [[Humour|humour]]
and wisdom and things set right, and [[Silence|silence]] falls on the [[house]].
Then there comes a faint sound of little feet, and for a moment,
as it were, the' elves look into the [[house]], asking which is the reality.
"Suppose we are the realities and they the shadows."  If that ending
were acted properly any modern man would feel shaken to his marrow
if he had to walk [[Home|home]] from the theatre through a country lane.

It is a trite matter, of course, though in a general criticism
a more or less indispensable one to comment upon another point
of artistic perfection, the extraordinarily human and accurate
manner in which the play catches the atmosphere of [[A Dream|a dream]].
The chase and tangle and frustration of the incidents and
personalities are [[Well|well]] known to every one who has dreamt of
perpetually falling over precipices or perpetually missing trains.
While following out clearly and legally the necessary narrative
of the drama, the author contrives to include every one of the main
peculiarities of the exasperating dream.  Here is the pursuit
of the man we cannot catch, the flight from the man we cannot see;
here is the perpetual returning to the same place, here is the crazy
alteration in the very objects of our [[Desire|desire]], the substitution
of one face for another face, the putting of the wrong souls
in the wrong bodies, the fantastic disloyalties of the [[Night|night]],
all this is as obvious as it is important.  It is perhaps somewhat
more -worth remarking that there is about this confusion of [[Comedy|comedy]]
yet another essential characteristic of [[Dreams|dreams]].  [[A Dream|A dream]] can commonly
be described as possessing an utter discordance of incident combined
with a curious [[unity]] of mood; everything changes but the dreamer.
It may begin with anything and end with anything, but if the dreamer
is sad at the end he will be sad as if by prescience at the beginning;
if he is cheerful at the beginning he will be cheerful if the [[Stars|stars]] fall.
<A Midsummer Night's Dream> has in a most singular degree effected
this difficult, this almost desperate subtlety.  The events in the
wandering wood are in themselves, and regarded as in broad daylight,
not merely [[Melancholy|melancholy]] but bitterly cruel and ignominious.
But yet by the spreading of an atmosphere as [[Magic|magic]] as the fog of [[Puck]],

Shakespeare contrives to make the whole matter mysteriously hilarious
while it is palpably tragic, and mysteriously charitable, while it is
in itself cynical.  He contrives somehow to rob [[Tragedy|tragedy]] and treachery
of their full sharpness, just as a toothache or a deadly danger from
a tiger, or a precipice, is robbed of its sharpness in a pleasant dream.
The creation of a brooding sentiment like this, a sentiment not merely
independent of but actually opposed to the events, is a much greater
triumph of art than the creation of the character of [[Othello]].

It is difficult to approach critically so great a figure as that of
[[Bottom]] the Weaver.  He is greater and more mysterious than [[Hamlet]],
because the interest of such men as [[Bottom]] consists of a rich
subconsciousness, and that of [[Hamlet]] in the comparatively superficial
matter of a rich consciousness.  And it is especially difficult in
the present age which has become hag-ridden with the mere intellect.
We are the victims of a curious confusion whereby being great is
supposed to have something to do with being clever, as if there
were the smallest reason to suppose that [[Achilles]] was clever,
as if there were not on the contrary a great deal of internal
evidence to indicate that he was next door to a [[Fool|fool]].  [[Greatness]] is
a certain indescribable but perfectly familiar and palpable quality
of size in the personality, of steadfastness, of strong flavour,
of easy and natural [[Self|self]]-expression. Such a man is as firm as a tree
and as unique as a rhinoceros, and he might quite easily be as
stupid as either of them.  Fully as much as the great poet towers
above the small poet the great [[Fool|fool]] towers above the small [[Fool|fool]].
We have all of us known rustics like [[Bottom]] the Weaver, men whose
faces would be blank with idiocy if we tried for -ten days to explain
the meaning of the National Debt, but who are yet great men,
akin to Sigurd and Hercules, heroes of the morning of the earth,
because their words were their own words, their memories their
own memories, and their [[Vanity|vanity]] as large and simple as a great [[Hill|hill]].
We have all of us known friends in our own circle, men whom the
intellectuals might justly describe as brainless, but whose presence
in a room was like a [[Fire|fire]] roaring in the grate changing everything,
lights and shadows and the air, whose entrances and exits were in some
strange fashion events, whose point of view once expressed haunts
and persuades the mind and almost intimidates it, whose manifest
absurdity clings to the fancy like the [[Beauty|beauty]] of first-[[Love|love]],
and whose follies are recounted like the legends of a paladin.
These ate great men, there are millions of them in the world,
though very few perhaps in the [[house|House]] of Commons.  It is not in
the cold halls of cleverness where celebrities seem to be important
that we should look for the great.  An intellectual salon is merely
a training-ground for one faculty, and is akin to a fencing class
or a rifle corps.  It is in our own homes and environments,
from Croydon to St. John's Wood, in old nurses, and gentlemen
with hobbies, and talkative spinisters and vast incomparable butlers,
that we may feel the presence of that [[Blood|blood]] of the gods.
And this creature so hard to describe, so easy to remember,
the august and memorable [[Fool|fool]], has never been so sumptuously painted
as in the [[Bottom]] of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.

Nella pura poesia e nell'ebbrezza delle parole, Shakespeare non [[Rose|si levò]] mai più in alto di quanto si elevi in questo dramma. Ma, a dispetto di ciò, il supremo merito letterario di <A Midsummer Night's Dream> è un merito di disegno. La stupefacente simmetria, la stupefacente [[Beauty|bellezza]] artistica e morale di quel disegno può essere enunciata in poche parole. La vicenda si apre nel mondo sano e comune con la piacevole serietà di giovanissimi [[lovers|innamorati]] e giovanissimi amici. Poi, man mano che le figure avanzano nell'intricato bosco dei giovani affanni e delle felicità rubate, un mutamento e uno smarrimento cominciano a scendere su di loro. Perdono la via e il senno, poiché sono nel cuore del regno delle fate. Le loro parole, le loro brame, le loro stesse figure si fanno sempre più fioche e fantastiche, come [[Dreams|sogni]] dentro [[Dreams|sogni]], nella [[Supernatural|soprannaturale]] nebbia di [[Puck]]. Poi i fumi del sogno cominciano a diradarsi, e personaggi e spettatori cominciano a destarsi insieme al suono dei corni e dei [[dogs|cani]] e al mattino terso e corroborante. [[Theseus]], l'incarnazione di un razionalismo felice e generoso, espone in versi triti e superbi la sana veduta di simili esperienze psichiche, additando con uno scetticismo reverente e comprensivo che tutte queste fate e questi incantesimi non sono a loro volta che le emanazioni, i capolavori inconsci, dell'uomo stesso. L'intera compagnia ricade in una splendida risata umana. C'è una corsa al banchetto e alle recite private, e su tutte queste cose si increspa una di quelle conversazioni frivole e ispirate in cui ogni buon motto sembra morire nel dar vita a un altro. Se mai il figlio dell'uomo, nei suoi errabondaggi, fu a [[Home|casa]] e in ozio bevve accanto al focolare, egli è a [[Home|casa]] nella [[house|dimora]] di [[Theseus]]. Tutti i [[Dreams|sogni]] sono stati dimenticati, come un sogno [[Melancholy|malinconico]] ricordato per tutto il mattino potrebbe essere dimenticato nella certezza umana di qualsiasi altra trionfante festa serale; e così il dramma pare naturalmente concluso. Cominciò sulla terra e sulla terra finisce. Chiudere così l'intero sogno di una [[Night|notte]] di mezza estate in un'eclissi di luce diurna è un effetto di genio. Ma di questa [[Comedy|commedia]], come ho detto, il segno è che il genio va oltre se stesso; e si aggiunge un tocco che rende il dramma colossale. [[Theseus]] e il suo seguito si ritirano con un finale fragoroso, colmo di [[Humour|arguzia]] e saggezza e cose rimesse a posto, e il [[Silence|silenzio]] cala sulla [[house|casa]]. Poi giunge un debole suono di piccoli piedi, e per un istante, per così dire, gli elfi si affacciano nella [[house|casa]], chiedendosi quale sia la realtà. «Supponiamo che i reali siamo noi e le ombre loro.» Se quel finale fosse recitato a dovere, ogni uomo moderno si sentirebbe scosso fino al midollo se dovesse tornare a [[Home|casa]] dal teatro attraverso una viuzza di campagna.

È cosa trita, certo, benché in una critica generale più o meno indispensabile, soffermarsi su un altro punto di perfezione artistica: il modo straordinariamente umano ed esatto in cui il dramma coglie l'atmosfera di [[A Dream|un sogno]]. L'inseguimento e il groviglio e la frustrazione degli episodi e delle personalità sono [[Well|ben]] noti a chiunque abbia sognato di cadere perpetuamente da precipizi o di perdere perpetuamente treni. Pur svolgendo con chiarezza e legalità la necessaria narrazione del dramma, l'autore riesce a includere ognuna delle principali peculiarità dell'esasperante sogno. Ecco l'inseguimento dell'uomo che non possiamo afferrare, la fuga dall'uomo che non possiamo vedere; ecco il perpetuo ritornare allo stesso luogo, ecco la folle alterazione degli oggetti stessi del nostro [[Desire|desiderio]], la sostituzione di un volto con un altro volto, il collocamento delle anime sbagliate nei corpi sbagliati, le fantastiche slealtà della [[Night|notte]]: tutto ciò è tanto ovvio quanto importante. Forse val la pena di notare ancor più che intorno a questa confusione della [[Comedy|commedia]] vi è un'altra caratteristica essenziale dei [[Dreams|sogni]]. [[A Dream|Un sogno]] può comunemente descriversi come dotato di un'assoluta discordanza di episodi congiunta a una curiosa [[unity|unità]] d'umore; tutto muta tranne il sognatore. Può cominciare con qualsiasi cosa e finire con qualsiasi cosa, ma se il sognatore è triste alla fine, sarà triste come per prescienza fin dal principio; se è allegro al principio, sarà allegro anche se le [[Stars|stelle]] cadono. <A Midsummer Night's Dream> ha realizzato in grado singolarissimo questa difficile, questa quasi disperata sottigliezza. Gli eventi nel bosco errante sono in se stessi, e considerati alla piena luce del giorno, non solo [[Melancholy|malinconici]] ma amaramente crudeli e ignominiosi. Eppure, stendendovi sopra un'atmosfera [[Magic|magica]] come la nebbia di [[Puck]],

Shakespeare riesce a rendere l'intera faccenda misteriosamente ilare pur essendo palpabilmente tragica, e misteriosamente caritatevole pur essendo in sé cinica. Riesce in qualche modo a spogliare la [[Tragedy|tragedia]] e il tradimento della loro piena acutezza, proprio come un mal di denti o un pericolo mortale — una tigre, o un precipizio — è spogliato della sua acutezza in un sogno piacevole. La creazione di un sentimento aleggiante come questo, un sentimento non solo indipendente dagli eventi ma anzi ad essi opposto, è un trionfo dell'arte assai più grande della creazione del carattere di [[Othello]].

È difficile accostarsi criticamente a una figura tanto grande quanto quella di [[Bottom]] il Tessitore. Egli è più grande e più misterioso di [[Hamlet]], perché l'interesse di uomini come [[Bottom]] consiste in una ricca subcoscienza, e quello di [[Hamlet]] nella materia comparativamente superficiale di una ricca coscienza. E ciò è particolarmente difficile nell'età presente, che è divenuta ossessionata dal mero intelletto. Siamo vittime di una curiosa confusione per cui l'esser grandi si suppone abbia qualcosa a che fare con l'esser intelligenti, come se vi fosse la minima ragione di supporre che [[Achilles]] fosse intelligente, come se non vi fosse anzi una gran quantità di prove interne a indicare che era a un passo dall'essere uno [[Fool|sciocco]]. La [[Greatness|grandezza]] è una certa indescrivibile ma perfettamente familiare e palpabile qualità di ampiezza nella personalità, di fermezza, di sapore forte, di facile e naturale espressione di [[Self|sé]]. Un uomo simile è saldo come un albero e unico come un rinoceronte, e potrebbe benissimo essere stupido quanto l'uno o l'altro. Tanto quanto il grande poeta torreggia sul piccolo poeta, il grande [[Fool|sciocco]] torreggia sul piccolo [[Fool|sciocco]]. Tutti noi abbiamo conosciuto rustici come [[Bottom]] il Tessitore, uomini il cui volto sarebbe vacuo d'idiozia se cercassimo per dieci giorni di spiegar loro il significato del Debito Nazionale, ma che sono nondimeno grandi uomini, affini a Sigurd e a Ercole, eroi del mattino della terra, perché le loro parole erano parole loro, i loro ricordi ricordi loro, e la loro [[Vanity|vanità]] ampia e semplice come una grande [[Hill|collina]]. Tutti noi abbiamo conosciuto amici nella nostra cerchia, uomini che gli intellettuali potrebbero giustamente dire senza cervello, ma la cui presenza in una stanza era come un [[Fire|fuoco]] ruggente nel focolare che muta ogni cosa, luci e ombre e l'aria, le cui entrate e uscite erano in qualche strana foggia degli eventi, il cui punto di vista, una volta espresso, ossessiona e persuade la mente e quasi la intimidisce, la cui manifesta assurdità s'aggrappa alla fantasia come la [[Beauty|bellezza]] del primo [[Love|amore]], e le cui follie si narrano come le leggende di un paladino. Questi sono grandi uomini, ce ne sono milioni al mondo, benché forse ben pochi nella [[house|Camera]] dei Comuni. Non è nelle fredde sale dell'intelligenza, dove le celebrità paiono importanti, che dovremmo cercare i grandi. Un salotto intellettuale è soltanto un campo d'addestramento per una sola facoltà, ed è affine a una classe di scherma o a un corpo di tiratori. È nelle nostre stesse case e nei nostri ambienti, da Croydon a St. John's Wood, in vecchie balie e signori con hobby, e zitelle chiacchierone e sterminati impareggiabili maggiordomi, che possiamo sentire la presenza di quel [[Blood|sangue]] degli dèi. E questa creatura tanto difficile da descrivere, tanto facile da ricordare, l'augusto e memorabile [[Fool|sciocco]], non è mai stata dipinta tanto sontuosamente quanto nel [[Bottom]] di <A Midsummer Night's Dream>.



<span class="atom-split" data-atom="part--part_03" data-title="midsummer nights dream (part 3)" data-chapter="part 03" data-kind="excerpt"></span>


[[Bottom]] has the supreme mark of this real [[Greatness|greatness]] in that like
the true saint or the true [[Hero|hero]] he only differs from humanity
in being as it were more human than humanity.  It is not true,
as the idle materialists of today suggest, that compared to the majority
of men the [[Hero|hero]] appears cold and dehumanised; it is the majority
who appear cold and dehumanised in the presence of [[Greatness|greatness]].
[[Bottom]], like [[Don Quixote]] and Uncle Toby and Mr. [[Richard]] Swiveller
and the rest of the Titans, has a huge and unfathomable weakness,
his silliness is on a great scale, and when he blows his own trumpet
it is like the trumpet of the [[Resurrection]].  The other rustics in
the play accept his leadership not merely naturally but exuberantly;
they have to the full that primary and savage unselfishness,
that uproarious abnegation which makes simple men take pleasure
in falling short of a [[Hero|hero]], that unquestionable element of basic
human [[Nature|nature]] which has never been expressed, outside this play,
so perfectly as in the incomparable chapter at the beginning
of <Evan Harrington> in which the praises of The Great Mel are
sung with a [[Lyric|lyric]] energy by the tradesmen whom he has cheated.
Twopenny sceptics write of the egoism of primal human [[Nature|nature]];
it is reserved for great men like Shakespeare and Meredith to detect
and make vivid this rude and subconscious unselfishness which is
older than [[Self|self]].  They [[Alone|alone]] with their insatiable tolerance can
perceive all the spiritual devotion in the [[Soul|soul]] of a snob.

And it is this natural play between the rich simplicity of [[Bottom]]
and the simple simplicity of his comrades which constitutes
the unapproachable excellence of the farcical scenes in this play.
[[Bottom]]'s sensibility to literature is perfectly fiery and genuine,
a great deal more genuine than that of a great many cultivated
critics of literature - "the raging rocks, and shivering
shocks shall break the locks of [[Prison|prison]] gates, and Phibbus'
car shall shine from far, and make and mar the foolish fates",
is exceedingly good poetical diction with a real throb and swell
in it, and if it is slightly and almost imperceptibly deficient
in the matter of sense, it is certainly every bit as sensible
as a good many other rhetorical speeches in Shakespeare put into
the mouths of kings and [[lovers]] and even the [[Spirits of the Dead|spirits of the dead]].
If [[Bottom]] liked cant for its own sake the fact only constitutes
another point of sympathy between him and his literary creator.
But the style of the thing, though deliberately bombastic and ludicrous,
is quite literary, the alliteration falls like wave upon wave,
and the whole verse, like a billow mounts higher and higher
before it crashes.  There is nothing mean about this folly;
nor is there in the whole realm of literature a figure so free
from vulgarity.  The man vitally base and foolish sings
"The Honeysuckle and the Bee"; he does not rant about "raging rocks"
and "the car of Phibbus".  Dickens, who more perhaps than any
modern man had the mental hospitality and the thoughtless wisdom
of Shakespeare, perceived and expressed admirably the same [[Truth|truth]].
He perceived, that is to say, that quite indefensible idiots
have very often a real sense of, and enthusiasm for letters.
Mr. Micawber loved eloquence and poetry with his whole immortal [[Soul|soul]];
words and visionary pictures kept him alive in the absence of food
and money, as they might have kept a saint fasting in a [[Desert|desert]].
[[Dick Swiveller]] did not make his inimitable quotations from Moore and
Byron merely as flippant digressions.  He made them because he loved
a great school of poetry.  The sincere [[Love|love]] of books has nothing to do
with cleverness or stupidity any more than any other sincere [[Love|love]].
It is a quality of character, a freshness, a power of pleasure,
a power of [[Faith|faith]].  A silly person may delight in reading
masterpieces just as a silly person may delight in picking [[Flowers|flowers]].
A [[Fool|fool]] may be in [[Love|love]] with a poet as he may be in [[Love|love]] with a woman.
And the triumph of [[Bottom]] is that he loves rhetoric and his own
taste in the arts, and this is all that can be achieved by [[Theseus]],
or for the matter of that by Cosimo di Medici.  It is worth remarking
as an extremely fine touch in the picture of [[Bottom]] that his literary
taste is almost everywhere concerned with sound rather than sense.
He begins the rehearsal with a boisterous readiness, "Thisby,
the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete."  "Odours, odours," says Quince,
in remonstrance, and the word is accepted in accordance with
the cold and heavy rules which require an element of meaning in a
poetical passage.  But "Thisby, the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete",
[[Bottom]]'s version, is an immeasurably finer and more resonant line.
The "i" which he inserts is an inspiration of metricism.

There is another aspect of this great play which ought to be
kept familiarly in the mind.  Extravagant as is the masquerade
of the story, it is a very perfect aesthetic harmony down to such
<coup-de-maître> as the name of [[Bottom]], or the flower called
[[Love]] in Idleness.  In the whole matter it may be said that there
is one accidental discord; that is in the name of [[Theseus]],
and the whole [[City|city]] of Athens in which the events take place.
Shakespeare's description of Athens in <A Midsummer Night's Dream>
is the best description of England that he or any one else ever wrote.
[[Theseus]] is quite obviously only an English squire, fond of hunting,
kindly to his tenants, hospitable with a certain flamboyant [[Vanity|vanity]].
The mechanics are English mechanics, talking to each other with
the queer formality of the poor.  Above all, the fairies are English;
to compare them with the beautiful patrician spirits of Irish legend,
for instance, is suddenly to discover that we have, after all,
a folk-lore and a mythology, or had it at least in Shakespeare's day.
Robin Goodfellow, upsetting the old women's ale, or pulling
the stool from under them, has nothing of the poignant
Celtic [[Beauty|beauty]]; his is the [[Horse|horse]]-play of the invisible world.
Perhaps it is some debased inheritance of English life which makes
American [[Ghosts|ghosts]] so fond of quite undignified practical jokes.
But this [[Union|union]] of mystery with farce is a note of the medieval English.
The play is the last glimpse of Merrie England, that distant
but shining and quite indubitable country.  It would be difficult
indeed to define wherein lay the peculiar [[Truth|truth]] of the phrase
"merrie England", though some conception of it is quite necessary
to the comprehension of <A Midsummer Night's Dream>. In some
cases at least, it may be said to lie in this, that the English
of the Middle Ages and the [[Renaissance]], unlike the England
of today, could conceive of the idea of a merry supernaturalism.
Amid all the great work of [[Puritanism]] the damning indictment
of it consists in one fact, that there was one only of the fables
of Christendom that it retained and renewed, and that was
the belief in witchcraft.  It cast away the generous and wholesome
superstition, it approved only of the morbid and the dangerous.
In their treatment of the great national fairy-tale of good
and evil, the Puritans killed St. George but carefully preserved
the Dragon, And this seventeenth-century tradition of dealing
with the psychic life still lies like a great [[Shadow|shadow]] over England
and [[America]], so that if we glance at a [[Novel|novel]] about occultism we
may be perfectly certain that it deals with sad or evil destiny.
Whatever else we expect we certainly should never expect to find
in it spirits such as those in <Aylwin> as inspirers of a tale of
tomfoolery like the <Wrong Box> or <The Londoners>. That impossibility
is the disappearance of "merrie England" and Robin Goodfellow.
It was a land to us incredible, the land of a jolly occultism where
the peasant cracked jokes with his patron saint, and only cursed
the fairies good-humouredly, as he might curse a lazy servant.
Shakespeare is English in everything, above all in his weaknesses.
just as [[London]], one of the greatest cities in the world, shows more
slums and hides more beauties than any other, so Shakespeare [[Alone|alone]]
among the four giants of poetry is a careless writer, and lets us
come upon his splendours by accident, as we come upon an old [[City]]
church in the twist of a [[City|city]] street.  He is English in nothing
so much as in that noble cosmopolitan unconsciousness which makes him
look eastward with the eyes of a [[Child|child]] towards Athens or [[Verona]].
He loved to talk of the glory of foreign lands, but he talked
of them with the tongue and unquenchable spirit of England.
It is too much the custom of a later patriotism to reverse this
method and talk of England from morning till [[Night|night]], but to talk
of her in a manner totally un-English. Casualness, incongruities,
and a certain fine absence of mind are in the temper of England;
the unconscious man with the ass's head is no bad type of the people.
Materialistic philosophers and mechanical politicians have certainly
succeeded in some cases in giving him a greater [[unity]].  The only
question is, to which animal has he been thus successfully conformed?

[[Bottom]] possiede il segno supremo di questa vera [[Greatness|grandezza]] in ciò che, come il vero santo o il vero [[Hero|eroe]], egli differisce dall'umanità solo per essere, per così dire, più umano dell'umanità stessa. Non è vero, come suggeriscono gli oziosi materialisti d'oggi, che, paragonato alla maggioranza degli uomini, l'[[Hero|eroe]] appaia freddo e disumanizzato; è la maggioranza che appare fredda e disumanizzata al cospetto della [[Greatness|grandezza]]. [[Bottom]], come [[Don Quixote]] e lo Zio Toby e il signor [[Richard]] Swiveller e il resto dei Titani, ha una debolezza enorme e insondabile, la sua scempiaggine è su vasta scala, e quando suona la propria tromba è come la tromba della [[Resurrection|Resurrezione]]. Gli altri rustici del dramma accettano la sua guida non soltanto con naturalezza ma con esuberanza; posseggono in pieno quel primario e selvaggio disinteresse, quella clamorosa abnegazione che fa sì che gli uomini semplici provino piacere nel restare al di sotto di un [[Hero|eroe]], quell'indubitabile elemento della [[Nature|natura]] umana fondamentale che non è mai stato espresso, fuori di questo dramma, così perfettamente come nell'impareggiabile capitolo all'inizio di <Evan Harrington>, in cui le lodi del Grande Mel sono cantate con energia [[Lyric|lirica]] dai bottegai che egli ha truffato. Scettici da due soldi scrivono dell'egoismo della [[Nature|natura]] umana primigenia; è riservato a grandi uomini come Shakespeare e Meredith il compito di scoprire e rendere vivo questo rude e subcosciente disinteresse che è più antico del [[Self|sé]]. Essi [[Alone|soli]], con la loro insaziabile tolleranza, sanno percepire tutta la devozione spirituale nell'[[Soul|anima]] di uno snob.

Ed è questo naturale gioco tra la ricca semplicità di [[Bottom]] e la semplice semplicità dei suoi compagni a costituire l'inavvicinabile eccellenza delle scene farsesche di questo dramma. La sensibilità di [[Bottom]] alla letteratura è perfettamente ardente e genuina, assai più genuina di quella di moltissimi critici colti della letteratura — «the raging rocks, and shivering shocks shall break the locks of [[Prison|prison]] gates, and Phibbus' car shall shine from far, and make and mar the foolish fates» è una dizione poetica straordinariamente buona, con un vero palpito e un vero rigonfiarsi, e se è lievemente e quasi impercettibilmente deficiente in fatto di senso, è di certo altrettanto sensata di parecchi altri discorsi retorici che in Shakespeare sono messi in bocca a re e [[lovers|innamorati]] e persino agli [[Spirits of the Dead|spiriti dei morti]]. Se [[Bottom]] amava la cantilena retorica per se stessa, il fatto non fa che costituire un altro punto di simpatia tra lui e il suo creatore letterario. Ma lo stile della cosa, per quanto deliberatamente ampolloso e ridicolo, è del tutto letterario, l'allitterazione cade come onda su onda, e l'intero verso, come un cavallone, monta sempre più alto prima di infrangersi. Non v'è nulla di meschino in questa follia; né v'è in tutto il regno della letteratura una figura tanto libera da volgarità. L'uomo vitalmente vile e sciocco canta «The Honeysuckle and the Bee»; non declama di «raging rocks» e del «car of Phibbus». Dickens, che più forse di ogni altro uomo moderno ebbe l'ospitalità mentale e l'irriflessa saggezza di Shakespeare, percepì ed espresse mirabilmente la stessa [[Truth|verità]]. Percepì, vale a dire, che idioti del tutto indifendibili hanno molto spesso un reale senso, e un reale entusiasmo, per le lettere. Il signor Micawber amava l'eloquenza e la poesia con tutta la sua immortale [[Soul|anima]]; parole e immagini visionarie lo tenevano in vita in assenza di cibo e denaro, come avrebbero potuto tenere in vita un santo digiunante in un [[Desert|deserto]]. [[Dick Swiveller]] non traeva le sue inimitabili citazioni da Moore e Byron come semplici frivole digressioni. Le faceva perché amava una grande scuola di poesia. Il sincero [[Love|amore]] dei libri non ha nulla a che fare con l'intelligenza o la stupidità, più di qualsiasi altro sincero [[Love|amore]]. È una qualità del carattere, una freschezza, una potenza di piacere, una potenza di [[Faith|fede]]. Una persona sciocca può dilettarsi a leggere capolavori proprio come una persona sciocca può dilettarsi a cogliere [[Flowers|fiori]]. Uno [[Fool|sciocco]] può essere innamorato di un poeta come può essere innamorato di una donna. E il trionfo di [[Bottom]] è che egli ama la retorica e il proprio gusto nelle arti, e questo è tutto ciò che possono conseguire [[Theseus]], o se è per questo Cosimo de' Medici. Val la pena di notare, come tocco estremamente fine nel ritratto di [[Bottom]], che il suo gusto letterario è quasi ovunque interessato al suono piuttosto che al senso. Egli comincia la prova con una chiassosa prontezza: «Thisby, the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete». «Odours, odours», dice Quince, in tono di rimprovero, e la parola è accettata in conformità alle fredde e pesanti regole che esigono un elemento di significato in un passo poetico. Ma «Thisby, the [[Flowers|flowers]] of odious savours sweete», la versione di [[Bottom]], è un verso incommensurabilmente più fine e più risonante. La «i» che egli inserisce è un'ispirazione di metrica.

C'è un altro aspetto di questo grande dramma che dovrebbe tenersi familiarmente a mente. Per quanto stravagante sia la mascherata della vicenda, essa è un'armonia estetica perfettissima fin nei minimi <coup-de-maître>, come il nome di [[Bottom]], o il fiore chiamato [[Love|Amore]] in Idleness. In tutta la faccenda si può dire che vi sia una sola discordanza accidentale; ed è nel nome di [[Theseus]], e nell'intera [[City|città]] di Atene in cui gli eventi hanno luogo. La descrizione che Shakespeare fa di Atene in <A Midsummer Night's Dream> è la migliore descrizione dell'Inghilterra che egli o chiunque altro abbia mai scritto. [[Theseus]] è del tutto evidentemente soltanto uno squire inglese, appassionato di caccia, benevolo verso i suoi fittavoli, ospitale con una certa fiammeggiante [[Vanity|vanità]]. Gli artigiani sono artigiani inglesi, che si parlano con la bizzarra formalità dei poveri. Soprattutto, le fate sono inglesi; paragonarle, per esempio, ai bellissimi spiriti patrizi della leggenda irlandese equivale a scoprire d'improvviso che possediamo, dopotutto, un folclore e una mitologia, o almeno li possedevamo ai tempi di Shakespeare. Robin Goodfellow, che rovescia la birra delle vecchiette o tira via lo sgabello da sotto di loro, non ha nulla della struggente [[Beauty|bellezza]] celtica; il suo è il gioco da [[Horse|cavalli]] del mondo invisibile. Forse è qualche degenerata eredità della vita inglese a rendere i [[Ghosts|fantasmi]] americani tanto ghiotti di scherzi pratici del tutto indegni. Ma questa [[Union|unione]] del mistero con la farsa è una nota degli inglesi medievali. Il dramma è l'ultimo barlume della Merrie England, quel paese lontano ma splendente e affatto indubitabile. Sarebbe invero difficile definire in che cosa risiedesse la peculiare [[Truth|verità]] della frase «merrie England», benché una qualche concezione di essa sia del tutto necessaria alla comprensione di <A Midsummer Night's Dream>. In alcuni casi almeno, si può dire che risieda in questo: che gli inglesi del Medioevo e del [[Renaissance|Rinascimento]], diversamente dall'Inghilterra d'oggi, sapevano concepire l'idea di un allegro soprannaturalismo. In mezzo a tutta la grande opera del [[Puritanism|Puritanesimo]], l'atto d'accusa che lo condanna consiste in un fatto: che vi fu una sola, fra le favole della cristianità, che esso conservò e rinnovò, e fu la credenza nella stregoneria. Gettò via la superstizione generosa e salubre, approvò soltanto ciò che era morboso e pericoloso. Nel loro trattamento della grande fiaba nazionale del bene e del male, i Puritani uccisero San Giorgio ma conservarono accuratamente il Drago. E questa tradizione secentesca di trattare la vita psichica giace ancora come una grande [[Shadow|ombra]] sull'Inghilterra e sull'[[America]], sicché, se gettiamo uno sguardo a un [[Novel|romanzo]] sull'occultismo, possiamo esser perfettamente certi che tratti di un destino triste o malvagio. Qualunque altra cosa ci aspettiamo, di certo non dovremmo mai aspettarci di trovarvi spiriti come quelli di <Aylwin> quali ispiratori di un racconto di buffonaggini come <Wrong Box> o <The Londoners>. Quell'impossibilità è la scomparsa della «merrie England» e di Robin Goodfellow. Era una terra per noi incredibile, la terra di un gaio occultismo dove il contadino scambiava battute col suo santo patrono, e malediceva le fate solo bonariamente, come avrebbe maledetto un servo pigro. Shakespeare è inglese in ogni cosa, soprattutto nelle sue debolezze. Proprio come [[London|Londra]], una delle più grandi città del mondo, mostra più tuguri e nasconde più bellezze di ogni altra, così Shakespeare [[Alone|solo]] fra i quattro giganti della poesia è uno scrittore trascurato, e ci lascia imbatterci nei suoi splendori per caso, come ci si imbatte in una vecchia chiesa della [[City]] nella svolta di una strada [[City|cittadina]]. Egli è inglese in nulla tanto quanto in quella nobile inconsapevolezza cosmopolita che lo fa guardare a oriente con gli occhi di un [[Child|fanciullo]] verso Atene o [[Verona]]. Amava parlare della gloria delle terre straniere, ma ne parlava con la lingua e con lo spirito inestinguibile dell'Inghilterra. È troppo il costume di un patriottismo più tardo rovesciare questo metodo e parlare dell'Inghilterra dal mattino alla [[Night|sera]], ma parlarne in maniera totalmente non-inglese. La noncuranza, le incongruenze, e una certa fine distrazione della mente sono nel temperamento dell'Inghilterra; l'uomo inconsapevole con la testa d'asino non è un cattivo tipo di quel popolo. Filosofi materialisti e politici meccanici sono certamente riusciti, in alcuni casi, a dargli una maggiore [[unity|unità]]. L'unica questione è: a quale animale è stato così efficacemente conformato?
