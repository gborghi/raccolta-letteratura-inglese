The Meaning of the Crusade

By G.K. Chesterton 

From "The New Jerusalem" 1920

There are three examples of Western work on the great eastern slope
of the Mount of Olives; and they form a sort of triangle illustrating
the [[concepts/truth|truth]] about the different influences of the West on the East.
At the foot of the [[motifs/hill|hill]] is the [[motifs/garden|garden]] kept by the Franciscans
on the alleged site of Gethsemane, and containing the hoary olive
that is supposed to be the terrible tree of the agony of [[characters/christ|Christ]].
Given the great age and slow growth of the olives, the tradition
is not so unreasonable as some may suppose.  But whether or not it
is historically right, it is not artistically wrong.  The instinct,
if it was only an instinct, that made men fix upon this strange
growth of grey and twisted wood, was a true imaginative instinct.
One of the strange qualities of this strange Southern tree is
its almost startling hardness; accidentally to strike the branch
of an olive is like striking rock.  With its stony surface,
stunted stature, and strange holes and hollows, it is often more
like a grotto than a tree.  Hence it does not seem so unnatural
that it should be treated as a holy grotto; or that this strange
vegetation should claim to stand for ever like a sculptured monument.
Even the shimmering or shivering silver foliage of the living
olive might [[motifs/well|well]] have a legend like that of the aspen; as if it
had grown grey with [[concepts/fear|fear]] from the apocalyptic [[concepts/paradox|paradox]] of a divine
vision of [[motifs/death|death]].  A [[archetypes/child|child]] from one of the villages said to me,
in broken English, that it was the place where God said his prayers.
I for one could not ask for a finer or more defiant statement
of all that separates the Christian from the Moslem or the Jew;
<credo quia impossibile>.

Around this terrible spot the Franciscans have done something which will
strike many good and thoughtful people as quite fantastically inadequate;
and which strikes me as fantastically but precisely right.
They have laid out the [[motifs/garden|garden]] simply as a [[motifs/garden|garden]], in a way
that is completely natural because it is completely artificial.
They have made flower-beds in the shape of [[motifs/stars|stars]] and moons,
and coloured them with [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] like those in the backyard of a cottage.
The combination of these bright patterns in the sunshine
with the awful [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] in the centre is certainly an incongruity
in the sense of a contrast.  But it is a poetical contrast,
like that of [[motifs/birds|birds]] building in a temple or [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] growing on a tomb.
The best way of suggesting what I for one feel about it would
be something like this; suppose we imagine a company of children,
such as those whom [[characters/christ|Christ]] blessed in Jerusalem, afterwards put
permanently in charge of a field full of his sorrow; it is probable that,
if they could do anything with it, they would do something like this.
They might cut it up into quaint shapes and dot it with red
daisies or yellow marigolds.  I really do not know that there
is anything better that grown up people could do, since anything
that the greatest of them could do must be, must look quite as small.
"Shall I, the gnat that dances in Thy ray, dare to be reverent?"
The Franciscans have not dared to be reverent; they have only dared
to be cheerful.  It may be too awful an adventure of the [[concepts/imagination|imagination]]
to imagine [[characters/christ|Christ]] in that [[motifs/garden|garden]].  But there is not the smallest
difficulty about imagining St. Francis there; and that is something
to say of an institution which is eight hundred years old.

Immediately above this little [[motifs/garden|garden]], overshadowing and almost
overhanging it, is a gorgeous gilded building with golden domes
and minarets glittering in the sun, and filling a splendid situation
with almost shameless splendour; the Russian church built over
the upper part of the [[motifs/garden|garden]], belonging to the Orthodox-Greeks.
Here again many Western travellers will be troubled; and will think
that golden building much too like a fairy palace in a pantomime.
But here again I shall differ from them, though perhaps less strongly.
It may be that the pleasure is childish rather than childlike;
but I can imagine a [[archetypes/child|child]] clapping his hands at the mere sight
of those great domes like bubbles of gold against the blue sky.
It is a little like Aladdin's Palace, but it has a place in art
as Aladdin has a place in literature; especially since it is
oriental literature.  Those wise missionaries in China who were not
afraid to depict the Twelve Apostles in the costume of Chinamen
might have built such a church in a land of glittering mosques.
And as it is said that the Russian has in him something of the [[archetypes/child|child]]
and something of the oriental, such a style may be quite sincere,
and have even a certain simplicity in its splendour.
It is genuine of its kind; it was built for those who like it;
and those who do not like it can look at something else.  This sort
of thing may be called tawdry, but it is not what I call meretricious.
What I call really meretricious can be found yet higher on the [[motifs/hill|hill]];
towering to the sky and dominating all the valleys.

The [[concepts/nature|nature]] of the difference, I think, is worth noting.
The German Hospice, which served as a sort of palace for the
German Emperor, is a very big building with a very high tower,
planned I believe with great efficiency, solidity and comfort,
and fitted with a thousand things that mark its modernity
compared with the things around, with the quaint [[motifs/garden|garden]]
of the Franciscans or the fantastic temple of the Russians.
It is what I can only describe as a handsome building; rather as
the more vulgar of the Victorian wits used to talk about a fine woman.
By calling it a handsome building I mean that from the top of its dizzy
tower to the [[characters/bottom|bottom]] of its deepest foundations there is not one line
or one tint of [[concepts/beauty|beauty]].  This negative fact, however, would be nothing;
it might be honestly ugly and utilitarian like a factory or a [[motifs/prison|prison]];
but it is not.  It is as pretentious as the gilded dome below it;
and it is pretentious in a wicked way where the other is pretentious
in a good and [[archetypes/innocent|innocent]] way.  What annoys me about it is that it
was not built by children, or even by savages, but by professors;
and the professors could profess the art and could not practice it.
The architects knew everything about a Romanesque building except
how to build it.  We feel that they accumulated on that spot
all the learning and organization and information and wealth of
the world, to do this one particular thing; and then did it wrong.
They did it wrong, not through superstition, not through fanatical
exaggeration, not through provincial ignorance, but through pure,
profound, internal, intellectual incompetence; that intellectual
incompetence which so often goes with intellectual [[concepts/pride|pride]].
I will mention only one matter out of a hundred.  All the columns
in the Kaiser's Chapel are in one way very suitable to their place;
every one of them has a swelled head.  The column itself is slender
but the capital is not only big but bulging; and it has the air
of bulging <downwards>, as if pressing heavily on something too
slender to support it.  This is false, not to any of the particular
schools of architecture about which professors can read in libraries,
but to the inmost instinctive idea of architecture itself.
A Norman capital can be heavy because the Norman column is thick,
and the whole thing expresses an elephantine massiveness and repose.
And a Gothic column can be slender, because its strength is energy;
and is expressed in its line, which shoots upwards like the life of
a tree, like the jet of a fountain or even like the rush of a rocket.
But a slender thing beneath, obviously oppressed by a bloated
thing above, suggests weakness by one of those miraculous mistakes
that are as precisely wrong as masterpieces are precisely right.
And to all this is added the intolerable intuition; that the Russians
and the Franciscans, even if we credit them with fantastic ignorance,
are at least looking up at the sky; and we know how the learned
Germans would look down upon them, from their monstrous tower
upon the [[motifs/hill|hill]].

And this is as true of the moral as of the artistic elements
in the modern Jerusalem.  To show that I am not unjustly partisan,
I will say frankly that I see little to complain of in that common
subject of complaint, the mosaic portrait of the Emperor on the ceiling
of the chapel.  It is but one among many figures; and it is not an unknown
practice to include a figure of the founder in such church decorations.
The real example of that startling moral stupidity which marked
the barbaric imperialism can be found in another figure of which,
curiously enough, considerably less notice seems to have been taken.
It is the more remarkable because it is but an artistic [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of
the actual fact; and merely records in outline and relief the temporary
masquerade in which the man walked about in broad daylight.
I mean the really astounding trick of dressing himself up as a Crusader.

That was, under the circumstances, far more ludicrous and lunatic
a proceeding than if he had filled the whole ceiling with cherub
heads with his own features, or festooned all the walls with one
ornamental pattern of his moustaches.

The German Emperor came to Jerusalem under the escort of the Turks,
as the ally of the Turks, and solely because of the victory
and surpremacy of the Turks.  In other words, he came to Jerusalem
solely because the Crusaders had lost Jerusalem; he came there solely
because the Crusaders had been routed, ruined, butchered before
and after the disaster of Hattin; because the [[motifs/cross|Cross]] had gone
down in [[motifs/blood|blood]] before the Crescent, under which alone he could
ride in with safety.  Under those circumstances to dress up
as a Crusader, as if for a fancy dress ball, was a mixture
of [[concepts/madness|madness]] and vulgarity which literally stops the breath.
There is no need whatever to blame him for being in alliance with
the Turks; hundreds of people have been in alliance with the Turks;
the English especially have been far too much in alliance with them.
But if any one wants to appreciate the true difference, distinct from all
the cant of newspaper nationality, between the English and the Germans
(who were classed together by the same newspapers a little [[motifs/time|time]]
before the war) let him take this single incident as a test.
Lord Palmerston, for instance, was a firm friend of the Turks.
Imagine Lord Palmerston appearing in chain mail and the shield
of a Red [[motifs/cross|Cross]] Knight.

It is obvious enough that Palmerston would have said that he cared
no more for the Crusade than for the Siege of Troy; that his diplomacy
was directed by practical patriotic considerations of the moment;
and that he regarded the religious wars of the twelfth century
as a rubbish heap of remote superstitions.  In this he would be
quite wrong, but quite intelligible and quite sincere; an English
aristocrat of the nineteenth century inheriting from the English
aristocrats of the eighteenth century; whose views were simply
those of Voltaire.  And these things are something of an [[forms/allegory|allegory]].
For the Voltairian version of the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]] is still by far
the most reasonable of all merely hostile views of the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]].
If they were not a creative movement of religion, then they were
simply a destructive movement of superstition; and whether we agree
with Voltaire in calling it superstition or with Villehardouin in
calling it religion, at least both these very clear-headed Frenchmen
would agree that the motive did exist and did explain the facts.
But just as there is a clumsy German building with statues that at once
patronise and [[forms/parody|parody]] the Crusaders, so there is a clumsy German theory
that at once patronises and minimises the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]].  According to this
theory the essential [[concepts/truth|truth]] about a Crusade was that it was not a Crusade.
It was something that the professors, in the old days before the war,
used to call a Teutonic Folk-Wandering. Godfrey and St. Louis
were not, as Villehardouin would say, fighting for the [[concepts/truth|truth]];
they were not even, as Voltaire would say, fighting for what they
thought was the [[concepts/truth|truth]]; this was only what they thought they thought,
and they were really thinking of something entirely different.
They were not moved either by piety or priestcraft, but by a new
and unexpected nomadism.  They were not inspired either by [[concepts/faith|faith]]
or [[concepts/fanaticism|fanaticism]], but by an unusually aimless taste for foreign travel.
This theory that the war of the two great religions could be
explained by "Wanderlust" was current about twenty years ago among
the historical professors of [[settings/germany|Germany]], and with many of their other
views was often accepted by the historical professors of England.
It was swallowed by an earthquake, along with other rubbish,
in the year 1914.

Since then, so far as I know, the only person who has been patient
enough to dig it up again is Mr. Ezra Pound.  He is [[motifs/well|well]] known
as an American poet; and he is, I believe, a man of great talent
and information.  His attempt to recover the old Teutonic theory
of the Folk-Wandering of Peter the Hermit was expressed, however,
in prose; in an article in the <New Age>. I have no reason to [[concepts/doubt|doubt]]
that he was to be counted among the most loyal of our allies;
but he is evidently one of those who, quite without being Pro-German,
still manage to be German.  The Teutonic theory was very Teutonic;
like the German Hospice on the [[motifs/hill|hill]] it was put together with great care
and knowledge and it is rotten from top to [[characters/bottom|bottom]].  I do not understand,
for that matter, why that alliance which we enjoy with Mr. Pound
should not be treated in the same way as the other historical event;
or why the war should not be an example of the Wanderlust.
Surely the American Army in France must have drifted eastward merely
through the same vague nomadic need as the Christian Army in Palestine.
Surely Pershing as [[motifs/well|well]] as Peter the Hermit was merely a rather restless
gentleman who found his health improved by frequent change of scene.
The Americans said, and perhaps thought, that they were fighting
for [[concepts/democracy|democracy]]; and the Crusaders said, and perhaps thought,
that they were fighting for [[historical-references/christianity|Christianity]].  But as we know what
the Crusaders meant better than they did themselves, I cannot
quite understand why we do not enjoy the same valuable omniscience
about the Americans.  Indeed I do not see why we should not enjoy it
(for it would be very enjoyable) about any individual American.
Surely it was this vague vagabond spirit that moved Mr. Pound,
not only to come to England, but in a fashion to come to Fleet Street.
A. dim tribal tendency, vast and invisible as the [[motifs/wind|wind]], carried him
and his article like an [[motifs/autumn|autumn]] leaf to alight on the <New Age> doorstep.
Or a blind aboriginal impulse, wholly without rational motive,
led him one day to put on his hat, and go out with his article
in an envelope and put it in a pillar-box. It is vain to correct
by cold logic the power of such primitive appetites; [[concepts/nature|nature]] herself
was behind the seemingly random thoughtlessness of the deed.
And now that it is irrevocably done, he can look back on it and trace
the large lines of an awful law of averages; wherein it is ruled
by a ruthless necessity that a certain number of such Americans
should write a certain number of such articles, as the [[motifs/leaves|leaves]] fall
or the [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] return.

In plain words, this sort of theory is a blasphemy against
the intellectual dignity of man.  It is a blunder as [[motifs/well|well]] as
a blasphemy; for it goes miles out of its way to find a bestial
explanation when there is obviously a human explanation.
It is as if a man told me that a dim [[concepts/survival|survival]] of the instincts of a
quadruped was the reason of my sitting on a chair with four legs.
I answer that I do it because I foresee that there may be [[motifs/grave|grave]]
disadvantages in sitting on a chair with one leg.  Or it is as if I
were told that I liked to swim in the sea, solely because some early
forms of amphibian life came out of the sea on to the shore.
I answer that I know why I swim in the sea; and it is because
the divine gift of reason tells me that it would be unsatisfactory
to swim on the land.  In short this sort of vague evolutionary
theorising simply amounts to finding an unconvincing explanation
of something that needs no explanation.  And the case is really quite
as simple with great political and religious movements by which man
has from [[motifs/time|time]] to [[motifs/time|time]] changed the world in this or that respect
in which he happened to think it would be the better for a change.
The Crusade was a religious movement, but it was also a perfectly
rational movement; one might almost say a rationalist movement.
I could quite understand Mr. Pound saying that such a campaign for
a creed was immoral; and indeed it often has been, and now perhaps
generally is, quite horribly immoral.  But when he implies that it
is irrational he has selected exactly the thing which it is not.
It is not enlightenment, on the contrary it is ignorance and insularity,
which causes most of us to miss this fact.  But it certainly is the fact
that religious war is in itself much more rational than patriotic way.
I for one have often defended and even encouraged patriotic war,
and should always be ready to defend and encourage patriotic passion.
But it cannot be denied that there is more of mere passion,
of mere preference and prejudice, in short of mere personal accident,
in fighting another nation than in fighting another [[concepts/faith|faith]].
The Crusader is in every sense more rational than the modern
conscript or professional soldier.  He is more rational in
his object, which is the intelligent and intelligible object
of [[concepts/conversion|conversion]]; where the modern militarist has an object much
more confused by momentary [[concepts/vanity|vanity]] and one-sided satisfaction.
The Crusader wished to make Jerusalem a Christian town;
but the Englishman does not wish to make Berlin an English town.
He has only a healthy hatred of it as a Prussian town.
The Moslem wished to make the Christian a Moslem; but even
the Prussian did not wish to make the Frenchman a Prussian.
He only wished to make the Frenchman admire a Prussian;
and not only were the means he adopted somewhat ill-considered for
this purpose, but the purpose itself is looser and more irrational.
The object of all war is peace; but the object of religious
war is mental as [[motifs/well|well]] as material peace; it is agreement.
In short religious war aims ultimately at equality, where national
war aims relatively at superiority.  [[concepts/conversion|Conversion]] is the one sort
of conquest in which the conquered must rejoice.

In that sense alone it is foolish for us in the West to sneer
at those who kill men when a foot is set in a holy place,
when we ourselves kill hundreds of thousands when a foot is put
across a frontier.  It is absurd for us to despise those who shed
[[motifs/blood|blood]] for a relic when we have shed rivers of [[motifs/blood|blood]] for a rag.
But above all the Crusade, or, for that matter, the Jehad,
is by far the most philosophical sort of fighting, not only
in its conception of ending the difference, but in its mere act
of recognising the difference, as the deepest kind of difference.
It is to reverse all reason to suggest that a man's politics matter
and his religion does not matter.  It is to say he is affected
by the town he lives in, but not by the world he lives in.
It is to say that he is altered when he is a fellow-citizen walking
under new lamp-posts, but not altered when he is another creature walking
under strange [[motifs/stars|stars]].  It is exactly as if we were to say that two people
ought to live in the same house, but it need not be in the same town.
It is exactly as if we said that so long as the address included
York it did not matter whether it was [[settings/new-york|New York]]; or that so long
as a man is in Essex we do not care whether he is in England.

Christendom would have been entirely justified in the abstract
in being alarmed or suspicious at the mere rise of a great power
that was not Christian.  Nobody nowadays would think it odd
to express regret at the rise of a power because it was Militarist
or Socialist or even Protectionist.  But it is far more natural
to be conscious of a difference, not about the order of battle but
the battle of life; not about our definable enjoyment of possessions,
but about our much more doubtful possession of enjoyment;
not about the fiscal divisions between us and foreigners
but about the spiritual divisions even between us and friends.
These are the things that differ profoundly with differing views
of the ultimate [[concepts/nature|nature]] of the universe.  For the things of our country
are often distant; but the things of our cosmos are always near;
we can shut our doors upon the wheeled traffic of our native town;
but in our own inmost chamber we hear the sound that never ceases;
that [[motifs/wheel|wheel]] which [[historical-references/dante|Dante]] and a popular proverb have dared
to christen as the [[motifs/love|love]] that makes the world go round.
For this is the great [[concepts/paradox|paradox]] of life; that there are not only
wheels within wheels, but the larger wheels within the smaller.
When a whole community rests on one conception of life and [[motifs/death|death]]
and the origin of things, it is quite entitled to watch the rise
of another community founded on another conception as the rise
of something certain to be different and likely to be hostile.
Indeed, as I have pointed out touching certain political theories,
we already admit this [[concepts/truth|truth]] in its small and questionable examples.
We only deny the large and obvious examples.

Christendom might quite reasonably have been alarmed if it had not
been attacked.  But as a matter of [[concepts/history|history]] it had been attacked.
The Crusader would have been quite justified in suspecting the Moslem
even if the Moslem had merely been a new stranger; but as a matter
of [[concepts/history|history]] he was already an old enemy.  The critic of the Crusade
talks as if it had sought out some inoffensive tribe or temple in the
interior of Thibet, which was never discovered until it was invaded.
They seem entirely to forget that long before the Crusaders had dreamed
of riding to Jerusalem, the Moslems had almost ridden into [[settings/paris|Paris]].
They seem to forget that if the Crusaders nearly conquered Palestine,
it was but a return upon the Moslems who had nearly conquered Europe.
There was no need for them to argue by an appeal to reason, as I
have argued above, that a religious division must make a difference;
it had already made a difference.  The difference stared them
in the face in the startling transformation of Roman Barbary
and of Roman Spain.  In short it was something which must happen
in theory and which did happen in practice; all expectation
suggested that it would be so and all experience said it was so.
Having thought it out theoretically and experienced it practically,
they proceeded to deal with it equally practically.  The first division
involved every principle of the science of thought; and the last
developments followed out every principle of the science of war.
The Crusade was the counter-attack. It was the defensive army taking
the offensive in its turn, and driving back the enemy to his base.
And it is this process, reasonable from its first axiom to its last act,
that Mr. Pound actually selects as a sort of automatic wandering
of an animal.  But a man so intelligent would not have made a mistake
so extraordinary but for another error which it is here very essential
to consider.  To suggest that men engaged, rightly or wrongly,
in so logical a military and political operation were only migrating
like [[motifs/birds|birds]] or swarming like [[motifs/bees|bees]] is as ridiculous as to say that
the Prohibition campaign in [[settings/america|America]] was only an animal reversion
towards lapping as the dog lappeth, or Rowland [[motifs/hill|Hill]]'s introduction
of postage stamps an animal taste for licking as the cat licks.
Why should we provide other people with a remote reason for their
own actions, when they themselves are ready to tell us the reason,
and it is a perfectly reasonable reason?

I have compared this pompous imposture of scientific [[concepts/history|history]] to
the pompous and clumsy building of the scientific Germans on the Mount
of Olives, because it substitutes in the same way a modern stupidity
for the medieval simplicity.  But just as the German Hospice after
all stands on a fine site, and might have been a fine building,
so there is after all another [[concepts/truth|truth]], somewhat analogous,
which the German historians of the Folk-Wanderings might possibly
have meant, as distinct from all that they have actually said.
There is indeed one respect in which the case of the Crusade does
differ very much from modern political cases like prohibition
or the penny post.  I do not refer to such incidental peculiarities
as the fact that Prohibition could only have succeeded through
the enormous power of modern plutocracy, or that even the convenience
of the postage goes along with an extreme coercion by the police.
It is a somewhat deeper difference that I mean; and it may possibly be
what these critics mean.  But the difference is not in the evolutionary,
but rather the revolutionary spirit.

The First Crusade was not a racial migration; it was something much
more intellectual and dignified; a riot.  In order to understand this
religious war we must class it, not so much with the wars of, [[concepts/history|history]] as
with the revolutions of [[concepts/history|history]].  As I shall try to show briefly on
a later page, it not only had all the peculiar good and the peculiar
evil of things like the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]] or the Russian Revolution,
but it was a more purely popular revolution than either of them.
The truly modern mind will of course regard the contention that it
was popular as tantamount to a confession that it was animal.
In these days when papers and speeches are full of words like
[[concepts/democracy|democracy]] and [[concepts/self|self]]-determination, anything really resembling
the movement of a mass of angry men Is regarded as no better than
a stampede of bulls or a scurry of rats.  The new sociologists
call it the herd instinct, just as the old reactionaries called it
the many-headed beast.  But both agree in implying that it is hardly
worth while to count how many head there are of such cattle.
In face of such fashionable comparisons it will seem comparatively
mild to talk of migration as it occurs among [[motifs/birds|birds]] or [[motifs/insects|insects]].
Nevertheless we may venture to state with some confidence
that both the sociologists and the reactionaries are wrong.
It does not follow that human beings become less than human because their
ideas appeal to more and more of humanity.  Nor can we deduce that men
are mindless solely from the fact that they are all of one mind.
In plain fact the virtues of a mob cannot be found in a herd
of bulls or a pack of wolves, any more than the crimes of a mob
can be committed by a flock of sheep or a shoal of herrings.
[[motifs/birds|Birds]] have never been known to besiege and capture an empty cage
of an aviary, on a point of principle, merely because it had kept a few
other [[motifs/birds|birds]] in captivity, as the mob besieged and captured the almost
empty Bastille, merely because it was the fortress of a historic tyranny.
And rats have never been known to die by thousands merely in order
to visit a particular trap in which a particular rat had perished,
as the poor peasants of the First Crusade died in thousands for a
far-off sight of the Sepulchre or a [[forms/fragment|fragment]] of the true [[motifs/cross|cross]].
In this sense indeed the Crusade was not rationalistic, if the rat
is the only rationalist.  But it will seem more truly rational
to point out that the inspiration of such a crowd is not in such
instincts as we share with the [[motifs/animals|animals]], but precisely in such ideas
as the [[motifs/animals|animals]] never (with all their virtues) understand.

What is peculiar about the First Crusade is that it was in quite
a new and abnormal sense a popular movement.  I might almost say
it was the only popular movement there ever was in the world.
For it was not a thing which the populace followed; it was actually
a thing which the populace led.  It was not only essentially
a revolution, but it was the only revolution I know of in which
the masses began by acting alone, and practically without any
support from any of the classes.  When they had acted, the classes
came in; and it is perfectly true, and indeed only natural,
that the masses alone failed where the two together succeeded.
But it was the uneducated who educated the educated.
The case of the Crusade is emphatically not a case in which certain
ideas were first suggested by a few philosophers, and then preached
by demagogues to the [[concepts/democracy|democracy]].  This was to a great extent true
of the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]]; it was probably yet more true of the
Russian Revolution; and we need not here pause upon the fine shade
of difference that Rousseau was right and Karl Marx was wrong.
In the First Crusade it was the ordinary man who was right or wrong.
He came out in a fury at the insult to his own little images or
private prayers, as if he had come out to fight with his own domestic
poker or private carving-knife. He was not armed with new weapons
of wit and logic served round from the arsenal of an academy.
There was any amount of wit and logic in the academies of the Middle Ages;
but the typical leader of the Crusade was not Abélard or [[characters/aquinas|Aquinas]]
but Peter the Hermit, who can hardly be called even a popular leader,
but rather a popular [[motifs/flag|flag]].  And it was his army, or rather
his enormous rabble, that first marched across the world to die
for the deliverance of Jerusalem.

Historians say that in that huge host of thousands there were only
nine knights.  To any one who knows even a little of medieval
war the fact seems astounding.  It is indeed a long exploded
fallacy to regard medievalism as identical with feudalism.
There were countless democratic institutions, such as the guilds;
sometimes as many as twenty guilds in one small town.
But it is really true that the military organization of the Middle Ages
was almost entirely feudal; indeed we might rather say that feudalism
was the name of their military organization.  That so vast a military
mass should have attempted to move at all, with only nine of the natural
military leaders, seems to me a prodigy of popular initiative.
It is as if a parliament were elected at the next general election,
in which only two men could afford to read a daily newspaper.

This mob marched against the military discipline of the Moslems
and was massacred; or, might I so mystically express it, martyred.
Many of the great kings and knights who followed in their tracks
did not so clearly deserve any haloes for the simplicity and purity
of their motives.  The canonization of such a crowd might be impossible,
and would certainly be resisted in modern opinion; chiefly because they
indulged their democratic violence on the way by killing various usurers;
a course which naturally fills modern society with an anger verging
on alarm.  A perversity leads me to weep rather more over the many
slaughtered peasants than over the few slaughtered usurers;
but in any case the peasants certainly were not slaughtered in vain.
The common [[concepts/conscience|conscience]] of all classes, in a [[motifs/time|time]] when all had
a common creed, was aroused, and a new army followed of a very
different type of skill and training; led by most of the ablest
captains and by some of the most chivalrous gentlemen of the age.
For curiously enough, the host contained more than one cultured
gentleman who was as simple a Christian as any peasant,
and as recklessly ready to be butchered or tortured for the mere
name of [[characters/christ|Christ]].

It is a tag of the materialists that the [[concepts/truth|truth]] about [[concepts/history|history]]
rubs away the romance of [[concepts/history|history]].  It is dear to the modern mind
because it is depressing; but it does not happen to be true.
Nothing emerges more clearly from a study that is truly realistic,
than the curious fact that romantic people were really romantic.
It is rather the historical novels that will lead a modern
man vaguely to expect to find the leader of the new knights,
Godfrey de Bouillon, to have been merely a brutal baron.
The historical facts are all in favour of his having been much
more like a knight of the Round Table.  In fact he was a far
better man than most of the knights of the Round Table, in whose
characters the fabulist, knowing that he was writing a [[forms/fable|fable]],
was tactful enough to introduce a larger admixture of vice.  [[concepts/truth|Truth]] is
not only stranger than fiction, but often saintlier than fiction.
For [[concepts/truth|truth]] is real, while fiction is bound to be realistic.
Curiously enough Godfrey seems to have been heroic even in those
admirable accidents which are generally and perhaps rightly regarded
as the trappings of fiction.  Thus he was of heroic stature,
a handsome red-bearded man of great personal strength and daring;
and he was himself the first man over the wall of Jerusalem,
like any boy [[archetypes/hero|hero]] in a boy's adventure story.  But he was also,
the realist will be surprised to hear, a perfectly honest man,
and a perfectly genuine practicer of the theoretical magnanimity
of knighthood.  Everything about him suggests it; from his first
[[concepts/conversion|conversion]] from the imperial to the papal (and popular) cause, to his
great refusal of the kinghood of the [[motifs/city|city]] he had taken; "I will
not wear a [[motifs/crown|crown]] of gold where my Master wore a [[motifs/crown|crown]] of thorns."
He was a just ruler, and the laws he made were full of the plainest
public spirit.  But even if we dismiss all that was written
of him by Christian chroniclers because they might be his friends
(which would be a pathetic and exaggerated compliment to the harmonious
unity of Crusaders and of Christians) he would still remain
sufficiently assailed crowned with the words of his enemies.
For a Saracen chronicler wrote of him, with a fine simplicity,
that if all [[concepts/truth|truth]] and [[concepts/honour|honour]] had otherwise withered off the earth,
there would still remain enough of them so long as Duke Godfrey was alive.

Allied with Godfrey were Tancred the Italian, Raymond of Toulouse
with the southern French and Robert of Normandy, the adventurous
son of the Conqueror, with the Normans and the English.
But it would be an error, I think, and one tending to make the whole
subsequent story a thing not so much misunderstood as unintelligible,
to suppose that the whole crusading movement had been suddenly
and unnaturally stiffened with the highest chivalric discipline.
Unless I am much mistaken, a great mass of that army
was still very much of a mob.  It is probable <a priori>,
since the great popular movement was still profoundly popular.
It is supported by a thousand things in the story of the campaign;
the extraordinary emotionalism that made throngs of men weep and
wail together, the importance of the demagogue, Peter the Hermit,
in spite of his unmilitary character, and the wide differences between
the designs of the leaders and the actions of the rank and file.
It was a crowd of rude and simple men that cast themselves
on the sacred [[motifs/dust|dust]] at the first sight of the little mountain
town which they had tramped for two thousand miles to see.
Tancred saw it first from the slope by the village of Bethlehem,
which had opened its gates willingly to his hundred Italian knights;
for Bethlehem then as now was an island of Christendom in the sea
of [[historical-references/islam|Islam]].  Meanwhile Godfrey came up the [[motifs/road|road]] from Jaffa,
and crossing the mountain ridge, saw also with his living eyes
his vision of the world's [[motifs/desire|desire]].  But the poorest men about him
probably felt the same as he; all ranks knelt together in the [[motifs/dust|dust]],
and the whole story is one wave of numberless and nameless men.
It was a mob that had risen like a man for the [[concepts/faith|faith]].
It was a mob that had truly been tortured like a man for the [[concepts/faith|faith]].
It was already transfigured by [[motifs/pain|pain]] as [[motifs/well|well]] as passion.
Those that know war in those deserts through the [[motifs/summer|summer]] months,
even with modern supplies and appliances and modern maps and calculations,
know that it could only be described as a hell full of heroes.
What it must have been to those little local serfs and peasants from
the Northern villages, who had never dreamed in nightmares of such
landscapes or such a sun, who knew not how men lived at all in such
a furnace and could neither guess the alleviations nor get them,
is beyond the [[concepts/imagination|imagination]] of man.  They arrived dying with thirst,
dropping with weariness, lamenting the loss of the dead that rotted
along their [[motifs/road|road]]; they arrived shrivelled to rags or already raving
with fever and they did what they had come to do.

Above all, it is clear that they had the vices as [[motifs/well|well]] as the virtues
of a mob.  The shocking massacre in which they indulged in the sudden
relaxation of success is quite obviously a massacre by a mob.
It is all the more profoundly revolutionary because it must have
been for the most part a French mob.  It was of the same order
as the Massacre of September, and it is but a part of the same [[concepts/truth|truth]]
that the First Crusade was as revolutionary as the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]].
It was of the same order as the Massacre of St. Bartholomew,
which was also a piece of purely popular [[concepts/fanaticism|fanaticism]], directed
against what was also regarded as an anti-national aristocracy.
It is practically [[concepts/self|self]]-evident that the Christian commanders were
opposed to it, and tried to stop it.  Tancred promised their lives
to the Moslems in the mosque, but the mob clearly disregarded him.
Raymond of Toulouse himself saved those in the Tower of David,
and managed to send them safely with their property to Ascalon.
But revolution with all its evil as [[motifs/well|well]] as its good was loose
and raging in the streets of the Holy [[motifs/city|City]].  And in nothing do we
see that spirit of revolution more clearly than in the sight
of all those peasants and serfs and vassals, in that one wild
moment in revolt, not only against the conquered lords of [[historical-references/islam|Islam]],
but even against the conquering lords of Christendom.

The whole strain of the siege indeed had been one of high and even
horrible excitement.  Those who tell us to-day about the psychology
of the crowd will agree that men who have so suffered and so succeeded
are not normal; that their brains are in a dreadful balance which may
turn either way.  They entered the [[motifs/city|city]] at last in a mood in which they
might all have become [[characters/monks|monks]]; and instead they all became murderers.
A brilliant general, who played a decisive part in our own recent
Palestinian campaign, told me with a sort of grim humour that he hardly
wondered at the story; for he himself had entered Jerusalem in a sort
of fury of disappointment; "We went through such a hell to get there,
and now it's spoilt for all of us."  Such is the heavy irony that
hangs over our human [[concepts/nature|nature]], making it enter the Holy [[motifs/city|City]] as if it
were the Heavenly [[motifs/city|City]], and more than any earthly [[motifs/city|city]] can be.
But the struggle which led to the scaling of Jerusalem in the
First Crusade was something much wilder and more incalculable than
anything that can be conceived in modern war.  We can hardly [[concepts/wonder|wonder]]
that the crusading crowd saw the town in front of them as a sort
of tower full of demons, and the hills around them as an enchanted
and accursed land.  For in one very real sense it really was so;
for all the elements and expedients were alike unknown qualities.
All their enemies' methods were [[motifs/secrets|secrets]] sprung upon them.
All their own methods were new things made out of nothing.
They wondered alike what would be done on the other side and what
could be done on their own side; every movement against them
was a stab out of the darkness and every movement they made
was a leap in the dark.  First, on the one side, we have Tancred
trying to take the whole fortified [[motifs/city|city]] by climbing up a single
slender ladder, as if a man tried to lasso the peak of a mountain.
Then we have the flinging from the turrets of a strange
and frightful fiery rain, as if [[motifs/water|water]] itself had caught [[motifs/fire|fire]].
It was afterwards known as the Greek [[motifs/fire|Fire]] and was probably petroleum;
but to those who had never seen (or felt) it before it may [[motifs/well|well]] have
seemed the flaming oil of witchcraft.  Then Godfrey and the wiser
of the warriors set about to build wooden siege-towers and found
they had next to no wood to build them.  There was scarcely anything
in that rocky waste but the [[characters/dwarf|dwarf]] trees of olive; a poetic fantasy
woven about that war in after ages described them as hindered
even in their wood-cutting by the demons of that weird place.
And indeed the fancy had an essential [[concepts/truth|truth]], for the very [[concepts/nature|nature]]
of the land fought against them; and each of those [[characters/dwarf|dwarf]] trees,
hard and hollow and twisted, may [[motifs/well|well]] have seemed like a grinning goblin.
It is said that they found timbers by accident in a cavern;
they tore down the beams from ruined houses; at last they got into touch
with some craftsmen from Genoa who went to work more successfully;
skinning the cattle, who had died in heaps, and covering the timbers.
They built three high towers on rollers, and men and beasts
dragged them heavily against the high towers of the [[motifs/city|city]].
The catapaults of the [[motifs/city|city]] answered them, the cataracts of
devouring [[motifs/fire|fire]] came down; the wooden towers swayed and tottered,
and two of them suddenly stuck motionless and useless.
And as the darkness fell a great flare must have told them that
the third and last was in flames.

All that [[motifs/night|night]] Godfrey was toiling to retrieve the disaster.
He took down the whole tower from where it stood and raised
it again on the high ground to the north of the [[motifs/city|city]] which is
now marked by the pine tree that grows outside Herod's gate.
And all the [[motifs/time|time]] he toiled, it was said, sinister sorcerers sat
upon the battlements, working unknown marvels for the undoing
of the labour of man.  If the great knight had a touch of such
symbolism on his own side, he might have seen in his own strife
with the solid timber something of the craft that had surrounded
the birth of his creed, and the sacred trade of the carpenter.
And indeed the very pattern of all carpentry is cruciform, and there
is something more than an accident in the [[forms/allegory|allegory]].  The transverse
position of the timber does indeed involve many of those mathematical
that are analogous to moral truths and almost every structural
shape has the [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the mystic rood, as the three dimensions
have a [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the Trinity.  Here is the true mystery of equality;
since the longer beam might lengthen itself to infinity, and never
be nearer to the symbolic shape without the help of the shorter.
Here is that war and wedding between two contrary forces, resisting and
supporting each other; the meeting-place of contraries which we,
by a sort of pietistic pun, still call the crux of the question.
Here is our angular and defiant answer to the [[concepts/self|self]]-devouring circle
of Asia.  It may be improbable, though it is far from impossible
(for the age was philosophical enough) that a man like Godfrey
thus extended the mystical to the metaphysical; but the writer
of a real romance about him would be [[motifs/well|well]] within his rights in making
him see the symbolism of his own tower, a tower rising above
him through the clouds of [[motifs/night|night]] as if taking hold on the heaven
or showing its network of beams black against the daybreak;
scaling the skies and open to all the winds, a ladder and a labyrinth,
repeating till it was lost in the twilight the pattern of the sign
of the [[motifs/cross|cross]].

When [[motifs/dawn|dawn]] was come all those starving peasants may [[motifs/well|well]] have stood
before the high impregnable walls in the broad daylight of [[concepts/despair|despair]].
Even their nightmares during the [[motifs/night|night]], of unearthly necromancers
looking down at them from the battlements and with signs and spells
paralysing all their potential toils, may [[motifs/well|well]] have been a sort
of pessimistic consolation, anticipating and accounting for failure.
The Holy [[motifs/city|City]] had become for them a fortress full of fiends, when Godfrey
de Bouillon again set himself sword in hand upon the wooden tower and gave
the order once more to drag it tottering towards the towers on either
side of the pastern gate.  So they crawled again across the fosse
full of the slain, dragging their huge house of timber behind them,
and all the blast and din of war broke again about their heads.
A hail of bolts hammered such shields as covered them for a canopy,
stones and rocks fell on them and crushed them like flies in
the mire, and from the engines of the Greek [[motifs/fire|Fire]] all the torrents
of their torment came down on them like red rivers of hell.
For indeed the souls of those peasants must have been sickened
with something of the topsy-turvydom felt by too many peasants of our
own [[motifs/time|time]] under the frightful flying batteries of scientific war;
a blasphemy of inverted battle in which hell itself has occupied heaven.
Something of the vapours vomited by such cruel chemistry may
have mingled with the [[motifs/dust|dust]] of battle, and darkened such light
as showed where shattering rocks were rending a roof of shields,
to men bowed and blinded as they are by such labour of dragging
and such a hailstorm of [[motifs/death|death]].  They may have heard through
all the racket of nameless noises the high minaret cries
of Moslem triumph rising shriller like a [[motifs/wind|wind]] in shrill pipes,
and known little else of what was happening above or beyond them.
It was most likely that they laboured and strove in that lower darkness,
not knowing that high over their heads, and up above the cloud
of battle, the tower of timber and the tower of stone had touched
and met in mid-heaven; and great Godfrey, alone and alive had leapt
upon the wall of Jerusalem.

Il significato della Crociata

Di G.K. Chesterton 

Da "The New Jerusalem" 1920

Vi sono tre esempi di opera occidentale sul grande pendio orientale del Monte degli Ulivi; ed essi formano una sorta di triangolo che illustra la [[concepts/truth|verità]] intorno alle diverse influenze dell'Occidente sull'Oriente. Ai piedi del [[motifs/hill|colle]] sta il [[motifs/garden|giardino]] tenuto dai francescani sul presunto sito del Getsemani, e che racchiude il canuto olivo che si suppone sia il terribile albero dell'agonia di [[characters/christ|Cristo]]. Data la grande età e la lenta crescita degli olivi, la tradizione non è tanto irragionevole quanto alcuni potrebbero credere. Ma che sia o no storicamente giusta, essa non è artisticamente sbagliata. L'istinto, se era soltanto un istinto, che spinse gli uomini a fissarsi su questa strana crescita di legno grigio e contorto, era un vero istinto immaginativo. Una delle strane qualità di questo strano albero meridionale è la sua durezza quasi sconcertante; colpire accidentalmente il ramo di un olivo è come colpire la roccia. Con la sua superficie petrosa, la statura stentata, e i suoi strani fori e cavità, esso somiglia spesso più a una grotta che a un albero. Perciò non pare tanto innaturale che venga trattato come una santa grotta; o che questa strana vegetazione pretenda di ergersi per sempre come un monumento scolpito. Persino lo scintillante o tremulo fogliame argenteo dell'olivo vivente potrebbe [[motifs/well|bene]] avere una leggenda come quella del pioppo tremulo; come se fosse ingrigito di [[concepts/fear|paura]] dinanzi all'apocalittico [[concepts/paradox|paradosso]] di una divina visione di [[motifs/death|morte]]. Un [[archetypes/child|bambino]] di uno dei villaggi mi disse, in inglese stentato, che era il luogo dove Dio diceva le sue preghiere. Io per primo non saprei chiedere un'affermazione più bella o più fiera di tutto ciò che separa il cristiano dal musulmano o dall'ebreo; <credo quia impossibile>.

Attorno a questo terribile luogo i francescani hanno fatto qualcosa che colpirà molte persone buone e riflessive come fantasticamente inadeguato; e che colpisce me come fantasticamente ma precisamente giusto. Hanno disposto il [[motifs/garden|giardino]] semplicemente come un [[motifs/garden|giardino]], in un modo che è del tutto naturale perché del tutto artificiale. Hanno fatto aiuole a forma di [[motifs/stars|stelle]] e di lune, e le hanno colorate con [[motifs/flowers|fiori]] come quelli nel cortile di una casetta. La combinazione di questi vividi motivi al sole con la terribile [[archetypes/shadow|ombra]] al centro è certamente un'incongruenza nel senso di un contrasto. Ma è un contrasto poetico, come quello di [[motifs/birds|uccelli]] che nidificano in un tempio o di [[motifs/flowers|fiori]] che crescono su una tomba. Il modo migliore di suggerire ciò che io per primo provo al riguardo sarebbe qualcosa di simile a questo; supponiamo d'immaginare una compagnia di bambini, come quelli che [[characters/christ|Cristo]] benedisse in Gerusalemme, poi posti permanentemente a custodia di un campo colmo del suo dolore; è probabile che, se potessero farne qualcosa, farebbero qualcosa di simile a questo. Potrebbero ritagliarlo in bizzarre forme e punteggiarlo di rosse pratoline o di gialle calendule. Davvero non so se vi sia nulla di meglio che gli adulti potrebbero fare, poiché qualunque cosa il più grande di essi potesse fare dovrebbe, dovrebbe apparire altrettanto piccola. "Oserò io, il moscerino che danza nel Tuo raggio, di essere reverente?" I francescani non hanno osato essere reverenti; hanno osato soltanto essere allegri. Può essere un'avventura troppo tremenda dell'[[concepts/imagination|immaginazione]] immaginare [[characters/christ|Cristo]] in quel [[motifs/garden|giardino]]. Ma non c'è la minima difficoltà a immaginarvi san Francesco; e questo è qualcosa da dire di un'istituzione che ha ottocento anni.

Immediatamente sopra questo piccolo [[motifs/garden|giardino]], che lo sovrasta e quasi lo incombe, sta uno sfarzoso edificio dorato con cupole d'oro e minareti scintillanti al sole, che colma una posizione splendida con quasi spudorato splendore; la chiesa russa costruita sulla parte superiore del [[motifs/garden|giardino]], appartenente ai greco-ortodossi. Anche qui molti viaggiatori occidentali saranno turbati; e penseranno che quell'edificio dorato somigli troppo a un palazzo fatato di una pantomima. Ma anche qui io dissentirò da loro, benché forse meno fermamente. Può darsi che il piacere sia puerile piuttosto che infantile; ma posso immaginare un [[archetypes/child|bambino]] battere le mani alla sola vista di quelle grandi cupole simili a bolle d'oro contro il cielo azzurro. È un po' come il Palazzo di Aladino, ma ha un posto nell'arte come Aladino ha un posto nella letteratura; specialmente poiché si tratta di letteratura orientale. Quei saggi missionari in Cina che non ebbero timore di raffigurare i Dodici Apostoli in costume da cinesi avrebbero potuto costruire una chiesa simile in una terra di scintillanti moschee. E poiché si dice che il russo abbia in sé qualcosa del [[archetypes/child|bambino]] e qualcosa dell'orientale, uno stile così può essere assai sincero, e avere persino una certa semplicità nel suo splendore. È genuino nel suo genere; fu costruito per coloro cui piace; e coloro cui non piace possono guardare qualcos'altro. Questa sorta di cosa può dirsi pacchiana, ma non è ciò che io chiamo meretricio. Ciò che io chiamo veramente meretricio si può trovare ancora più in alto sul [[motifs/hill|colle]]; che torreggia verso il cielo e domina tutte le valli.

La [[concepts/nature|natura]] della differenza, io penso, merita d'essere notata. L'Ospizio Tedesco, che servì da una sorta di palazzo per l'Imperatore tedesco, è un edificio assai grande con una torre assai alta, progettato, credo, con grande efficienza, solidità e comodità, e dotato di mille cose che ne segnano la modernità rispetto alle cose circostanti, rispetto al bizzarro [[motifs/garden|giardino]] dei francescani o al fantastico tempio dei russi. È ciò che posso solo descrivere come un bell'edificio; piuttosto come i più volgari degli spiriti vittoriani solevano parlare di una bella donna. Chiamandolo un bell'edificio intendo che dalla cima della sua vertiginosa torre al [[characters/bottom|fondo]] delle sue più profonde fondamenta non vi è una sola linea o una sola tinta di [[concepts/beauty|bellezza]]. Questo fatto negativo, tuttavia, non sarebbe nulla; potrebbe essere onestamente brutto e utilitaristico come una fabbrica o una [[motifs/prison|prigione]]; ma non lo è. È pretenzioso quanto la cupola dorata al di sotto; ed è pretenzioso in modo malvagio là dove l'altra è pretenziosa in modo buono e [[archetypes/innocent|innocente]]. Ciò che mi irrita in esso è che non fu costruito da bambini, e nemmeno da selvaggi, ma da professori; e i professori potevano professare l'arte ma non praticarla. Gli architetti sapevano ogni cosa su un edificio romanico tranne come costruirlo. Sentiamo che accumularono su quel luogo tutta la dottrina e l'organizzazione e l'informazione e la ricchezza del mondo, per fare questa unica cosa particolare; e poi la fecero male. La fecero male, non per superstizione, non per esagerazione fanatica, non per ignoranza provinciale, ma per pura, profonda, interiore, intellettuale incompetenza; quell'incompetenza intellettuale che tanto spesso va di pari passo con l'[[concepts/pride|orgoglio]] intellettuale. Menzionerò una sola cosa su un centinaio. Tutte le colonne nella Cappella del Kaiser sono per un verso assai adatte al loro posto; ognuna di esse ha la testa gonfia. La colonna in sé è snella ma il capitello non solo è grande bensì rigonfio; e ha l'aria di rigonfiarsi <verso il basso>, come se premesse pesantemente su qualcosa di troppo snello per sostenerlo. Questo è falso, non rispetto ad alcuna delle particolari scuole di architettura di cui i professori possono leggere nelle biblioteche, ma rispetto all'idea più intima e istintiva dell'architettura stessa. Un capitello normanno può essere pesante perché la colonna normanna è spessa, e il tutto esprime una massiccia mole elefantina e riposo. E una colonna gotica può essere snella, perché la sua forza è energia; ed è espressa nella sua linea, che si slancia verso l'alto come la vita di un albero, come il getto di una fontana o persino come lo scatto di un razzo. Ma una cosa snella al di sotto, ovviamente oppressa da una cosa gonfia al di sopra, suggerisce debolezza per uno di quei miracolosi errori che sono precisamente sbagliati come i capolavori sono precisamente giusti. E a tutto ciò si aggiunge l'intollerabile intuizione; che i russi e i francescani, anche se li accreditassimo di fantastica ignoranza, stanno almeno guardando in su verso il cielo; e sappiamo come i dotti tedeschi guarderebbero dall'alto in basso su di loro, dalla loro mostruosa torre sul [[motifs/hill|colle]].

E questo è vero degli elementi morali quanto di quelli artistici nella Gerusalemme moderna. Per mostrare che non sono un ingiusto partigiano, dirò francamente che vedo poco da lamentare in quel comune motivo di lamentela, il ritratto a mosaico dell'Imperatore sul soffitto della cappella. È solo una fra molte figure; e non è una pratica ignota includere una figura del fondatore in simili decorazioni ecclesiastiche. Il vero esempio di quella sconcertante stupidità morale che contrassegnò l'imperialismo barbarico si può trovare in un'altra figura di cui, per quanto curioso, sembra essersi presa assai minor nota. È tanto più notevole in quanto è solo un'[[archetypes/shadow|ombra]] artistica del fatto reale; e registra soltanto, in linea e rilievo, la temporanea mascherata in cui quell'uomo se ne andava in giro in pieno giorno. Intendo la trovata davvero sbalorditiva di travestirsi da Crociato.

Questo era, date le circostanze, un procedimento assai più ridicolo e folle che se avesse riempito l'intero soffitto di teste di cherubino con i propri lineamenti, o festonato tutte le pareti con un unico motivo ornamentale dei suoi baffi.

L'Imperatore tedesco venne a Gerusalemme sotto la scorta dei turchi, come alleato dei turchi, e unicamente in virtù della vittoria e della supremazia dei turchi. La Prima Crociata non fu una migrazione razziale; fu qualcosa di molto più intellettuale e dignitoso: un tumulto. Ciò che vi è di singolare nella Prima Crociata è che essa fu, in un senso del tutto nuovo e anomalo, un movimento popolare. Direi quasi che fu l'unico movimento popolare che vi sia mai stato al mondo. Perché non fu una cosa che il popolo seguì; fu anzi una cosa che il popolo guidò. Non solo fu essenzialmente una rivoluzione, ma fu l'unica rivoluzione di cui io sappia in cui le masse cominciarono ad agire da sole, e praticamente senza alcun sostegno da parte di nessuna delle classi. Nella Prima Crociata fu l'uomo comune ad avere ragione o torto. Egli si levò furibondo per l'offesa alle proprie piccole immagini o preghiere private, come se fosse uscito a combattere col proprio attizzatoio domestico o col proprio trinciante. The Meaning of the Crusade

By G.K. Chesterton 

From "The New Jerusalem" 1920

There are three examples of Western work on the great eastern slope
of the Mount of Olives; and they form a sort of triangle illustrating
the [[concepts/truth|truth]] about the different influences of the West on the East.
At the foot of the [[motifs/hill|hill]] is the [[motifs/garden|garden]] kept by the Franciscans
on the alleged site of Gethsemane, and containing the hoary olive
that is supposed to be the terrible tree of the agony of [[characters/christ|Christ]].
Given the great age and slow growth of the olives, the tradition
is not so unreasonable as some may suppose.  But whether or not it
is historically right, it is not artistically wrong.  The instinct,
if it was only an instinct, that made men fix upon this strange
growth of grey and twisted wood, was a true imaginative instinct.
One of the strange qualities of this strange Southern tree is
its almost startling hardness; accidentally to strike the branch
of an olive is like striking rock.  With its stony surface,
stunted stature, and strange holes and hollows, it is often more
like a grotto than a tree.  Hence it does not seem so unnatural
that it should be treated as a holy grotto; or that this strange
vegetation should claim to stand for ever like a sculptured monument.
Even the shimmering or shivering silver foliage of the living
olive might [[motifs/well|well]] have a legend like that of the aspen; as if it
had grown grey with [[concepts/fear|fear]] from the apocalyptic [[concepts/paradox|paradox]] of a divine
vision of [[motifs/death|death]].  A [[archetypes/child|child]] from one of the villages said to me,
in broken English, that it was the place where God said his prayers.
I for one could not ask for a finer or more defiant statement
of all that separates the Christian from the Moslem or the Jew;
<credo quia impossibile>.

Around this terrible spot the Franciscans have done something which will
strike many good and thoughtful people as quite fantastically inadequate;
and which strikes me as fantastically but precisely right.
They have laid out the [[motifs/garden|garden]] simply as a [[motifs/garden|garden]], in a way
that is completely natural because it is completely artificial.
They have made flower-beds in the shape of [[motifs/stars|stars]] and moons,
and coloured them with [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] like those in the backyard of a cottage.
The combination of these bright patterns in the sunshine
with the awful [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] in the centre is certainly an incongruity
in the sense of a contrast.  But it is a poetical contrast,
like that of [[motifs/birds|birds]] building in a temple or [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] growing on a tomb.
The best way of suggesting what I for one feel about it would
be something like this; suppose we imagine a company of children,
such as those whom [[characters/christ|Christ]] blessed in Jerusalem, afterwards put
permanently in charge of a field full of his sorrow; it is probable that,
if they could do anything with it, they would do something like this.
They might cut it up into quaint shapes and dot it with red
daisies or yellow marigolds.  I really do not know that there
is anything better that grown up people could do, since anything
that the greatest of them could do must be, must look quite as small.
"Shall I, the gnat that dances in Thy ray, dare to be reverent?"
The Franciscans have not dared to be reverent; they have only dared
to be cheerful.  It may be too awful an adventure of the [[concepts/imagination|imagination]]
to imagine [[characters/christ|Christ]] in that [[motifs/garden|garden]].  But there is not the smallest
difficulty about imagining St. Francis there; and that is something
to say of an institution which is eight hundred years old.

Immediately above this little [[motifs/garden|garden]], overshadowing and almost
overhanging it, is a gorgeous gilded building with golden domes
and minarets glittering in the sun, and filling a splendid situation
with almost shameless splendour; the Russian church built over
the upper part of the [[motifs/garden|garden]], belonging to the Orthodox-Greeks.
Here again many Western travellers will be troubled; and will think
that golden building much too like a fairy palace in a pantomime.
But here again I shall differ from them, though perhaps less strongly.
It may be that the pleasure is childish rather than childlike;
but I can imagine a [[archetypes/child|child]] clapping his hands at the mere sight
of those great domes like bubbles of gold against the blue sky.
It is a little like Aladdin's Palace, but it has a place in art
as Aladdin has a place in literature; especially since it is
oriental literature.  Those wise missionaries in China who were not
afraid to depict the Twelve Apostles in the costume of Chinamen
might have built such a church in a land of glittering mosques.
And as it is said that the Russian has in him something of the [[archetypes/child|child]]
and something of the oriental, such a style may be quite sincere,
and have even a certain simplicity in its splendour.
It is genuine of its kind; it was built for those who like it;
and those who do not like it can look at something else.  This sort
of thing may be called tawdry, but it is not what I call meretricious.
What I call really meretricious can be found yet higher on the [[motifs/hill|hill]];
towering to the sky and dominating all the valleys.

The [[concepts/nature|nature]] of the difference, I think, is worth noting.
The German Hospice, which served as a sort of palace for the
German Emperor, is a very big building with a very high tower,
planned I believe with great efficiency, solidity and comfort,
and fitted with a thousand things that mark its modernity
compared with the things around, with the quaint [[motifs/garden|garden]]
of the Franciscans or the fantastic temple of the Russians.
It is what I can only describe as a handsome building; rather as
the more vulgar of the Victorian wits used to talk about a fine woman.
By calling it a handsome building I mean that from the top of its dizzy
tower to the [[characters/bottom|bottom]] of its deepest foundations there is not one line
or one tint of [[concepts/beauty|beauty]].  This negative fact, however, would be nothing;
it might be honestly ugly and utilitarian like a factory or a [[motifs/prison|prison]];
but it is not.  It is as pretentious as the gilded dome below it;
and it is pretentious in a wicked way where the other is pretentious
in a good and [[archetypes/innocent|innocent]] way.  What annoys me about it is that it
was not built by children, or even by savages, but by professors;
and the professors could profess the art and could not practice it.
The architects knew everything about a Romanesque building except
how to build it.  We feel that they accumulated on that spot
all the learning and organization and information and wealth of
the world, to do this one particular thing; and then did it wrong.
They did it wrong, not through superstition, not through fanatical
exaggeration, not through provincial ignorance, but through pure,
profound, internal, intellectual incompetence; that intellectual
incompetence which so often goes with intellectual [[concepts/pride|pride]].
I will mention only one matter out of a hundred.  All the columns
in the Kaiser's Chapel are in one way very suitable to their place;
every one of them has a swelled head.  The column itself is slender
but the capital is not only big but bulging; and it has the air
of bulging <downwards>, as if pressing heavily on something too
slender to support it.  This is false, not to any of the particular
schools of architecture about which professors can read in libraries,
but to the inmost instinctive idea of architecture itself.
A Norman capital can be heavy because the Norman column is thick,
and the whole thing expresses an elephantine massiveness and repose.
And a Gothic column can be slender, because its strength is energy;
and is expressed in its line, which shoots upwards like the life of
a tree, like the jet of a fountain or even like the rush of a rocket.
But a slender thing beneath, obviously oppressed by a bloated
thing above, suggests weakness by one of those miraculous mistakes
that are as precisely wrong as masterpieces are precisely right.
And to all this is added the intolerable intuition; that the Russians
and the Franciscans, even if we credit them with fantastic ignorance,
are at least looking up at the sky; and we know how the learned
Germans would look down upon them, from their monstrous tower
upon the [[motifs/hill|hill]].

Di G.K. Chesterton 

Da "The New Jerusalem" 1920

Vi sono tre esempi di opera occidentale sul grande pendio orientale del Monte degli Ulivi; ed essi formano una sorta di triangolo che illustra la [[concepts/truth|verità]] intorno alle diverse influenze dell'Occidente sull'Oriente. Ai piedi del [[motifs/hill|colle]] sta il [[motifs/garden|giardino]] tenuto dai francescani sul presunto sito del Getsemani, e che racchiude il canuto olivo che si suppone sia il terribile albero dell'agonia di [[characters/christ|Cristo]]. Data la grande età e la lenta crescita degli olivi, la tradizione non è tanto irragionevole quanto alcuni potrebbero credere. Ma che sia o no storicamente giusta, essa non è artisticamente sbagliata. L'istinto, se era soltanto un istinto, che spinse gli uomini a fissarsi su questa strana crescita di legno grigio e contorto, era un vero istinto immaginativo. Una delle strane qualità di questo strano albero meridionale è la sua durezza quasi sconcertante; colpire accidentalmente il ramo di un olivo è come colpire la roccia. Con la sua superficie petrosa, la statura stentata, e i suoi strani fori e cavità, esso somiglia spesso più a una grotta che a un albero. Perciò non pare tanto innaturale che venga trattato come una santa grotta; o che questa strana vegetazione pretenda di ergersi per sempre come un monumento scolpito. Persino lo scintillante o tremulo fogliame argenteo dell'olivo vivente potrebbe [[motifs/well|bene]] avere una leggenda come quella del pioppo tremulo; come se fosse ingrigito di [[concepts/fear|paura]] dinanzi all'apocalittico [[concepts/paradox|paradosso]] di una divina visione di [[motifs/death|morte]]. Un [[archetypes/child|bambino]] di uno dei villaggi mi disse, in inglese stentato, che era il luogo dove Dio diceva le sue preghiere. Io per primo non saprei chiedere un'affermazione più bella o più fiera di tutto ciò che separa il cristiano dal musulmano o dall'ebreo; <credo quia impossibile>.

Attorno a questo terribile luogo i francescani hanno fatto qualcosa che colpirà molte persone buone e riflessive come fantasticamente inadeguato; e che colpisce me come fantasticamente ma precisamente giusto. Hanno disposto il [[motifs/garden|giardino]] semplicemente come un [[motifs/garden|giardino]], in un modo che è del tutto naturale perché del tutto artificiale. Hanno fatto aiuole a forma di [[motifs/stars|stelle]] e di lune, e le hanno colorate con [[motifs/flowers|fiori]] come quelli nel cortile di una casetta. La combinazione di questi vividi motivi al sole con la terribile [[archetypes/shadow|ombra]] al centro è certamente un'incongruenza nel senso di un contrasto. Ma è un contrasto poetico, come quello di [[motifs/birds|uccelli]] che nidificano in un tempio o di [[motifs/flowers|fiori]] che crescono su una tomba. Il modo migliore di suggerire ciò che io per primo provo al riguardo sarebbe qualcosa di simile a questo; supponiamo d'immaginare una compagnia di bambini, come quelli che [[characters/christ|Cristo]] benedisse in Gerusalemme, poi posti permanentemente a custodia di un campo colmo del suo dolore; è probabile che, se potessero farne qualcosa, farebbero qualcosa di simile a questo. Potrebbero ritagliarlo in bizzarre forme e punteggiarlo di rosse pratoline o di gialle calendule. Davvero non so se vi sia nulla di meglio che gli adulti potrebbero fare, poiché qualunque cosa il più grande di essi potesse fare dovrebbe, dovrebbe apparire altrettanto piccola. "Oserò io, il moscerino che danza nel Tuo raggio, di essere reverente?" I francescani non hanno osato essere reverenti; hanno osato soltanto essere allegri. Può essere un'avventura troppo tremenda dell'[[concepts/imagination|immaginazione]] immaginare [[characters/christ|Cristo]] in quel [[motifs/garden|giardino]]. Ma non c'è la minima difficoltà a immaginarvi san Francesco; e questo è qualcosa da dire di un'istituzione che ha ottocento anni.

Immediatamente sopra questo piccolo [[motifs/garden|giardino]], che lo sovrasta e quasi lo incombe, sta uno sfarzoso edificio dorato con cupole d'oro e minareti scintillanti al sole, che colma una posizione splendida con quasi spudorato splendore; la chiesa russa costruita sulla parte superiore del [[motifs/garden|giardino]], appartenente ai greco-ortodossi. Anche qui molti viaggiatori occidentali saranno turbati; e penseranno che quell'edificio dorato somigli troppo a un palazzo fatato di una pantomima. Ma anche qui io dissentirò da loro, benché forse meno fermamente. Può darsi che il piacere sia puerile piuttosto che infantile; ma posso immaginare un [[archetypes/child|bambino]] battere le mani alla sola vista di quelle grandi cupole simili a bolle d'oro contro il cielo azzurro. È un po' come il Palazzo di Aladino, ma ha un posto nell'arte come Aladino ha un posto nella letteratura; specialmente poiché si tratta di letteratura orientale. Quei saggi missionari in Cina che non ebbero timore di raffigurare i Dodici Apostoli in costume da cinesi avrebbero potuto costruire una chiesa simile in una terra di scintillanti moschee. E poiché si dice che il russo abbia in sé qualcosa del [[archetypes/child|bambino]] e qualcosa dell'orientale, uno stile così può essere assai sincero, e avere persino una certa semplicità nel suo splendore. È genuino nel suo genere; fu costruito per coloro cui piace; e coloro cui non piace possono guardare qualcos'altro. Questa sorta di cosa può dirsi pacchiana, ma non è ciò che io chiamo meretricio. Ciò che io chiamo veramente meretricio si può trovare ancora più in alto sul [[motifs/hill|colle]]; che torreggia verso il cielo e domina tutte le valli.

La [[concepts/nature|natura]] della differenza, io penso, merita d'essere notata. L'Ospizio Tedesco, che servì da una sorta di palazzo per l'Imperatore tedesco, è un edificio assai grande con una torre assai alta, progettato, credo, con grande efficienza, solidità e comodità, e dotato di mille cose che ne segnano la modernità rispetto alle cose circostanti, rispetto al bizzarro [[motifs/garden|giardino]] dei francescani o al fantastico tempio dei russi. È ciò che posso solo descrivere come un bell'edificio; piuttosto come i più volgari degli spiriti vittoriani solevano parlare di una bella donna. Chiamandolo un bell'edificio intendo che dalla cima della sua vertiginosa torre al [[characters/bottom|fondo]] delle sue più profonde fondamenta non vi è una sola linea o una sola tinta di [[concepts/beauty|bellezza]]. Questo fatto negativo, tuttavia, non sarebbe nulla; potrebbe essere onestamente brutto e utilitaristico come una fabbrica o una [[motifs/prison|prigione]]; ma non lo è. È pretenzioso quanto la cupola dorata al di sotto; ed è pretenzioso in modo malvagio là dove l'altra è pretenziosa in modo buono e [[archetypes/innocent|innocente]]. Ciò che mi irrita in esso è che non fu costruito da bambini, e nemmeno da selvaggi, ma da professori; e i professori potevano professare l'arte ma non praticarla. Gli architetti sapevano ogni cosa su un edificio romanico tranne come costruirlo. Sentiamo che accumularono su quel luogo tutta la dottrina e l'organizzazione e l'informazione e la ricchezza del mondo, per fare questa unica cosa particolare; e poi la fecero male. La fecero male, non per superstizione, non per esagerazione fanatica, non per ignoranza provinciale, ma per pura, profonda, interiore, intellettuale incompetenza; quell'incompetenza intellettuale che tanto spesso va di pari passo con l'[[concepts/pride|orgoglio]] intellettuale. Menzionerò una sola cosa su un centinaio. Tutte le colonne nella Cappella del Kaiser sono per un verso assai adatte al loro posto; ognuna di esse ha la testa gonfia. La colonna in sé è snella ma il capitello non solo è grande bensì rigonfio; e ha l'aria di rigonfiarsi <verso il basso>, come se premesse pesantemente su qualcosa di troppo snello per sostenerlo. Questo è falso, non rispetto ad alcuna delle particolari scuole di architettura di cui i professori possono leggere nelle biblioteche, ma rispetto all'idea più intima e istintiva dell'architettura stessa. Un capitello normanno può essere pesante perché la colonna normanna è spessa, e il tutto esprime una massiccia mole elefantina e riposo. E una colonna gotica può essere snella, perché la sua forza è energia; ed è espressa nella sua linea, che si slancia verso l'alto come la vita di un albero, come il getto di una fontana o persino come lo scatto di un razzo. Ma una cosa snella al di sotto, ovviamente oppressa da una cosa gonfia al di sopra, suggerisce debolezza per uno di quei miracolosi errori che sono precisamente sbagliati come i capolavori sono precisamente giusti. E a tutto ciò si aggiunge l'intollerabile intuizione; che i russi e i francescani, anche se li accreditassimo di fantastica ignoranza, stanno almeno guardando in su verso il cielo; e sappiamo come i dotti tedeschi guarderebbero dall'alto in basso su di loro, dalla loro mostruosa torre sul [[motifs/hill|colle]].

And this is as true of the moral as of the artistic elements
in the modern Jerusalem.  To show that I am not unjustly partisan,
I will say frankly that I see little to complain of in that common
subject of complaint, the mosaic portrait of the Emperor on the ceiling
of the chapel.  It is but one among many figures; and it is not an unknown
practice to include a figure of the founder in such church decorations.
The real example of that startling moral stupidity which marked
the barbaric imperialism can be found in another figure of which,
curiously enough, considerably less notice seems to have been taken.
It is the more remarkable because it is but an artistic [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of
the actual fact; and merely records in outline and relief the temporary
masquerade in which the man walked about in broad daylight.
I mean the really astounding trick of dressing himself up as a Crusader.

That was, under the circumstances, far more ludicrous and lunatic
a proceeding than if he had filled the whole ceiling with cherub
heads with his own features, or festooned all the walls with one
ornamental pattern of his moustaches.

The German Emperor came to Jerusalem under the escort of the Turks,
as the ally of the Turks, and solely because of the victory
and surpremacy of the Turks.  In other words, he came to Jerusalem
solely because the Crusaders had lost Jerusalem; he came there solely
because the Crusaders had been routed, ruined, butchered before
and after the disaster of Hattin; because the [[motifs/cross|Cross]] had gone
down in [[motifs/blood|blood]] before the Crescent, under which alone he could
ride in with safety.  Under those circumstances to dress up
as a Crusader, as if for a fancy dress ball, was a mixture
of [[concepts/madness|madness]] and vulgarity which literally stops the breath.
There is no need whatever to blame him for being in alliance with
the Turks; hundreds of people have been in alliance with the Turks;
the English especially have been far too much in alliance with them.
But if any one wants to appreciate the true difference, distinct from all
the cant of newspaper nationality, between the English and the Germans
(who were classed together by the same newspapers a little [[motifs/time|time]]
before the war) let him take this single incident as a test.
Lord Palmerston, for instance, was a firm friend of the Turks.
Imagine Lord Palmerston appearing in chain mail and the shield
of a Red [[motifs/cross|Cross]] Knight.

It is obvious enough that Palmerston would have said that he cared
no more for the Crusade than for the Siege of Troy; that his diplomacy
was directed by practical patriotic considerations of the moment;
and that he regarded the religious wars of the twelfth century
as a rubbish heap of remote superstitions.  In this he would be
quite wrong, but quite intelligible and quite sincere; an English
aristocrat of the nineteenth century inheriting from the English
aristocrats of the eighteenth century; whose views were simply
those of Voltaire.  And these things are something of an [[forms/allegory|allegory]].
For the Voltairian version of the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]] is still by far
the most reasonable of all merely hostile views of the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]].
If they were not a creative movement of religion, then they were
simply a destructive movement of superstition; and whether we agree
with Voltaire in calling it superstition or with Villehardouin in
calling it religion, at least both these very clear-headed Frenchmen
would agree that the motive did exist and did explain the facts.
But just as there is a clumsy German building with statues that at once
patronise and [[forms/parody|parody]] the Crusaders, so there is a clumsy German theory
that at once patronises and minimises the [[historical-references/crusades|Crusades]].  According to this
theory the essential [[concepts/truth|truth]] about a Crusade was that it was not a Crusade.
It was something that the professors, in the old days before the war,
used to call a Teutonic Folk-Wandering. Godfrey and St. Louis
were not, as Villehardouin would say, fighting for the [[concepts/truth|truth]];
they were not even, as Voltaire would say, fighting for what they
thought was the [[concepts/truth|truth]]; this was only what they thought they thought,
and they were really thinking of something entirely different.
They were not moved either by piety or priestcraft, but by a new
and unexpected nomadism.  They were not inspired either by [[concepts/faith|faith]]
or [[concepts/fanaticism|fanaticism]], but by an unusually aimless taste for foreign travel.
This theory that the war of the two great religions could be
explained by "Wanderlust" was current about twenty years ago among
the historical professors of [[settings/germany|Germany]], and with many of their other
views was often accepted by the historical professors of England.
It was swallowed by an earthquake, along with other rubbish,
in the year 1914.

Since then, so far as I know, the only person who has been patient
enough to dig it up again is Mr. Ezra Pound.  He is [[motifs/well|well]] known
as an American poet; and he is, I believe, a man of great talent
and information.  His attempt to recover the old Teutonic theory
of the Folk-Wandering of Peter the Hermit was expressed, however,
in prose; in an article in the <New Age>. I have no reason to [[concepts/doubt|doubt]]
that he was to be counted among the most loyal of our allies;
but he is evidently one of those who, quite without being Pro-German,
still manage to be German.  The Teutonic theory was very Teutonic;
like the German Hospice on the [[motifs/hill|hill]] it was put together with great care
and knowledge and it is rotten from top to [[characters/bottom|bottom]].  I do not understand,
for that matter, why that alliance which we enjoy with Mr. Pound
should not be treated in the same way as the other historical event;
or why the war should not be an example of the Wanderlust.
Surely the American Army in France must have drifted eastward merely
through the same vague nomadic need as the Christian Army in Palestine.
Surely Pershing as [[motifs/well|well]] as Peter the Hermit was merely a rather restless
gentleman who found his health improved by frequent change of scene.
The Americans said, and perhaps thought, that they were fighting
for [[concepts/democracy|democracy]]; and the Crusaders said, and perhaps thought,
that they were fighting for [[historical-references/christianity|Christianity]].  But as we know what
the Crusaders meant better than they did themselves, I cannot
quite understand why we do not enjoy the same valuable omniscience
about the Americans.  Indeed I do not see why we should not enjoy it
(for it would be very enjoyable) about any individual American.
Surely it was this vague vagabond spirit that moved Mr. Pound,
not only to come to England, but in a fashion to come to Fleet Street.
A. dim tribal tendency, vast and invisible as the [[motifs/wind|wind]], carried him
and his article like an [[motifs/autumn|autumn]] leaf to alight on the <New Age> doorstep.
Or a blind aboriginal impulse, wholly without rational motive,
led him one day to put on his hat, and go out with his article
in an envelope and put it in a pillar-box. It is vain to correct
by cold logic the power of such primitive appetites; [[concepts/nature|nature]] herself
was behind the seemingly random thoughtlessness of the deed.
And now that it is irrevocably done, he can look back on it and trace
the large lines of an awful law of averages; wherein it is ruled
by a ruthless necessity that a certain number of such Americans
should write a certain number of such articles, as the [[motifs/leaves|leaves]] fall
or the [[motifs/flowers|flowers]] return.

E questo è vero degli elementi morali quanto di quelli artistici nella Gerusalemme moderna. Per mostrare che non sono un ingiusto partigiano, dirò francamente che vedo poco da lamentare in quel comune motivo di lamentela, il ritratto a mosaico dell'Imperatore sul soffitto della cappella. È solo una fra molte figure; e non è una pratica ignota includere una figura del fondatore in simili decorazioni ecclesiastiche. Il vero esempio di quella sconcertante stupidità morale che contrassegnò l'imperialismo barbarico si può trovare in un'altra figura di cui, per quanto curioso, sembra essersi presa assai minor nota. È tanto più notevole in quanto è solo un'[[archetypes/shadow|ombra]] artistica del fatto reale; e registra soltanto, in linea e rilievo, la temporanea mascherata in cui quell'uomo se ne andava in giro in pieno giorno. Intendo la trovata davvero sbalorditiva di travestirsi da Crociato.

Questo era, date le circostanze, un procedimento assai più ridicolo e folle che se avesse riempito l'intero soffitto di teste di cherubino con i propri lineamenti, o festonato tutte le pareti con un unico motivo ornamentale dei suoi baffi.

L'Imperatore tedesco venne a Gerusalemme sotto la scorta dei turchi, come alleato dei turchi, e unicamente in virtù della vittoria e della supremazia dei turchi. In altre parole, venne a Gerusalemme unicamente perché i Crociati avevano perduto Gerusalemme; vi venne unicamente perché i Crociati erano stati messi in rotta, rovinati, macellati prima e dopo il disastro di Hattin; perché la [[motifs/cross|Croce]] era andata giù nel [[motifs/blood|sangue]] dinanzi alla Mezzaluna, sotto la quale soltanto egli poteva entrare in sella con sicurezza. In tali circostanze travestirsi da Crociato, come per un ballo in maschera, era una mescolanza di [[concepts/madness|follia]] e di volgarità che letteralmente toglie il fiato. Non v'è alcun bisogno di biasimarlo per essere in alleanza con i turchi; centinaia di persone sono state in alleanza con i turchi; gli inglesi in particolare sono stati fin troppo in alleanza con loro. Ma se qualcuno vuol cogliere la vera differenza, distinta da tutta la retorica giornalistica delle nazionalità, tra gli inglesi e i tedeschi (che erano accomunati dagli stessi giornali un [[motifs/time|poco tempo]] prima della guerra), prenda questo singolo episodio come banco di prova. Lord Palmerston, per esempio, era un saldo amico dei turchi. Si immagini Lord Palmerston comparire in cotta di maglia e con lo scudo di un Cavaliere della [[motifs/cross|Croce]] Rossa.

È abbastanza ovvio che Palmerston avrebbe detto che della Crociata gli importava non più che dell'assedio di Troia; che la sua diplomazia era guidata da pratiche considerazioni patriottiche del momento; e che considerava le guerre religiose del dodicesimo secolo come un cumulo di rifiuti di remote superstizioni. In ciò avrebbe avuto del tutto torto, ma sarebbe stato del tutto comprensibile e del tutto sincero; un aristocratico inglese del diciannovesimo secolo erede degli aristocratici inglesi del diciottesimo secolo; le cui vedute erano semplicemente quelle di Voltaire. E queste cose hanno qualcosa di un'[[forms/allegory|allegoria]]. Poiché la versione voltairiana delle [[historical-references/crusades|Crociate]] è tuttora di gran lunga la più ragionevole di tutte le vedute meramente ostili sulle [[historical-references/crusades|Crociate]]. Se esse non furono un movimento creativo di religione, allora furono semplicemente un movimento distruttivo di superstizione; e sia che concordiamo con Voltaire nel chiamarla superstizione, sia con Villehardouin nel chiamarla religione, almeno entrambi questi lucidissimi francesi concorderebbero che il movente esistette davvero e davvero spiegava i fatti. Ma proprio come vi è un goffo edificio tedesco con statue che a un tempo omaggiano e [[forms/parody|parodiano]] i Crociati, così vi è una goffa teoria tedesca che a un tempo omaggia e minimizza le [[historical-references/crusades|Crociate]]. Secondo questa teoria la [[concepts/truth|verità]] essenziale su una Crociata era che non era una Crociata. Era qualcosa che i professori, ai vecchi tempi prima della guerra, solevano chiamare una Migrazione di Popoli teutonica. Goffredo e san Luigi non stavano, come direbbe Villehardouin, combattendo per la [[concepts/truth|verità]]; non stavano nemmeno, come direbbe Voltaire, combattendo per ciò che ritenevano fosse la [[concepts/truth|verità]]; questo era solo ciò che pensavano di pensare, mentre in realtà stavano pensando a tutt'altro. Non erano mossi né da pietà né da inganno sacerdotale, ma da un nuovo e inatteso nomadismo. Non erano ispirati né da [[concepts/faith|fede]] né da [[concepts/fanaticism|fanatismo]], ma da un gusto insolitamente senza scopo per i viaggi all'estero. Questa teoria, secondo cui la guerra delle due grandi religioni potesse spiegarsi con la "Wanderlust", era corrente circa vent'anni fa tra i professori di storia della [[settings/germany|Germania]], e con molte delle loro altre vedute veniva spesso accolta dai professori di storia dell'Inghilterra. Fu inghiottita da un terremoto, insieme ad altri rifiuti, nell'anno 1914.

Da allora, per quanto ne so, l'unica persona abbastanza paziente da dissotterrarla di nuovo è il signor Ezra Pound. Egli è [[motifs/well|ben]] noto come poeta americano; ed è, credo, un uomo di grande talento e sapere. Il suo tentativo di recuperare la vecchia teoria teutonica della Migrazione di Popoli di Pietro l'Eremita fu espresso, tuttavia, in prosa; in un articolo sul <New Age>. Non ho alcun motivo di [[concepts/doubt|dubitare]] ch'egli fosse da annoverare tra i più leali dei nostri alleati; ma è evidentemente uno di quelli che, pur senza essere affatto filotedeschi, riescono comunque a essere tedeschi. La teoria teutonica era assai teutonica; come l'Ospizio Tedesco sul [[motifs/hill|colle]] fu messa insieme con grande cura e sapere ed è marcia da cima a [[characters/bottom|fondo]]. Non capisco, del resto, perché quell'alleanza di cui godiamo con il signor Pound non debba essere trattata allo stesso modo dell'altro evento storico; o perché la guerra non debba essere un esempio della Wanderlust. Di certo l'esercito americano in Francia dev'essere andato alla deriva verso oriente semplicemente per lo stesso vago bisogno nomade dell'esercito cristiano in Palestina. Di certo Pershing non meno di [[motifs/well|quanto]] Pietro l'Eremita era semplicemente un gentiluomo piuttosto irrequieto che trovava giovamento alla salute nel frequente mutamento di scenario. Gli americani dicevano, e forse pensavano, di combattere per la [[concepts/democracy|democrazia]]; e i Crociati dicevano, e forse pensavano, di combattere per il [[historical-references/christianity|cristianesimo]]. Ma poiché noi sappiamo ciò che intendevano i Crociati meglio di loro stessi, non riesco proprio a capire perché non godiamo della medesima preziosa onniscienza riguardo agli americani. Anzi, non vedo perché non dovremmo goderne (poiché sarebbe assai piacevole) riguardo a qualsiasi singolo americano. Di certo fu questo vago spirito vagabondo che mosse il signor Pound non solo a venire in Inghilterra, ma in un certo modo a venire in Fleet Street. Una fosca tendenza tribale, vasta e invisibile come il [[motifs/wind|vento]], portò lui e il suo articolo come una foglia d'[[motifs/autumn|autunno]] a posarsi sulla soglia del <New Age>. Oppure un cieco impulso aborigeno, del tutto privo di movente razionale, lo indusse un giorno a mettersi il cappello e a uscire col suo articolo in una busta e a imbucarlo in una cassetta postale. È vano correggere con la fredda logica la potenza di simili appetiti primitivi; la [[concepts/nature|natura]] stessa stava dietro la sconsideratezza apparentemente casuale del gesto. E ora che esso è irrevocabilmente compiuto, egli può volgersi indietro a osservarlo e tracciare le grandi linee di un'atroce legge delle medie; per cui è stabilito da una spietata necessità che un certo numero di tali americani scriva un certo numero di tali articoli, come cadono le [[motifs/leaves|foglie]] o ritornano i [[motifs/flowers|fiori]].

In plain words, this sort of theory is a blasphemy against
the intellectual dignity of man.  It is a blunder as [[motifs/well|well]] as
a blasphemy; for it goes miles out of its way to find a bestial
explanation when there is obviously a human explanation.
It is as if a man told me that a dim [[concepts/survival|survival]] of the instincts of a
quadruped was the reason of my sitting on a chair with four legs.
I answer that I do it because I foresee that there may be [[motifs/grave|grave]]
disadvantages in sitting on a chair with one leg.  Or it is as if I
were told that I liked to swim in the sea, solely because some early
forms of amphibian life came out of the sea on to the shore.
I answer that I know why I swim in the sea; and it is because
the divine gift of reason tells me that it would be unsatisfactory
to swim on the land.  In short this sort of vague evolutionary
theorising simply amounts to finding an unconvincing explanation
of something that needs no explanation.  And the case is really quite
as simple with great political and religious movements by which man
has from [[motifs/time|time]] to [[motifs/time|time]] changed the world in this or that respect
in which he happened to think it would be the better for a change.
The Crusade was a religious movement, but it was also a perfectly
rational movement; one might almost say a rationalist movement.
I could quite understand Mr. Pound saying that such a campaign for
a creed was immoral; and indeed it often has been, and now perhaps
generally is, quite horribly immoral.  But when he implies that it
is irrational he has selected exactly the thing which it is not.
It is not enlightenment, on the contrary it is ignorance and insularity,
which causes most of us to miss this fact.  But it certainly is the fact
that religious war is in itself much more rational than patriotic way.
I for one have often defended and even encouraged patriotic war,
and should always be ready to defend and encourage patriotic passion.
But it cannot be denied that there is more of mere passion,
of mere preference and prejudice, in short of mere personal accident,
in fighting another nation than in fighting another [[concepts/faith|faith]].
The Crusader is in every sense more rational than the modern
conscript or professional soldier.  He is more rational in
his object, which is the intelligent and intelligible object
of [[concepts/conversion|conversion]]; where the modern militarist has an object much
more confused by momentary [[concepts/vanity|vanity]] and one-sided satisfaction.
The Crusader wished to make Jerusalem a Christian town;
but the Englishman does not wish to make Berlin an English town.
He has only a healthy hatred of it as a Prussian town.
The Moslem wished to make the Christian a Moslem; but even
the Prussian did not wish to make the Frenchman a Prussian.
He only wished to make the Frenchman admire a Prussian;
and not only were the means he adopted somewhat ill-considered for
this purpose, but the purpose itself is looser and more irrational.
The object of all war is peace; but the object of religious
war is mental as [[motifs/well|well]] as material peace; it is agreement.
In short religious war aims ultimately at equality, where national
war aims relatively at superiority.  [[concepts/conversion|Conversion]] is the one sort
of conquest in which the conquered must rejoice.

In that sense alone it is foolish for us in the West to sneer
at those who kill men when a foot is set in a holy place,
when we ourselves kill hundreds of thousands when a foot is put
across a frontier.  It is absurd for us to despise those who shed
[[motifs/blood|blood]] for a relic when we have shed rivers of [[motifs/blood|blood]] for a rag.
But above all the Crusade, or, for that matter, the Jehad,
is by far the most philosophical sort of fighting, not only
in its conception of ending the difference, but in its mere act
of recognising the difference, as the deepest kind of difference.
It is to reverse all reason to suggest that a man's politics matter
and his religion does not matter.  It is to say he is affected
by the town he lives in, but not by the world he lives in.
It is to say that he is altered when he is a fellow-citizen walking
under new lamp-posts, but not altered when he is another creature walking
under strange [[motifs/stars|stars]].  It is exactly as if we were to say that two people
ought to live in the same house, but it need not be in the same town.
It is exactly as if we said that so long as the address included
York it did not matter whether it was [[settings/new-york|New York]]; or that so long
as a man is in Essex we do not care whether he is in England.

Christendom would have been entirely justified in the abstract
in being alarmed or suspicious at the mere rise of a great power
that was not Christian.  Nobody nowadays would think it odd
to express regret at the rise of a power because it was Militarist
or Socialist or even Protectionist.  But it is far more natural
to be conscious of a difference, not about the order of battle but
the battle of life; not about our definable enjoyment of possessions,
but about our much more doubtful possession of enjoyment;
not about the fiscal divisions between us and foreigners
but about the spiritual divisions even between us and friends.
These are the things that differ profoundly with differing views
of the ultimate [[concepts/nature|nature]] of the universe.  For the things of our country
are often distant; but the things of our cosmos are always near;
we can shut our doors upon the wheeled traffic of our native town;
but in our own inmost chamber we hear the sound that never ceases;
that [[motifs/wheel|wheel]] which [[historical-references/dante|Dante]] and a popular proverb have dared
to christen as the [[motifs/love|love]] that makes the world go round.
For this is the great [[concepts/paradox|paradox]] of life; that there are not only
wheels within wheels, but the larger wheels within the smaller.
When a whole community rests on one conception of life and [[motifs/death|death]]
and the origin of things, it is quite entitled to watch the rise
of another community founded on another conception as the rise
of something certain to be different and likely to be hostile.
Indeed, as I have pointed out touching certain political theories,
we already admit this [[concepts/truth|truth]] in its small and questionable examples.
We only deny the large and obvious examples.


<span class="qlang-split" data-lang="it"></span>

In parole povere, questa sorta di teoria è una bestemmia contro la dignità intellettuale dell'uomo. È un errore oltre che [[motifs/well|una]] bestemmia; poiché fa miglia di strada fuori dal suo cammino per trovare una spiegazione bestiale là dove vi è ovviamente una spiegazione umana. È come se un uomo mi dicesse che una fosca [[concepts/survival|sopravvivenza]] degli istinti di un quadrupede fosse la ragione del mio sedermi su una sedia a quattro gambe. Io rispondo che lo faccio perché prevedo che possano esservi [[motifs/grave|gravi]] svantaggi nel sedersi su una sedia a una gamba sola. Oppure è come se mi si dicesse che mi piace nuotare nel mare, unicamente perché qualche forma primitiva di vita anfibia uscì dal mare sulla riva. Io rispondo che so perché nuoto nel mare; ed è perché il divino dono della ragione mi dice che sarebbe poco soddisfacente nuotare sulla terraferma. In breve, questa sorta di vago teorizzare evoluzionistico equivale semplicemente a trovare una spiegazione poco convincente di qualcosa che non ha bisogno di spiegazione. E il caso è davvero altrettanto semplice per i grandi movimenti politici e religiosi con cui l'uomo ha di [[motifs/time|tempo]] in [[motifs/time|tempo]] mutato il mondo in questo o quel rispetto in cui gli accadeva di pensare che sarebbe stato meglio con un cambiamento. La Crociata fu un movimento religioso, ma fu anche un movimento perfettamente razionale; si potrebbe quasi dire un movimento razionalista. Potrei benissimo capire il signor Pound se dicesse che una tale campagna per un credo fosse immorale; e in effetti spesso lo è stata, e ora forse generalmente lo è, in modo assai orribilmente immorale. Ma quando egli insinua che sia irrazionale ha scelto esattamente la cosa che essa non è. Non è l'illuminazione, al contrario è l'ignoranza e l'insularità, a far sì che i più di noi manchino di cogliere questo fatto. Ma è certamente il fatto che la guerra religiosa è in sé assai più razionale della guerra patriottica. Io per primo ho spesso difeso e persino incoraggiato la guerra patriottica, e sarei sempre pronto a difendere e incoraggiare la passione patriottica. Ma non si può negare che vi sia più di mera passione, di mera preferenza e pregiudizio, in breve di mero accidente personale, nel combattere un'altra nazione che nel combattere un'altra [[concepts/faith|fede]]. Il Crociato è in ogni senso più razionale del moderno coscritto o soldato professionale. È più razionale nel suo obiettivo, che è l'intelligente e intelligibile obiettivo della [[concepts/conversion|conversione]]; laddove il moderno militarista ha un obiettivo assai più confuso da momentanea [[concepts/vanity|vanità]] e da soddisfazione unilaterale. Il Crociato desiderava fare di Gerusalemme una città cristiana; ma l'inglese non desidera fare di Berlino una città inglese. Ne prova soltanto un sano odio in quanto città prussiana. Il musulmano desiderava fare del cristiano un musulmano; ma persino il prussiano non desiderava fare del francese un prussiano. Desiderava soltanto fare in modo che il francese ammirasse un prussiano; e non solo i mezzi che adottò furono alquanto mal ponderati per questo scopo, ma lo scopo stesso è più vago e più irrazionale. L'obiettivo di ogni guerra è la pace; ma l'obiettivo della guerra religiosa è la pace mentale oltre [[motifs/well|che]] materiale; è l'accordo. In breve, la guerra religiosa mira in ultimo all'uguaglianza, là dove la guerra nazionale mira relativamente alla superiorità. La [[concepts/conversion|conversione]] è l'unica sorta di conquista in cui il conquistato deve rallegrarsi.

In quel senso soltanto è sciocco per noi in Occidente schernire coloro che uccidono uomini quando un piede è posto in un luogo santo, mentre noi stessi ne uccidiamo centinaia di migliaia quando un piede è posto oltre una frontiera. È assurdo per noi disprezzare coloro che versano [[motifs/blood|sangue]] per una reliquia quando noi abbiamo versato fiumi di [[motifs/blood|sangue]] per uno straccio. Ma soprattutto la Crociata, o, se è per questo, la Jehad, è di gran lunga la più filosofica specie di combattimento, non solo nella sua concezione di porre fine alla differenza, ma nel suo mero atto di riconoscere la differenza, come la più profonda specie di differenza. È rovesciare ogni ragione suggerire che la politica di un uomo conti e la sua religione non conti. È dire che egli è influenzato dalla città in cui vive, ma non dal mondo in cui vive. È dire che egli è mutato quando è un concittadino che cammina sotto nuovi lampioni, ma non mutato quando è un'altra creatura che cammina sotto strane [[motifs/stars|stelle]]. È esattamente come se dicessimo che due persone devono vivere nella stessa casa, ma non è necessario che sia nella stessa città. È esattamente come se dicessimo che, purché l'indirizzo includa York, non importa se si tratta di [[settings/new-york|New York]]; o che, purché un uomo sia nell'Essex, non c'importa se sia in Inghilterra.

La cristianità sarebbe stata del tutto giustificata in astratto nell'allarmarsi o insospettirsi al solo sorgere di una grande potenza che non fosse cristiana. Nessuno oggigiorno troverebbe strano esprimere rammarico per il sorgere di una potenza perché militarista o socialista o persino protezionista. Ma è assai più naturale essere consapevoli di una differenza, non riguardo all'ordine di battaglia bensì alla battaglia della vita; non riguardo al nostro definibile godimento dei possessi, ma al nostro assai più dubbio possesso del godimento; non riguardo alle divisioni fiscali tra noi e gli stranieri, ma alle divisioni spirituali persino tra noi e gli amici. Queste sono le cose che differiscono profondamente col differire delle vedute sull'ultima [[concepts/nature|natura]] dell'universo. Poiché le cose della nostra patria sono spesso lontane; ma le cose del nostro cosmo sono sempre vicine; possiamo chiudere le nostre porte al traffico su ruote della nostra città natale; ma nella nostra più intima stanza udiamo il suono che mai cessa; quella [[motifs/wheel|ruota]] che [[historical-references/dante|Dante]] e un proverbio popolare hanno osato battezzare come l'[[motifs/love|amor]] che move il sole e l'altre stelle. Poiché questo è il grande [[concepts/paradox|paradosso]] della vita; che non vi sono solo ruote dentro le ruote, ma le ruote più grandi dentro quelle più piccole. Quando un'intera comunità si fonda su una sola concezione della vita e della [[motifs/death|morte]] e dell'origine delle cose, essa è pienamente autorizzata a osservare il sorgere di un'altra comunità fondata su un'altra concezione come il sorgere di qualcosa di certamente diverso e verosimilmente ostile. Anzi, come ho fatto notare a proposito di certe teorie politiche, noi già ammettiamo questa [[concepts/truth|verità]] nei suoi esempi piccoli e discutibili. Neghiamo soltanto gli esempi grandi e ovvi.



<span class="atom-split" data-atom="part--part_04" data-title="meaning of the crusade (part 4)" data-chapter="part 04" data-kind="excerpt" data-tags="motif/animals,motif/bees,motif/birds,motif/cross,motif/hill,motif/insects,concept/democracy,concept/history,concept/self,concept/truth,form/fragment,form/history,histref/america,histref/french_revolution,setting/america,setting/paris"></span>


Christendom might quite reasonably have been alarmed if it had not
been attacked.  But as a matter of [[concepts/history|history]] it had been attacked.
The Crusader would have been quite justified in suspecting the Moslem
even if the Moslem had merely been a new stranger; but as a matter
of [[concepts/history|history]] he was already an old enemy.  The critic of the Crusade
talks as if it had sought out some inoffensive tribe or temple in the
interior of Thibet, which was never discovered until it was invaded.
They seem entirely to forget that long before the Crusaders had dreamed
of riding to Jerusalem, the Moslems had almost ridden into [[settings/paris|Paris]].
They seem to forget that if the Crusaders nearly conquered Palestine,
it was but a return upon the Moslems who had nearly conquered Europe.
There was no need for them to argue by an appeal to reason, as I
have argued above, that a religious division must make a difference;
it had already made a difference.  The difference stared them
in the face in the startling transformation of Roman Barbary
and of Roman Spain.  In short it was something which must happen
in theory and which did happen in practice; all expectation
suggested that it would be so and all experience said it was so.
Having thought it out theoretically and experienced it practically,
they proceeded to deal with it equally practically.  The first division
involved every principle of the science of thought; and the last
developments followed out every principle of the science of war.
The Crusade was the counter-attack. It was the defensive army taking
the offensive in its turn, and driving back the enemy to his base.
And it is this process, reasonable from its first axiom to its last act,
that Mr. Pound actually selects as a sort of automatic wandering
of an animal.  But a man so intelligent would not have made a mistake
so extraordinary but for another error which it is here very essential
to consider.  To suggest that men engaged, rightly or wrongly,
in so logical a military and political operation were only migrating
like [[motifs/birds|birds]] or swarming like [[motifs/bees|bees]] is as ridiculous as to say that
the Prohibition campaign in [[settings/america|America]] was only an animal reversion
towards lapping as the dog lappeth, or Rowland [[motifs/hill|Hill]]'s introduction
of postage stamps an animal taste for licking as the cat licks.
Why should we provide other people with a remote reason for their
own actions, when they themselves are ready to tell us the reason,
and it is a perfectly reasonable reason?

I have compared this pompous imposture of scientific [[concepts/history|history]] to
the pompous and clumsy building of the scientific Germans on the Mount
of Olives, because it substitutes in the same way a modern stupidity
for the medieval simplicity.  But just as the German Hospice after
all stands on a fine site, and might have been a fine building,
so there is after all another [[concepts/truth|truth]], somewhat analogous,
which the German historians of the Folk-Wanderings might possibly
have meant, as distinct from all that they have actually said.
There is indeed one respect in which the case of the Crusade does
differ very much from modern political cases like prohibition
or the penny post.  I do not refer to such incidental peculiarities
as the fact that Prohibition could only have succeeded through
the enormous power of modern plutocracy, or that even the convenience
of the postage goes along with an extreme coercion by the police.
It is a somewhat deeper difference that I mean; and it may possibly be
what these critics mean.  But the difference is not in the evolutionary,
but rather the revolutionary spirit.

The First Crusade was not a racial migration; it was something much
more intellectual and dignified; a riot.  In order to understand this
religious war we must class it, not so much with the wars of, [[concepts/history|history]] as
with the revolutions of [[concepts/history|history]].  As I shall try to show briefly on
a later page, it not only had all the peculiar good and the peculiar
evil of things like the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]] or the Russian Revolution,
but it was a more purely popular revolution than either of them.
The truly modern mind will of course regard the contention that it
was popular as tantamount to a confession that it was animal.
In these days when papers and speeches are full of words like
[[concepts/democracy|democracy]] and [[concepts/self|self]]-determination, anything really resembling
the movement of a mass of angry men Is regarded as no better than
a stampede of bulls or a scurry of rats.  The new sociologists
call it the herd instinct, just as the old reactionaries called it
the many-headed beast.  But both agree in implying that it is hardly
worth while to count how many head there are of such cattle.
In face of such fashionable comparisons it will seem comparatively
mild to talk of migration as it occurs among [[motifs/birds|birds]] or [[motifs/insects|insects]].
Nevertheless we may venture to state with some confidence
that both the sociologists and the reactionaries are wrong.
It does not follow that human beings become less than human because their
ideas appeal to more and more of humanity.  Nor can we deduce that men
are mindless solely from the fact that they are all of one mind.
In plain fact the virtues of a mob cannot be found in a herd
of bulls or a pack of wolves, any more than the crimes of a mob
can be committed by a flock of sheep or a shoal of herrings.
[[motifs/birds|Birds]] have never been known to besiege and capture an empty cage
of an aviary, on a point of principle, merely because it had kept a few
other [[motifs/birds|birds]] in captivity, as the mob besieged and captured the almost
empty Bastille, merely because it was the fortress of a historic tyranny.
And rats have never been known to die by thousands merely in order
to visit a particular trap in which a particular rat had perished,
as the poor peasants of the First Crusade died in thousands for a
far-off sight of the Sepulchre or a [[forms/fragment|fragment]] of the true [[motifs/cross|cross]].
In this sense indeed the Crusade was not rationalistic, if the rat
is the only rationalist.  But it will seem more truly rational
to point out that the inspiration of such a crowd is not in such
instincts as we share with the [[motifs/animals|animals]], but precisely in such ideas
as the [[motifs/animals|animals]] never (with all their virtues) understand.


<span class="qlang-split" data-lang="it"></span>

La Cristianità avrebbe potuto con ragione allarmarsi se non fosse stata attaccata. Ma come fatto di [[concepts/history|storia]] era già stata attaccata.
Il crociato sarebbe stato pienamente giustificato nel sospettare il mussulmano anche se questi fosse stato semplicemente uno straniero nuovo; ma come fatto di [[concepts/history|storia]] egli era già un vecchio nemico. Il critico della Crociata parla come se questa avesse cercato fuori qualche tribù o tempio inoffensivo nell'interno del Tibet, che non venne mai scoperto finché non fu invaso.
Sembra dimentichino completamente che molto prima che i crociati avessero sognato di cavalcare fino a Gerusalemme, i mussulmani erano quasi arrivati a [[settings/paris|Parigi]].
Sembrano dimenticare che se i crociati conquistarono quasi la Palestina, fu solo un ritorno sui mussulmani che avevano quasi conquistato l'Europa.
Non c'era bisogno che argomentassero facendo appello alla ragione, come ho fatto io sopra, che una divisione religiosa doveva fare la differenza; essa aveva già fatto la differenza. La differenza li fissava in volto nella sorprendente trasformazione della Barberia romana e della Spagna romana.
In breve era qualcosa che doveva succedere in teoria e che è successo in pratica; ogni aspettativa suggeriva che sarebbe stato così ed ogni esperienza diceva che lo fosse. Avendo pensato la cosa teoricamente ed essendosene fatta l'esperienza praticamente, procedettero a trattarla altrettanto praticamente.
La prima divisione coinvolgeva ogni principio della scienza del pensiero; e gli ultimi sviluppi seguivano ogni principio della scienza della guerra. La Crociata fu il contrattacco. Fu l'esercito difensivo che prendeva l'offensiva a sua volta, respingendo l'invasore alla sua base.
Ed è questo processo, ragionevole dal suo primo assioma al suo ultimo atto, che Mr. Pound in realtà sceglie come una specie di vagabondaggio automatico di un animale. Ma un uomo così intelligente non avrebbe fatto uno sbaglio così straordinario se non fosse stato per un altro errore che qui è molto essenziale considerare.
Suggerire che uomini impegnati, giustamente o ingiustamente, in una operazione militare e politica tanto logica stessero solo migrando come [[motifs/birds|uccelli]] o formando uno sciame come [[motifs/bees|api]] è altrettanto ridicolo che dire
che la campagna del Proibizionismo in [[settings/america|America]] fosse stata solo una regressione animale verso il leccarsi come fa un cane, o l'introduzione dei francobolli da Rowland [[motifs/hill|Hill]] un gusto animale per il leccarsi come fa un gatto.
Perché dovremmo fornire agli altri una ragione remota delle loro azioni quando essi stessi sono pronti a dircene la ragione, e questa è una ragione perfettamente ragionevole?

Ho paragonato questa pomposa impostura della [[concepts/history|storia]] scientifica al pomposo e goffo edificio dei tedeschi scientifici sul Monte degli Ulivi, perché sostituisce allo stesso modo una stupidità moderna alla semplicità medievale. Ma proprio come l'Ospizio Tedesco in fin dei conti si trova su un sito magnifico e avrebbe potuto essere un edificio magnifico, così c'è pure un'altra [[concepts/truth|verità]], abbastanza analoga, che gli storici tedeschi delle Migrationsvölker forse intendevano dire, a parte tutto quello che hanno effettivamente detto. C'è infatti un aspetto in cui il caso della Crociata si differenzia molto dai casi politici moderni come la proibizione o l'affrancatura postale di un penny. Non mi riferisco a peculiarità occasionali, come il fatto che la Proibizione avrebbe potuto avere successo solo grazie all'enorme potere della plutocrazia moderna, o che anche la comodità del servizio postale va di pari passo con una coercizione estrema da parte della polizia. È una differenza un po' più profonda quella a cui mi riferisco; e forse è quello che intendono questi critici. Ma la differenza non sta nello spirito evolutivo, bensì piuttosto in quello rivoluzionario.

La Prima Crociata non fu una migrazione razziale; fu qualcosa di molto più intellettuale e dignitoso: un tumulto. Per comprendere questa guerra religiosa dobbiamo classificarla, non tanto con le guerre della [[concepts/history|storia]] quanto con le rivoluzioni della [[concepts/history|storia]]. Come cercherò di dimostrare brevemente in una pagina successiva, essa aveva non solo tutto il bene peculiare e il male peculiare di eventi come la [[historical-references/french-revolution|Rivoluzione Francese]] o la Rivoluzione Russa, ma era una rivoluzione popolare più puramente popolare di entrambe.
La mente veramente moderna considererà naturalmente l'affermazione che fosse popolare come equivalente a una confessione che fosse animalesca. In questi giorni in cui i giornali e i discorsi sono pieni di parole come [[concepts/democracy|democrazia]] e [[concepts/self|autodeterminazione]], qualsiasi cosa assomigli veramente al movimento di una massa di uomini arrabbiati è considerata non migliore di una fuga di tori o di un'incursione di ratti. I nuovi sociologi la chiamano istinto gregario, proprio come i vecchi reazionari la chiamavano bestia dalle molte teste. Ma entrambi concordano nell'implicare che non vale quasi la pena contare quante teste ci sono di tali bestie.
Di fronte a simili paragoni alla moda sembrerà relativamente mite parlare di migrazione come avviene tra [[motifs/birds|uccelli]] o [[motifs/insects|insetti]]. Tuttavia possiamo osare di affermare con una certa sicurezza che sia i sociologi che i reazionari hanno torto. Non segue che gli esseri umani diventino meno che umani perché le loro idee fanno appello sempre più all'umanità. E neppure possiamo dedurre che gli uomini siano privi di mente solo dal fatto che sono tutti della stessa opinione.
In chiaro, le virtù di una folla non possono essere trovate in un branco di tori o in un pacchetto di lupi, tanto quanto i crimini di una folla non possono essere commessi da uno stormo di pecore o da uno scalo di aringhe. Gli [[motifs/birds|uccelli]] non sono mai stati noti per aver assediato e catturato una gabbia vuota di un voliera, su un principio, solo perché aveva tenuto in cattività alcuni altri [[motifs/birds|uccelli]], come la folla ha assediato e catturato la quasi vuota Bastiglia, solo perché era la fortezza di una tirannia storica. E i ratti non sono mai stati noti per essere morti a migliaia solo per visitare una particolare trappola in cui un particolare ratto era perito, come i poveri contadini della Prima Crociata morirono a migliaia per uno sguardo lontano al Sepolcro o per un [[forms/fragment|frammento]] della vera [[motifs/cross|croce]]. In questo senso infatti la Crociata non era razionalistica, se il ratto è l'unico razionalista. Ma sembrerà più veramente razionale sottolineare che l'ispirazione di una tale folla non sta in tali istinti che condividiamo con gli [[motifs/animals|animali]], ma precisamente in idee come quelle che gli [[motifs/animals|animali]] non capiscono mai (con tutte le loro virtù).



<span class="atom-split" data-atom="part--part_05" data-title="meaning of the crusade (part 5)" data-chapter="part 05" data-kind="excerpt" data-tags="archetype/hero,motif/city,motif/crown,motif/flag,motif/time,concept/conscience,concept/conversion,concept/democracy,concept/history,concept/honour,concept/truth,form/fable,form/history,histref/french_revolution,character/aquinas,character/christ"></span>


What is peculiar about the First Crusade is that it was in quite
a new and abnormal sense a popular movement.  I might almost say
it was the only popular movement there ever was in the world.
For it was not a thing which the populace followed; it was actually
a thing which the populace led.  It was not only essentially
a revolution, but it was the only revolution I know of in which
the masses began by acting alone, and practically without any
support from any of the classes.  When they had acted, the classes
came in; and it is perfectly true, and indeed only natural,
that the masses alone failed where the two together succeeded.
But it was the uneducated who educated the educated.
The case of the Crusade is emphatically not a case in which certain
ideas were first suggested by a few philosophers, and then preached
by demagogues to the [[concepts/democracy|democracy]].  This was to a great extent true
of the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]]; it was probably yet more true of the
Russian Revolution; and we need not here pause upon the fine shade
of difference that Rousseau was right and Karl Marx was wrong.
In the First Crusade it was the ordinary man who was right or wrong.
He came out in a fury at the insult to his own little images or
private prayers, as if he had come out to fight with his own domestic
poker or private carving-knife. He was not armed with new weapons
of wit and logic served round from the arsenal of an academy.
There was any amount of wit and logic in the academies of the Middle Ages;
but the typical leader of the Crusade was not Abélard or [[characters/aquinas|Aquinas]]
but Peter the Hermit, who can hardly be called even a popular leader,
but rather a popular [[motifs/flag|flag]].  And it was his army, or rather
his enormous rabble, that first marched across the world to die
for the deliverance of Jerusalem.

Historians say that in that huge host of thousands there were only
nine knights.  To any one who knows even a little of medieval
war the fact seems astounding.  It is indeed a long exploded
fallacy to regard medievalism as identical with feudalism.
There were countless democratic institutions, such as the guilds;
sometimes as many as twenty guilds in one small town.
But it is really true that the military organization of the Middle Ages
was almost entirely feudal; indeed we might rather say that feudalism
was the name of their military organization.  That so vast a military
mass should have attempted to move at all, with only nine of the natural
military leaders, seems to me a prodigy of popular initiative.
It is as if a parliament were elected at the next general election,
in which only two men could afford to read a daily newspaper.

This mob marched against the military discipline of the Moslems
and was massacred; or, might I so mystically express it, martyred.
Many of the great kings and knights who followed in their tracks
did not so clearly deserve any haloes for the simplicity and purity
of their motives.  The canonization of such a crowd might be impossible,
and would certainly be resisted in modern opinion; chiefly because they
indulged their democratic violence on the way by killing various usurers;
a course which naturally fills modern society with an anger verging
on alarm.  A perversity leads me to weep rather more over the many
slaughtered peasants than over the few slaughtered usurers;
but in any case the peasants certainly were not slaughtered in vain.
The common [[concepts/conscience|conscience]] of all classes, in a [[motifs/time|time]] when all had
a common creed, was aroused, and a new army followed of a very
different type of skill and training; led by most of the ablest
captains and by some of the most chivalrous gentlemen of the age.
For curiously enough, the host contained more than one cultured
gentleman who was as simple a Christian as any peasant,
and as recklessly ready to be butchered or tortured for the mere
name of [[characters/christ|Christ]].

It is a tag of the materialists that the [[concepts/truth|truth]] about [[concepts/history|history]]
rubs away the romance of [[concepts/history|history]].  It is dear to the modern mind
because it is depressing; but it does not happen to be true.
Nothing emerges more clearly from a study that is truly realistic,
than the curious fact that romantic people were really romantic.
It is rather the historical novels that will lead a modern
man vaguely to expect to find the leader of the new knights,
Godfrey de Bouillon, to have been merely a brutal baron.
The historical facts are all in favour of his having been much
more like a knight of the Round Table.  In fact he was a far
better man than most of the knights of the Round Table, in whose
characters the fabulist, knowing that he was writing a [[forms/fable|fable]],
was tactful enough to introduce a larger admixture of vice.  [[concepts/truth|Truth]] is
not only stranger than fiction, but often saintlier than fiction.
For [[concepts/truth|truth]] is real, while fiction is bound to be realistic.
Curiously enough Godfrey seems to have been heroic even in those
admirable accidents which are generally and perhaps rightly regarded
as the trappings of fiction.  Thus he was of heroic stature,
a handsome red-bearded man of great personal strength and daring;
and he was himself the first man over the wall of Jerusalem,
like any boy [[archetypes/hero|hero]] in a boy's adventure story.  But he was also,
the realist will be surprised to hear, a perfectly honest man,
and a perfectly genuine practicer of the theoretical magnanimity
of knighthood.  Everything about him suggests it; from his first
[[concepts/conversion|conversion]] from the imperial to the papal (and popular) cause, to his
great refusal of the kinghood of the [[motifs/city|city]] he had taken; "I will
not wear a [[motifs/crown|crown]] of gold where my Master wore a [[motifs/crown|crown]] of thorns."
He was a just ruler, and the laws he made were full of the plainest
public spirit.  But even if we dismiss all that was written
of him by Christian chroniclers because they might be his friends
(which would be a pathetic and exaggerated compliment to the harmonious
unity of Crusaders and of Christians) he would still remain
sufficiently assailed crowned with the words of his enemies.
For a Saracen chronicler wrote of him, with a fine simplicity,
that if all [[concepts/truth|truth]] and [[concepts/honour|honour]] had otherwise withered off the earth,
there would still remain enough of them so long as Duke Godfrey was alive.

Ciò che vi è di singolare nella Prima Crociata è che essa fu, in un senso del tutto nuovo e anomalo, un movimento popolare. Direi quasi che fu l'unico movimento popolare che vi sia mai stato al mondo. Perché non fu una cosa che il popolo seguì; fu anzi una cosa che il popolo guidò. Non solo fu essenzialmente una rivoluzione, ma fu l'unica rivoluzione di cui io sappia in cui le masse cominciarono ad agire da sole, e praticamente senza alcun sostegno da parte di nessuna delle classi. Quando ebbero agito, le classi si unirono; ed è perfettamente vero, e anzi del tutto naturale, che le masse da sole fallirono là dove le due insieme riuscirono. Ma furono gli ignoranti a istruire gli istruiti. Il caso della Crociata non è affatto un caso in cui certe idee furono dapprima suggerite da pochi filosofi, e poi predicate dai demagoghi alla [[concepts/democracy|democrazia]]. Ciò fu in gran parte vero per la [[historical-references/french-revolution|Rivoluzione francese]]; fu probabilmente ancor più vero per la Rivoluzione russa; e non occorre qui soffermarci sulla sottile sfumatura di differenza per cui Rousseau aveva ragione e Karl Marx torto. Nella Prima Crociata fu l'uomo comune ad avere ragione o torto. Egli si levò furibondo per l'offesa alle proprie piccole immagini o preghiere private, come se fosse uscito a combattere col proprio attizzatoio domestico o col proprio trinciante. Non era armato di nuove armi d'arguzia e logica distribuite dall'arsenale di un'accademia. Vi era arguzia e logica a volontà nelle accademie del Medioevo; ma il capo tipico della Crociata non fu Abelardo o [[characters/aquinas|Aquino]], bensì Pietro l'Eremita, che a stento si può dire persino un capo popolare, ma piuttosto una [[motifs/flag|bandiera]] popolare. E fu il suo esercito, o meglio la sua enorme accozzaglia, a marciare per primo attraverso il mondo per morire per la liberazione di Gerusalemme.

Gli storici dicono che in quell'immensa schiera di migliaia vi erano soltanto nove cavalieri. A chiunque conosca anche solo un poco la guerra medievale il fatto pare sbalorditivo. È infatti un errore da tempo sfatato considerare il Medioevo come identico al feudalesimo. Vi erano innumerevoli istituzioni democratiche, come le corporazioni; talvolta fino a venti corporazioni in una sola piccola città. Ma è davvero vero che l'organizzazione militare del Medioevo era quasi interamente feudale; anzi potremmo piuttosto dire che il feudalesimo era il nome della loro organizzazione militare. Che una massa militare così vasta abbia potuto tentare di muoversi, con soli nove dei capi militari naturali, mi pare un prodigio d'iniziativa popolare. È come se un parlamento venisse eletto alle prossime elezioni generali, nel quale solo due uomini potessero permettersi di leggere un quotidiano.

Questa folla marciò contro la disciplina militare dei Musulmani e fu massacrata; o, se posso esprimermi tanto misticamente, martirizzata. Molti dei grandi re e cavalieri che ne seguirono le orme non meritavano così chiaramente alcuna aureola per la semplicità e la purezza dei loro moventi. La canonizzazione di una tale moltitudine sarebbe forse impossibile, e certamente incontrerebbe resistenza nell'opinione moderna; soprattutto perché durante il cammino sfogarono la loro violenza democratica uccidendo vari usurai; una condotta che naturalmente riempie la società moderna di uno sdegno che rasenta l'allarme. Una perversità mi induce a piangere piuttosto più sui molti contadini trucidati che sui pochi usurai trucidati; ma in ogni caso i contadini di certo non furono trucidati invano. La comune [[concepts/conscience|coscienza]] di tutte le classi, in un [[motifs/time|tempo]] in cui tutti avevano un credo comune, si destò, e ne seguì un nuovo esercito di ben diversa abilità e addestramento; guidato dai più valenti capitani e da alcuni fra i gentiluomini più cavallereschi dell'epoca. Perché, curiosamente, la schiera conteneva più di un colto gentiluomo che era cristiano tanto semplice quanto qualsiasi contadino, e temerariamente pronto a farsi scannare o torturare per il solo nome di [[characters/christ|Cristo]].

È un ritornello dei materialisti che la [[concepts/truth|verità]] sulla [[concepts/history|storia]] cancelli il romanzesco della [[concepts/history|storia]]. Esso è caro alla mente moderna perché è deprimente; ma dà il caso che non sia vero. Nulla emerge più chiaramente da uno studio veramente realistico del curioso fatto che le persone romantiche erano davvero romantiche. Sono piuttosto i romanzi storici a indurre l'uomo moderno ad aspettarsi vagamente che il capo dei nuovi cavalieri, Goffredo di Buglione, sia stato un mero e brutale barone. I fatti storici depongono tutti a favore di un uomo assai più simile a un cavaliere della Tavola Rotonda. Anzi, egli fu un uomo assai migliore della maggior parte dei cavalieri della Tavola Rotonda, nei cui caratteri il favolista, sapendo di scrivere una [[forms/fable|favola]], ebbe il tatto d'introdurre una maggior mescolanza di vizio. La [[concepts/truth|verità]] non è soltanto più strana della finzione, ma spesso più santa della finzione. Perché la [[concepts/truth|verità]] è reale, mentre la finzione è costretta a essere realistica. Curiosamente, Goffredo pare essere stato eroico persino in quei mirabili accidenti che generalmente, e forse a ragione, si considerano orpelli della finzione. Così era di statura eroica, un bell'uomo dalla barba rossa, di grande forza e ardimento; e fu lui stesso il primo uomo a scavalcare le mura di Gerusalemme, come un [[archetypes/hero|eroe]] ragazzo in una storia d'avventura per ragazzi. Ma fu anche, il realista sarà sorpreso di apprenderlo, un uomo perfettamente onesto, e un praticante perfettamente genuino della magnanimità teorica della cavalleria. Tutto in lui lo suggerisce; dalla sua prima [[concepts/conversion|conversione]] dalla causa imperiale a quella papale (e popolare), fino al suo grande rifiuto della regalità della [[motifs/city|città]] che aveva conquistato: «Non porterò una [[motifs/crown|corona]] d'oro là dove il mio Signore portò una [[motifs/crown|corona]] di spine». Fu un sovrano giusto, e le leggi che promulgò erano piene del più schietto spirito pubblico. Ma anche se scartassimo tutto ciò che di lui scrissero i cronisti cristiani perché potevano essergli amici (il che sarebbe un patetico ed esagerato omaggio all'armoniosa unità dei Crociati e dei Cristiani), egli resterebbe comunque abbastanza incoronato dalle parole dei suoi nemici. Perché un cronista saraceno scrisse di lui, con bella semplicità, che se ogni [[concepts/truth|verità]] e [[concepts/honour|onore]] fossero altrimenti appassiti dalla terra, ne resterebbe ancora abbastanza finché il duca Goffredo fosse vivo.

Allied with Godfrey were Tancred the Italian, Raymond of Toulouse
with the southern French and Robert of Normandy, the adventurous
son of the Conqueror, with the Normans and the English.
But it would be an error, I think, and one tending to make the whole
subsequent story a thing not so much misunderstood as unintelligible,
to suppose that the whole crusading movement had been suddenly
and unnaturally stiffened with the highest chivalric discipline.
Unless I am much mistaken, a great mass of that army
was still very much of a mob.  It is probable <a priori>,
since the great popular movement was still profoundly popular.
It is supported by a thousand things in the story of the campaign;
the extraordinary emotionalism that made throngs of men weep and
wail together, the importance of the demagogue, Peter the Hermit,
in spite of his unmilitary character, and the wide differences between
the designs of the leaders and the actions of the rank and file.
It was a crowd of rude and simple men that cast themselves
on the sacred [[motifs/dust|dust]] at the first sight of the little mountain
town which they had tramped for two thousand miles to see.
Tancred saw it first from the slope by the village of Bethlehem,
which had opened its gates willingly to his hundred Italian knights;
for Bethlehem then as now was an island of Christendom in the sea
of [[historical-references/islam|Islam]].  Meanwhile Godfrey came up the [[motifs/road|road]] from Jaffa,
and crossing the mountain ridge, saw also with his living eyes
his vision of the world's [[motifs/desire|desire]].  But the poorest men about him
probably felt the same as he; all ranks knelt together in the [[motifs/dust|dust]],
and the whole story is one wave of numberless and nameless men.
It was a mob that had risen like a man for the [[concepts/faith|faith]].
It was a mob that had truly been tortured like a man for the [[concepts/faith|faith]].
It was already transfigured by [[motifs/pain|pain]] as [[motifs/well|well]] as passion.
Those that know war in those deserts through the [[motifs/summer|summer]] months,
even with modern supplies and appliances and modern maps and calculations,
know that it could only be described as a hell full of heroes.
What it must have been to those little local serfs and peasants from
the Northern villages, who had never dreamed in nightmares of such
landscapes or such a sun, who knew not how men lived at all in such
a furnace and could neither guess the alleviations nor get them,
is beyond the [[concepts/imagination|imagination]] of man.  They arrived dying with thirst,
dropping with weariness, lamenting the loss of the dead that rotted
along their [[motifs/road|road]]; they arrived shrivelled to rags or already raving
with fever and they did what they had come to do.

Above all, it is clear that they had the vices as [[motifs/well|well]] as the virtues
of a mob.  The shocking massacre in which they indulged in the sudden
relaxation of success is quite obviously a massacre by a mob.
It is all the more profoundly revolutionary because it must have
been for the most part a French mob.  It was of the same order
as the Massacre of September, and it is but a part of the same [[concepts/truth|truth]]
that the First Crusade was as revolutionary as the [[historical-references/french-revolution|French Revolution]].
It was of the same order as the Massacre of St. Bartholomew,
which was also a piece of purely popular [[concepts/fanaticism|fanaticism]], directed
against what was also regarded as an anti-national aristocracy.
It is practically [[concepts/self|self]]-evident that the Christian commanders were
opposed to it, and tried to stop it.  Tancred promised their lives
to the Moslems in the mosque, but the mob clearly disregarded him.
Raymond of Toulouse himself saved those in the Tower of David,
and managed to send them safely with their property to Ascalon.
But revolution with all its evil as [[motifs/well|well]] as its good was loose
and raging in the streets of the Holy [[motifs/city|City]].  And in nothing do we
see that spirit of revolution more clearly than in the sight
of all those peasants and serfs and vassals, in that one wild
moment in revolt, not only against the conquered lords of [[historical-references/islam|Islam]],
but even against the conquering lords of Christendom.

The whole strain of the siege indeed had been one of high and even
horrible excitement.  Those who tell us to-day about the psychology
of the crowd will agree that men who have so suffered and so succeeded
are not normal; that their brains are in a dreadful balance which may
turn either way.  They entered the [[motifs/city|city]] at last in a mood in which they
might all have become [[characters/monks|monks]]; and instead they all became murderers.
A brilliant general, who played a decisive part in our own recent
Palestinian campaign, told me with a sort of grim humour that he hardly
wondered at the story; for he himself had entered Jerusalem in a sort
of fury of disappointment; "We went through such a hell to get there,
and now it's spoilt for all of us."  Such is the heavy irony that
hangs over our human [[concepts/nature|nature]], making it enter the Holy [[motifs/city|City]] as if it
were the Heavenly [[motifs/city|City]], and more than any earthly [[motifs/city|city]] can be.
But the struggle which led to the scaling of Jerusalem in the
First Crusade was something much wilder and more incalculable than
anything that can be conceived in modern war.  We can hardly [[concepts/wonder|wonder]]
that the crusading crowd saw the town in front of them as a sort
of tower full of demons, and the hills around them as an enchanted
and accursed land.  For in one very real sense it really was so;
for all the elements and expedients were alike unknown qualities.
All their enemies' methods were [[motifs/secrets|secrets]] sprung upon them.
All their own methods were new things made out of nothing.
They wondered alike what would be done on the other side and what
could be done on their own side; every movement against them
was a stab out of the darkness and every movement they made
was a leap in the dark.  First, on the one side, we have Tancred
trying to take the whole fortified [[motifs/city|city]] by climbing up a single
slender ladder, as if a man tried to lasso the peak of a mountain.
Then we have the flinging from the turrets of a strange
and frightful fiery rain, as if [[motifs/water|water]] itself had caught [[motifs/fire|fire]].
It was afterwards known as the Greek [[motifs/fire|Fire]] and was probably petroleum;
but to those who had never seen (or felt) it before it may [[motifs/well|well]] have
seemed the flaming oil of witchcraft.  Then Godfrey and the wiser
of the warriors set about to build wooden siege-towers and found
they had next to no wood to build them.  There was scarcely anything
in that rocky waste but the [[characters/dwarf|dwarf]] trees of olive; a poetic fantasy
woven about that war in after ages described them as hindered
even in their wood-cutting by the demons of that weird place.
And indeed the fancy had an essential [[concepts/truth|truth]], for the very [[concepts/nature|nature]]
of the land fought against them; and each of those [[characters/dwarf|dwarf]] trees,
hard and hollow and twisted, may [[motifs/well|well]] have seemed like a grinning goblin.
It is said that they found timbers by accident in a cavern;
they tore down the beams from ruined houses; at last they got into touch
with some craftsmen from Genoa who went to work more successfully;
skinning the cattle, who had died in heaps, and covering the timbers.
They built three high towers on rollers, and men and beasts
dragged them heavily against the high towers of the [[motifs/city|city]].
The catapaults of the [[motifs/city|city]] answered them, the cataracts of
devouring [[motifs/fire|fire]] came down; the wooden towers swayed and tottered,
and two of them suddenly stuck motionless and useless.
And as the darkness fell a great flare must have told them that
the third and last was in flames.

Alleato con Goffredo c'era Tancredi l'italiano, Raimondo di Tolosa
con i francesi del sud e Roberto di Normandia, il figlio avventuroso del Conquistatore,
con i normanni e gli inglesi.
Ma sarebbe un errore, credo, e uno che tenderebbe a rendere tutta la storia
successiva non tanto incompresa quanto inintelligibile,
supporre che l'intero movimento crociato fosse stato improvvisamente
e innaturalmente irrigidito con la più alta disciplina cavalleresca.
A meno che non mi sbagli, una gran parte di quell'esercito
era ancora molto simile a una folla. È probabile <a priori>,
poiché il grande movimento popolare era ancora profondamente popolare.
È sostenuto da mille cose nella storia della campagna;
l'eccezionale emotività che faceva piangere e
piangere insieme folle di uomini, l'importanza del demagogo, Pietro l'Eremita,
nonostante il suo carattere poco militaresco, e le ampie differenze tra
i disegni dei capi e le azioni della truppa.
Era una folla di uomini rozzi e semplici che si gettò
sulla sacra [[motifs/dust|polvere]] alla prima vista del piccolo paese montano
che avevano percorso duemila miglia per vedere.
Tancredi lo vide per primo dalla pendenza vicino al villaggio di Betlemme,
che aveva aperto le porte volentieri ai suoi cento cavalieri italiani;
perché Betlemme allora come ora era un'isola della cristianità nel mare
dell'[[historical-references/islam|Islam]]. Nel frattempo Goffredo salì per la [[motifs/road|strada]] da Giaffa,
e attraversando il crinale della montagna, vide anche lui con i suoi occhi viventi
la sua visione del [[motifs/desire|desiderio]] del mondo. Ma gli uomini più poveri intorno a lui
probabilmente provavano la stessa cosa; tutte le classi si inginocchiarono insieme nella [[motifs/dust|polvere]],
e tutta la storia è un'onda di innumerevoli e senza nome.
Era una folla che si era sollevata come un uomo per la [[concepts/faith|fede]].
Era una folla che era stata veramente torturata come un uomo per la [[concepts/faith|fede]].
Era già trasfigurata dal [[motifs/pain|dolore]] così come dalla passione.
Coloro che conoscono la guerra in quei deserti durante i mesi dell'[[motifs/summer|estate]],
anche con forniture e apparecchiature moderne e mappe e calcoli moderni,
sanno che poteva essere descritta solo come un inferno pieno di eroi.
Quello che deve essere stato per quei piccoli servi locali e contadini
dei villaggi del Nord, che non avevano mai sognato in incubi paesaggi simili
o un tale sole, che non sapevano come vivessero gli uomini in una
tale fornace e non potevano nemmeno indovinare le attenuazioni né ottenerle,
è oltre l'[[concepts/imagination|immaginazione]] dell'uomo. Arrivarono morendo di sete,
cadendo per la stanchezza, lamentando la perdita dei morti che marcivano
lungo il loro [[motifs/road|cammino]]; arrivarono ridotti a stracci o già deliranti
per la febbre e fecero quello per cui erano venuti.

Soprattutto è chiaro che avevano i vizi così come le virtù di una folla.
La scioccante strage nella quale si abbandonarono nel rilassamento improvviso del successo è chiaramente una strage da folla. È ancor più profondamente rivoluzionaria perché deve essere stata per la maggior parte opera di una folla francese. Era dello stesso ordine
della Strage di Settembre, e fa parte della stessa [[concepts/truth|verità]] che la Prima Crociata fu tanto rivoluzionaria quanto la [[historical-references/french-revolution|Rivoluzione Francese]]. Era dello stesso ordine della Strage di San Bartolomeo,
che era anch'essa un puro [[concepts/fanaticism|fanatismo]] popolare, diretto
contro ciò che veniva considerato come una aristocrazia antinazionale.
È praticamente auto-evidente che i comandanti cristiani vi si opposero e cercarono di fermarla. Tancredi promise la vita ai musulmani nella moschea, ma la folla chiaramente lo ignorò.
Raimondo di Tolosa stesso salvò coloro nel Torrione di Davide,
e riuscì a mandarli in salvo con i loro beni ad Ascalona. Ma la rivoluzione col suo male così come il suo [[motifs/well|bene]] era scatenata
e infuriava nelle strade della Santa [[motifs/city|Città]]. E non vediamo quello spirito di rivoluzione più chiaramente che nella vista
di tutti quei contadini e servi e vassalli, in quel momento selvaggio,
in rivolta non solo contro i signori conquistati dell'[[historical-references/islam|Islam]],
ma anche contro i signori conquistatori della Cristianità.

La tensione dell'assedio era stata davvero di un'eccitazione intensa e persino orribile. Coloro che oggi ci parlano della psicologia della folla saranno d'accordo nel dire che uomini che hanno tanto sofferto e tanto vinto non sono normali; che i loro cervelli si trovano in uno squilibrio terribile che può inclinare da una parte o dall'altra. Entrarono infine nella [[motifs/city|città]] con un umore tale per cui avrebbero potuto tutti diventare [[characters/monks|monaci]]; invece divennero tutti assassini.
Un brillante generale, che ebbe un ruolo decisivo nella nostra recente campagna in Palestina, mi disse con una sorta di cupo umorismo che non si meravigliava affatto della storia; perché lui stesso era entrato a Gerusalemme in preda a una specie di furia delusa: "Abbiamo attraversato un tale inferno per arrivare lì, e ora è rovinata per tutti noi." Tale è la pesante ironia che grava sulla nostra natura umana, facendoci entrare nella [[concepts/nature|città]] Santa come se fosse la [[motifs/city|città]] Celeste, e più di quanto qualsiasi [[motifs/city|città]] terrena possa essere.
Ma la lotta che portò all'espugnazione di Gerusalemme nella Prima Crociata fu qualcosa di molto più selvaggio e imprevedibile di tutto ciò che si può concepire in una guerra moderna. Non ci stupiamo se la folla dei crociati vedeva il borgo davanti a loro come una sorta di torre piena di demoni, e le colline intorno come un paese incantato e maledetto. Perché in un senso molto reale era proprio così; poiché tutti gli elementi e i mezzi erano altrettante qualità sconosciute.
I metodi dei loro nemici erano [[motifs/secrets|segreti]] che li colpivano di sorpresa.
Tutti i loro metodi erano cose nuove fatte dal nulla.
Si chiedevano allo stesso modo cosa avrebbero fatto gli altri e cosa si poteva fare dalla loro parte; ogni movimento contro di loro era un pugnale uscito dall'oscurità, e ogni mossa che facevano era un salto nel buio. Prima, da una parte, abbiamo Tancredi che cerca di prendere l'intera [[motifs/city|città]] fortificata arrampicandosi su una sola fragile scala, come se uno cercasse di catturare con la laccia la vetta di una montagna. Poi abbiamo il lancio dalle torrette di una strana e spaventosa pioggia infuocata, come se [[motifs/water|acqua]] stessa fosse presa da [[motifs/fire|fuoco]]. In seguito divenne nota come il [[motifs/fire|Fuoco]] Greco ed era probabilmente petrolio; ma per coloro che non l'avevano mai vista (o sentita) prima, poteva [[motifs/well|ben]] sembrare l'olio infuocato della stregoneria. Poi Goffredo e i guerrieri più saggi si misero a costruire torri d'assedio di legno e scoprirono di non avere quasi legname per farle. Non c'era praticamente nulla in quel deserto roccioso se non gli alberi [[characters/dwarf|nani]] dell'ulivo; una fantasia poetica intessuta su quella guerra nelle età successive li descriveva ostacolati persino nel taglio del legno dai demoni di quel luogo strano.
E in effetti la fantasia aveva una [[concepts/truth|verità]] essenziale, perché la stessa [[concepts/nature|natura]] della terra combatteva contro di loro; e ciascuno di quegli alberi [[characters/dwarf|nani]], duri, vuoti e contorti, poteva [[motifs/well|ben]] sembrare come un goblin che ghigna.
Si dice che trovarono travi per caso in una caverna; strapparono i travetti da case in rovina; alla fine entrarono in contatto con alcuni artigiani di Genova che si misero all'opera più efficacemente;
scorticando il bestiame, morto a mucchi, e coprendo le travi.
Costruirono tre torri alte su ruote, e uomini e bestie

Le catapulta della [[motifs/city|città]] risposero loro, i cataratti di

E mentre calava l'oscurità, un grande bagliore deve aver loro detto che

All that [[motifs/night|night]] Godfrey was toiling to retrieve the disaster.
He took down the whole tower from where it stood and raised
it again on the high ground to the north of the [[motifs/city|city]] which is
now marked by the pine tree that grows outside Herod's gate.
And all the [[motifs/time|time]] he toiled, it was said, sinister sorcerers sat
upon the battlements, working unknown marvels for the undoing
of the labour of man.  If the great knight had a touch of such
symbolism on his own side, he might have seen in his own strife
with the solid timber something of the craft that had surrounded
the birth of his creed, and the sacred trade of the carpenter.
And indeed the very pattern of all carpentry is cruciform, and there
is something more than an accident in the [[forms/allegory|allegory]].  The transverse
position of the timber does indeed involve many of those mathematical
that are analogous to moral truths and almost every structural
shape has the [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the mystic rood, as the three dimensions
have a [[archetypes/shadow|shadow]] of the Trinity.  Here is the true mystery of equality;
since the longer beam might lengthen itself to infinity, and never
be nearer to the symbolic shape without the help of the shorter.
Here is that war and wedding between two contrary forces, resisting and
supporting each other; the meeting-place of contraries which we,
by a sort of pietistic pun, still call the crux of the question.
Here is our angular and defiant answer to the [[concepts/self|self]]-devouring circle
of Asia.  It may be improbable, though it is far from impossible
(for the age was philosophical enough) that a man like Godfrey
thus extended the mystical to the metaphysical; but the writer
of a real romance about him would be [[motifs/well|well]] within his rights in making
him see the symbolism of his own tower, a tower rising above
him through the clouds of [[motifs/night|night]] as if taking hold on the heaven
or showing its network of beams black against the daybreak;
scaling the skies and open to all the winds, a ladder and a labyrinth,
repeating till it was lost in the twilight the pattern of the sign
of the [[motifs/cross|cross]].

When [[motifs/dawn|dawn]] was come all those starving peasants may [[motifs/well|well]] have stood
before the high impregnable walls in the broad daylight of [[concepts/despair|despair]].
Even their nightmares during the [[motifs/night|night]], of unearthly necromancers
looking down at them from the battlements and with signs and spells
paralysing all their potential toils, may [[motifs/well|well]] have been a sort
of pessimistic consolation, anticipating and accounting for failure.
The Holy [[motifs/city|City]] had become for them a fortress full of fiends, when Godfrey
de Bouillon again set himself sword in hand upon the wooden tower and gave
the order once more to drag it tottering towards the towers on either
side of the pastern gate.  So they crawled again across the fosse
full of the slain, dragging their huge house of timber behind them,
and all the blast and din of war broke again about their heads.
A hail of bolts hammered such shields as covered them for a canopy,
stones and rocks fell on them and crushed them like flies in
the mire, and from the engines of the Greek [[motifs/fire|Fire]] all the torrents
of their torment came down on them like red rivers of hell.
For indeed the souls of those peasants must have been sickened
with something of the topsy-turvydom felt by too many peasants of our
own [[motifs/time|time]] under the frightful flying batteries of scientific war;
a blasphemy of inverted battle in which hell itself has occupied heaven.
Something of the vapours vomited by such cruel chemistry may
have mingled with the [[motifs/dust|dust]] of battle, and darkened such light
as showed where shattering rocks were rending a roof of shields,
to men bowed and blinded as they are by such labour of dragging
and such a hailstorm of [[motifs/death|death]].  They may have heard through
all the racket of nameless noises the high minaret cries
of Moslem triumph rising shriller like a [[motifs/wind|wind]] in shrill pipes,
and known little else of what was happening above or beyond them.
It was most likely that they laboured and strove in that lower darkness,
not knowing that high over their heads, and up above the cloud
of battle, the tower of timber and the tower of stone had touched
and met in mid-heaven; and great Godfrey, alone and alive had leapt
upon the wall of Jerusalem.

Tutta quella [[motifs/night|notte]] Godfrey si affannò per rimediare al disastro.
Abbatté l'intera torre dal luogo dove sorgeva e la ricostruì
sulle alture a nord della [[motifs/city|città]], che oggi sono segnate dalla pianta di pino
che cresce fuori dalle porte di Erode.
E tutto il [[motifs/time|tempo]] in cui si affannò, si diceva che sinistri maghi sedessero
sui merli, operando ignote meraviglie per disfare
il lavoro dell'uomo. Se il grande cavaliere avesse avuto un barlume di tale
simbolismo dalla sua parte, avrebbe potuto vedere nella propria lotta
con la solida trave qualcosa del mestiere che aveva circondato
la nascita della sua fede e il sacro lavoro del carpentiere.
E in effetti lo stesso schema di tutta la carpenteria è a forma di croce, e
c'è più di un caso nell'[[forms/allegory|allegoria]]. La posizione trasversale
della trave implica infatti molte di quelle verità matematiche
che sono analoghe alle verità morali e quasi ogni struttura
ha l'[[archetypes/shadow|ombra]] della mistica croce, come le tre dimensioni hanno un'[[archetypes/shadow|ombra]]
della Trinità. Ecco il vero mistero dell'uguaglianza;
poiché la trave più lunga potrebbe allungarsi fino all'infinito e non
avvicinarsi mai alla forma simbolica senza l'aiuto della più corta.
Ecco quella guerra e quel matrimonio tra due forze opposte, che si resistono e
si sostengono a vicenda; il luogo d'incontro dei contrari che noi,
con una sorta di pietistico gioco di parole, chiamiamo ancora la croce della questione.
Ecco la nostra angolare e provocatoria risposta al [[concepts/self|se]]-devorante cerchio
dell'Asia. Può essere improbabile, sebbene sia tutt'altro che impossibile
(perché quell'epoca era abbastanza filosofica) che un uomo come Godfrey
abbia così esteso il mistico al metafisico; ma lo scrittore
di una vera romanzo su di lui sarebbe [[motifs/well|bene]] nei suoi diritti nel farlo
vedere il simbolismo della sua torre, una torre che si ergeva sopra
di lui attraverso le nuvole della [[motifs/night|notte]] come se volesse afferrare il cielo
o mostrare la sua rete di travi nera contro l'alba;
che scalava i cieli ed era aperta a tutti i venti, una scala e un labirinto,
ripetendo fino a perdersi nel crepuscolo lo schema del segno
della [[motifs/cross|croce]].

Quando l'[[motifs/dawn|alba]] giunse, tutti quei contadini affamati poterono [[motifs/well|benissimo]] trovarsi di fronte alle alte mura inespugnabili alla piena luce del giorno dell'[[concepts/despair|disperazione]]. 
Anche i loro incubi durante la [[motifs/night|notte]], con negromanti sovrumani che li guardavano dall'alto dei merli e con segni e incantesimi paralizzavano tutti i loro potenziali sforzi, poterono [[motifs/well|benissimo]] essere una sorta di consolazione pessimistica, anticipando e giustificando il fallimento.
La Santa [[motifs/city|Città]] era diventata per loro una fortezza piena di demoni, quando Goffredo di Buglione si mise nuovamente all'opera con la spada in pugno sulla torre di legno e diede ancora l'ordine di trascinarla vacillante verso le torri su entrambi i lati della porta dei cavalli. 
Così strisciarono nuovamente attraverso il fossato pieno di cadaveri, trainando la loro enorme casa di legno dietro di sé, e tutto lo scoppio e il fragore della guerra si abbatterono ancora sulle loro teste.
Una grandinata di dardi martellò gli scudi che li coprivano come un baldacchino, pietre e massi caddero su di loro e li schiacciarono come mosche nel fango, e dalle macchine del [[motifs/fire|Fuoco]] greco tutti i torrenti della loro tortura si riversarono su di loro come fiumi rossi dell'inferno.
Perché invero le anime di quei contadini devono essersi sentite nauseate da qualcosa dello sconvolgimento provato da troppi contadini del nostro [[motifs/time|tempo]] sotto le spaventose batterie volanti della guerra scientifica;
un'empia inversione della battaglia in cui l'inferno stesso ha occupato il cielo.
Qualcosa dei vapori vomitati da tale crudele chimica poté
aver mescolato la [[motifs/dust|polvere]] della battaglia, e oscurato quella luce
che mostrava dove le rocce frantumanti stavano squarciando un tetto di scudi,
agli uomini curvi e accecati com'erano da tale fatica di trascinamento
e da tale grandine di [[motifs/death|morte]].  Poterono aver udito attraverso
tutto il frastuono dei rumori senza nome le alte grida dai minareti
del trionfo musulmano che si facevano più acute come un [[motifs/wind|vento]] in flauti acuti,
e sapere ben poco di ciò che accadeva sopra o oltre loro.
Fu molto probabile che lavorassero e lottassero in quella bassa oscurità,
ignorando che alto sopra le loro teste, e al di sopra della nuvola
della battaglia, la torre di legno e la torre di pietra si erano toccate
e incontrate nel mezzo del cielo; e il grande Goffredo, solo e vivo era balzato
sulla muraglia di Gerusalemme.
