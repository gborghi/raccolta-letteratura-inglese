[[characters/christabel|CHRISTABEL]] PREFACE
The first part of the following poem was written in the
year 1797, at Stowey, in the county of Somerset. The
second part, after my return from [[settings/germany|Germany]], in the year
1800, at Keswick, Cumberland. It is probable that if the
poem had been finished at either of the former periods, or   5
if even the first and second part had been published in the
year 1800, the impression of its originality would have been
much greater than I dare at present expect. But for this
I have only my own [[concepts/indolence|indolence]] to blame. The dates are
mentioned for the exclusive purpose of precluding charges of   10
plagiarism or servile imitation from myself. For there is
amongst us a set of critics, who seem to hold, that every
possible thought and image is traditional; who have no
notion that there are such things as fountains in the world,
small as [[motifs/well|well]] as great; and who would therefore charitably   15
derive every rill they behold flowing, from a perforation
made in some other man’s tank. I am confident, however,
that as far as the present poem is concerned, the celebrated
poets whose writings I might be suspected of having
imitated, either in particular passages, or in the tone and   20
the spirit of the whole, would be among the first to vindicate
me from the charge, and who, on any striking coincidence,
would permit me to address them in this doggerel version
of two monkish Latin hexameters.
‘Tis mine and it is likewise yours;    25
But an if this will not do;
Let it be mine, good friend! for I
Am the poorer of the two.
I have only to add that the metre of [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] is not,
properly speaking, irregular, though it may seem so from its   30
being founded on a new principle: namely, that of counting
in each line the accents, not the syllables. Though the latter
may vary from seven to twelve, yet in each line the accents
will be found to be only four. Nevertheless, this occasional
variation in number of syllables is not introduced wantonly,   35
or for the mere ends of convenience, but in correspondence
with some transition in the [[concepts/nature|nature]] of the imagery or passion.

CHRISTABELPART I  
‘Tis the middle of [[motifs/night|night]] by the [[settings/castle|castle]] [[motifs/clock|clock]],  
And the owls have awakened the crowing cock;  
Tu — whit! —— Tu — whoo!  
And hark, again! the crowing cock,  
How drowsily it crew.   5  
Sir Leoline, the Baron rich,  
Hath a toothless mastiff bitch;  
From her kennel beneath the rock  
She maketh answer to the [[motifs/clock|clock]],  
Four for the quarters, and twelve for the hour;   10  
Ever and aye, by shine and shower,  
Sixteen short howls, not over loud;  
Some say, she sees my lady’s shroud.  
Is the [[motifs/night|night]] chilly and dark?  
The [[motifs/night|night]] is chilly, but not dark.   15  
The thin gray cloud is spread on high,  
It covers but not hides the sky.  
The [[motifs/moon|moon]] is behind, and at the full;  
And yet she looks both small and dull.  
The [[motifs/night|night]] is chill, the cloud is gray:   20  
‘Tis a month before the month of May,  
And the [[motifs/spring|Spring]] comes slowly up this way.  
The lovely lady, [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],  
Whom her father loves so [[motifs/well|well]],  
What makes her in the wood so late,    25  
A furlong from the [[settings/castle|castle]] gate?  
She had [[motifs/dreams|dreams]] all yesternight  
Of her own betrothéd knight;  
And she in the midnight wood will pray  
For the weal of her [[archetypes/lover|lover]] that’s far away.   30  
She stole along, she nothing spoke,  
The sighs she heaved were soft and low,  
And naught was green upon the oak  
But moss and rarest misletoe:  
She kneels beneath the huge oak tree,   35  
And in [[motifs/silence|silence]] prayeth she.  
The lady sprang up suddenly,  
The lovely lady, [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]!  
It moaned as near, as near can be,  
But what it is she cannot tell. — 40  
On the other side it seems to be,  
Of the huge, broad-breasted, old oak tree.  
The [[motifs/night|night]] is chill; the forest bare;  
Is it the [[motifs/wind|wind]] that moaneth bleak?  
There is not [[motifs/wind|wind]] enough in the air    45  
To move away the ringlet curl  
From the lovely lady’s cheek —  
There is not [[motifs/wind|wind]] enough to twirl  
The one red leaf, the last of its clan,  
That dances as often as [[motifs/dance|dance]] it can,   50  
Hanging so light, and hanging so high,  
On the topmost twig that looks up at the sky.  
Hush, beating heart of [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]!  
Jesu, [[characters/maria|Maria]], shield her [[motifs/well|well]]!  
She folded her arms beneath her cloak,   55  
And stole to the other side of the oak.  
What sees she there?  
There she sees a [[archetypes/damsel|damsel]] bright,  
Drest in a silken robe of white,  
That shadowy in the moonlight shone:   60  
The neck that made that white robe wan,  
Her stately neck, and arms were bare;  
Her blue-veined feet unsandal’d were,  
And wildly glittered here and there  
The gems entangled in her hair.   65  
I guess, ‘twas frightful there to see  
A lady so richly clad as she —  
Beautiful exceedingly!  
Mary mother, save me now!  
(Said [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],) And who art thou?   70  
The lady strange made answer meet,  
And her voice was faint and sweet: —  
Have pity on my sore distress,  
I scarce can speak for weariness:  
Stretch forth thy hand, and have no [[concepts/fear|fear]]!   75  
Said [[characters/christabel|Christabel]], How camest thou here?  
And the lady, whose voice was faint and sweet,  
Did thus pursue her answer meet: —  
My sire is of a noble line,  
And my name is [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]:   80  
Five warriors seized me yestermorn,  
Me, even me, a maid forlorn:  
They choked my cries with force and fright,  
And tied me on a palfrey white.  
The palfrey was as fleet as [[motifs/wind|wind]],   85  
And they rode furiously behind.  
They spurred amain, their steeds were white:  
And once we crossed the shade of [[motifs/night|night]].  
As sure as Heaven shall rescue me,  
I have no thought what men they be;    90  
Nor do I know how long it is  
(For I have lain entranced I wis)  
Since one, the tallest of the five,  
Took me from the palfrey’s back,  
A weary woman, scarce alive.   95  
Some muttered words his comrades spoke:  
Ha placed me underneath this oak;  
He swore they would return with haste;  
Whither they went I cannot tell —  
I thought I heard, some minutes past,   100  
Sounds as of a [[settings/castle|castle]] bell.  
Stretch forth thy hand (thus ended she),  
And help a wretched maid to flee.  
Then [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] stretched forth her hand,  
And comforted fair [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]:   105  
O [[motifs/well|well]], bright dame! may you command  
The service of Sir Leoline;  
And gladly our stout chivalry  
Will he send forth and friends withal  
To guide and guard you safe and free   110  
[[motifs/home|Home]] to your noble father’s hall.  
She [[motifs/rose|rose]]: and forth with steps they passed  
That strove to be, and were not, fast.  
Her gracious [[motifs/stars|stars]] the lady blest,  
And thus spake on sweet [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]:   115  
All our household are at rest,  
The hall as silent as the cell;  
Sir Leoline is weak in health,  
And may not [[motifs/well|well]] awakened be,  
But we will move as if in stealth,    120  
And I beseech your courtesy,  
This [[motifs/night|night]], to share your couch with me.  
They crossed the moat, and [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]  
Took the key that fitted [[motifs/well|well]];  
A little door she opened straight,    125  
All in the middle of the gate;  
The gate that was ironed within and without,  
Where an army in battle array had marched out.  
The lady sank, belike through [[motifs/pain|pain]],  
And [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] with might and main    130  
Lifted her up, a weary weight,  
Over the threshold of the gate:  
Then the lady [[motifs/rose|rose]] again,  
And moved, as she were not in [[motifs/pain|pain]].  
So free from danger, free from [[concepts/fear|fear]],   135  
They crossed the [[settings/court|court]]: right glad they were.  
And [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] devoutly cried  
To the lady by her side,  
Praise we the Virgin all divine  
Who hath rescued thee from thy distress!   140  
Alas, alas! said [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]],  
I cannot speak for weariness.  
So free from danger, free from [[concepts/fear|fear]],  
They crossed the [[settings/court|court]]: right glad they were.  
Outside her kennel, the mastiff old   145  
Lay fast asleep, in moonshine cold.  
The mastiff old did not awake,  
Yet she an angry moan did make!  
And what can ail the mastiff bitch?  
Never till now she uttered yell   150  
Beneath the eye of [[characters/christabel|Christabel]].  
Perhaps it is the owlet’s scritch:  
For what can ail the mastiff bitch?  
They passed the hall, that echoes still,  
Pass as lightly as you will!   155  
The brands were flat, the brands were dying,  
Amid their own white ashes lying;  
But when the lady passed, there came  
A tongue of light, a fit of flame;  
And [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] saw the lady’s eye,    160  
And nothing else saw she thereby,  
Save the boss of the shield of Sir Leoline tall,  
Which hung in a murky old niche in the wall.  
O softly tread, said [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],  
My father seldom sleepeth [[motifs/well|well]].   165  
Sweet [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] her feet doth bare,  
And jealous of the listening air  
They steal their way from stair to stair,  
Now in glimmer, and now in gloom,  
And now they pass the Baron’s room,   170  
As still as [[motifs/death|death]], with stifled breath!  
And now have reached her chamber door;  
And now doth [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] press down  
The rushes of the chamber floor.  
The [[motifs/moon|moon]] shines dim in the open air,   175  
And not a moonbeam enters here.  
But they without its light can see  
The chamber carved so curiously,  
Carved with figures strange and sweet,  
All made out of the carver’s brain,   180  
For a lady’s chamber meet:  
The lamp with twofold silver chain  
Is fastened to an angel’s feet.  
The silver lamp burns dead and dim;  
But [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] the lamp will trim.    185  
She trimmed the lamp, and made it bright,  
And left it swinging to and fro,  
While [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]], in wretched plight,  
Sank down upon the floor below.  
O weary lady, [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]],   190  
I pray you, drink this cordial wine!  
It is a wine of virtuous powers;  
My mother made it of wild [[motifs/flowers|flowers]].  
And will your mother pity me,  
Who am a maiden most forlorn?   195  
[[characters/christabel|Christabel]] answered — Woe is me!  
She died the hour that I was born.  
I have heard the grey-haired friar tell  
How on her [[motifs/death|death]]-bed she did say,  
That she should hear the [[settings/castle|castle]]-bell   200  
Strike twelve upon my wedding-day.  
O mother dear! that thou wert here!  
I would, said [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]], she were!  
But soon with altered voice, said she —  
‘Off, wandering mother! Peak and pine!   205  
I have power to bid thee flee.’  
Alas! what ails poor [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]?  
Why stares she with unsettled eye?  
Can she the bodiless dead espy?  
And why with hollow voice cries she,   210  
‘Off, woman, off! this hour is mine —  
Though thou her guardian spirit be,  
Off, woman, off! ‘tis given to me.’  
Then [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] knelt by the lady’s side,  
And raised to heaven her eyes so blue — 215  
Alas! said she, this ghastly ride —  
Dear lady! it hath wildered you!  
The lady wiped her moist cold brow,  
And faintly said, ‘‘tis over now!’  
Again the wild-flower wine she drank:   220  
Her fair large eyes ‘gan glitter bright,  
And from the floor whereon she sank,  
The lofty lady stood upright:  
She was most beautiful to see,  
Like a lady of a far countrée.   225  
And thus the lofty lady spake —  
‘All they who live in the upper sky,  
Do [[motifs/love|love]] you, holy [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]!  
And you [[motifs/love|love]] them, and for their sake  
And for the good which me befel,   230  
Even I in my degree will try,  
Fair maiden, to requite you [[motifs/well|well]].  
But now unrobe yourself; for I  
Must pray, ere yet in bed I lie.’  
Quoth [[characters/christabel|Christabel]], So let it be!   235  
And as the lady bade, did she.  
Her gentle limbs did she undress,  
And lay down in her loveliness.  
But through her brain of weal and woe  
So many thoughts moved to and fro,    240  
That vain it were her lids to close;  
So half-way from the bed she [[motifs/rose|rose]],  
And on her elbow did recline  
To look at the lady [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]].  
Beneath the lamp the lady bowed,   245  
And slowly rolled her eyes around;  
Then drawing in her breath aloud,  
Like one that shuddered, she unbound  
The cincture from beneath her breast:  
Her silken robe, and inner vest,   250  
Dropt to her feet, and full in view,  
Behold! her bosom and half her side —  
A sight to dream of, not to tell!  
O shield her! shield sweet [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]!  
Yet [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] nor speaks nor stirs;   255  
Ah! what a stricken look was hers!  
Deep from within she seems half-way  
To lift some weight with sick assay,  
And eyes the maid and seeks delay;  
Then suddenly, as one defied,   260  
Collects herself in scorn and [[concepts/pride|pride]],  
And lay down by the Maiden’s side! —  
And in her arms the maid she took,  
Ah wel-a-day!  
And with low voice and doleful look   265  
These words did say:  
‘In the touch of this bosom there worketh a spell,  
Which is lord of thy utterance, [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]!  
Thou knowest to-[[motifs/night|night]], and wilt know to-morrow,  
This mark of my shame, this seal of my sorrow;   270  
But vainly thou warrest,  
For this is alone in  
Thy power to declare,  
That in the dim forest  
Thou heard’st a low moaning,    275  
And found’st a bright lady, surpassingly fair;  
And didst bring her [[motifs/home|home]] with thee in [[motifs/love|love]] and in [[concepts/charity|charity]],  
To shield her and shelter her from the damp air.’

[[characters/christabel|CHRISTABEL]] THE CONCLUSION TO PART I  
It was a lovely sight to see  
The lady [[characters/christabel|Christabel]], when she   280  
Was praying at the old oak tree.  
Amid the jaggéd shadows  
Of mossy leafless boughs,  
Kneeling in the moonlight,  
To make her gentle vows;   285  
Her slender palms together prest,  
Heaving sometimes on her breast;  
Her face resigned to bliss or bale —  
Her face, oh call it fair not pale,  
And both blue eyes more bright than clear,   290  
Each about to have a tear.  
With open eyes (ah woe is me!)  
Asleep, and dreaming fearfully,  
Fearfully dreaming, yet, I wis,  
Dreaming that alone, which is — 295  
O sorrow and shame! Can this be she,  
The lady, who knelt at the old oak tree?  
And lo! the worker of these harms,  
That holds the maiden in her arms,  
Seems to slumber still and mild,   300  
As a mother with her [[archetypes/child|child]].  
A star hath set, a star hath risen,  
O [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]! since arms of thine  
Have been the lovely lady’s [[motifs/prison|prison]].  
O [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]! one hour was thine — 305  
Thou’st had thy will! By tairn and rill,  
The [[motifs/night|night]]-[[motifs/birds|birds]] all that hour were still.  
But now they are jubilant anew,  
From cliff and tower, tu — whoo! tu — whoo!  
Tu — whoo! tu — whoo! from wood and fell!   310  
And see! the lady [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]  
Gathers herself from out her trance;  
Her limbs relax, her countenance  
Grows sad and soft; the smooth thin lids  
Close o’er her eyes; and [[motifs/tears|tears]] she sheds — 315  
Large [[motifs/tears|tears]] that leave the lashes bright!  
And oft the while she seems to smile  
As infants at a sudden light!  
Yea, she doth smile, and she doth weep,  
Like a youthful hermitess,   320  
Beauteous in a wilderness,  
Who, praying always, prays in [[motifs/sleep|sleep]].  
And, if she move unquietly,  
Perchance, ‘tis but the [[motifs/blood|blood]] so free  
Comes back and tingles in her feet.   325  
No [[concepts/doubt|doubt]], she hath a vision sweet.  
What if her guardian spirit ‘twere,  
What if she knew her mother near?  
But this she knows, in joys and woes,  
That saints will aid if men will call:   330  
For the blue sky bends over all!  
1797.

[[characters/christabel|CHRISTABEL]] PART II  
Each matin bell, the Baron saith,  
Knells us back to a world of [[motifs/death|death]].  
These words Sir Leoline first said,  
When he [[motifs/rose|rose]] and found his lady dead:   335  
These words Sir Leoline will say  
Many a morn to his dying day!  
And hence the custom and law began  
That still at [[motifs/dawn|dawn]] the sacristan,  
Who duly pulls the heavy bell,   340  
Five and forty beads must tell  
Between each stroke — a warning knell,  
Which not a [[motifs/soul|soul]] can choose but hear  
From Bratha Head to Wyndermere.  
Saith Bracy the bard, So let it knell!   345  
And let the drowsy sacristan  
Still count as slowly as he can!  
There is no lack of such, I ween,  
As [[motifs/well|well]] fill up the space between.  
In Langdale Pike and [[characters/witch|Witch]]’s Lair,    350  
And Dungeon-ghyll so foully rent,  
With ropes of rock and [[motifs/bells|bells]] of air  
Three sinful sextons’ [[motifs/ghosts|ghosts]] are pent,  
Who all give back, one after t’other,  
The [[motifs/death|death]]-note to their living brother;   355  
And oft too, by the knell offended,  
Just as their one! two! three! is ended,  
The devil mocks the doleful tale  
With a merry peal from Borodale.  
The air is still! through mist and cloud   360  
That merry peal comes ringing loud;  
And [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] shakes off her dread,  
And rises lightly from the bed;  
Puts on her silken vestments white,  
And tricks her hair in lovely plight,   365  
And nothing doubting of her spell  
Awakens the lady [[characters/christabel|Christabel]].  
‘[[motifs/sleep|Sleep]] you, sweet lady [[characters/christabel|Christabel]]?  
I trust that you have rested [[motifs/well|well]].’  
And [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] awoke and spied   370  
The same who lay down by her side —  
O rather say, the same whom she  
Raised up beneath the old oak tree!  
Nay, fairer yet! and yet more fair!  
For she belike hath drunken deep   375  
Of all the blessedness of [[motifs/sleep|sleep]]!  
And while she spake, her looks, her air  
Such gentle thankfulness declare,  
That (so it seemed) her girded vests  
Grew tight beneath her heaving breasts.   380  
‘Sure I have sinn’d!’ said [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],  
‘Now heaven be praised if all be [[motifs/well|well]]!’  
And in low faltering tones, yet sweet,  
Did she the lofty lady greet  
With such perplexity of mind   385  
As [[motifs/dreams|dreams]] too lively leave behind.  
So quickly she [[motifs/rose|rose]], and quickly arrayed  
Her maiden limbs, and having prayed  
That He, who on the [[motifs/cross|cross]] did groan,  
Might wash away her sins unknown,   390  
She forthwith led fair [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]  
To meet her sire, Sir Leoline.  
The lovely maid and the lady tall  
Are pacing both into the hall,  
And pacing on through page and groom,   395  
Enter the Baron’s presence-room.  
The Baron [[motifs/rose|rose]], and while he prest  
His gentle daughter to his breast,  
With cheerful [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] in his eyes  
The lady [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] espies,   400  
And gave such welcome to the same,  
As might beseem so bright a dame!  
But when he heard the lady’s tale,  
And when she told her father’s name,  
Why waxed Sir Leoline so pale,   405  
Murmuring o’er the name again,  
Lord Roland de Vaux of Tryermaine?  
Alas! they had been friends in youth;  
But whispering tongues can [[motifs/poison|poison]] [[concepts/truth|truth]];  
And constancy lives in realms above;   410  
And life is thorny; and youth is vain;  
And to be wroth with one we [[motifs/love|love]]  
Doth work like [[concepts/madness|madness]] in the brain.  
And thus it chanced, as I divine,  
With Roland and Sir Leoline.   415  
Each spake words of high disdain  
And insult to his heart’s best brother:  
They parted — ne’er to meet again!  
But never either found another  
To free the hollow heart from paining — 420  
They stood aloof, the scars remaining,  
Like cliffs which had been rent asunder;  
A dreary sea now flows between; —  
But neither heat, nor [[motifs/frost|frost]], nor thunder,  
Shall wholly do away, I ween,   425  
The marks of that which once hath been.  
Sir Leoline, a moment’s space,  
Stood gazing on the [[archetypes/damsel|damsel]]’s face:  
And the youthful Lord of Tryermaine  
Came back upon his heart again.   430  
O then the Baron forgot his age,  
His noble heart swelled high with rage;  
He swore by the wounds in Jesu’s side  
He would proclaim it far and wide,  
With trump and solemn heraldry,   435  
That they, who thus had wronged the dame,  
Were base as spotted infamy!  
‘And if they dare deny the same,  
My herald shall appoint a week,  
And let the recreant traitors seek    440  
My tourney [[settings/court|court]] — that there and then  
I may dislodge their reptile souls  
From the bodies and forms of men!’  
He spake: his eye in [[motifs/lightning|lightning]] rolls!  
For the lady was ruthlessly seized; and he kenned   445  
In the beautiful lady the [[archetypes/child|child]] of his friend!  
And now the [[motifs/tears|tears]] were on his face,  
And fondly in his arms he took  
Fair [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]], who met the embrace,  
Prolonging it with joyous look.   450  
Which when she viewed, a vision fell  
Upon the [[motifs/soul|soul]] of [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],  
The vision of [[concepts/fear|fear]], the touch and [[motifs/pain|pain]]!  
She shrunk and shuddered, and saw again —  
(Ah, woe is me! Was it for thee,   455  
Thou gentle maid! such sights to see?)  
Again she saw that bosom old,  
Again she felt that bosom cold,  
And drew in her breath with a hissing sound:  
Whereat the Knight turned wildly round,   460  
And nothing saw, but his own sweet maid  
With eyes upraised, as one that prayed.  
The touch, the sight, had passed away,  
And in its stead that vision blest,  
Which comforted her after-rest   465  
While in the lady’s arms she lay,  
Had put a rapture in her breast,  
And on her lips and o’er her eyes  
Spread smiles like light!  
With new surprise,  
‘What ails then my belovéd [[archetypes/child|child]]?’    470  
The Baron said — His daughter mild  
Made answer, ‘All will yet be [[motifs/well|well]]!’  
I ween, she had no power to tell  
Aught else: so mighty was the spell.  
Yet he, who saw this [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]],   475  
Had deemed her sure a thing divine:  
Such sorrow with such grace she blended,  
As if she feared she had offended  
Sweet [[characters/christabel|Christabel]], that gentle maid!  
And with such lowly tones she prayed   480  
She might be sent without delay  
[[motifs/home|Home]] to her father’s mansion.  
‘Nay!  
Nay, by my [[motifs/soul|soul]]!’ said Leoline.  
‘Ho! Bracy the bard, the charge be thine!  
Go thou, with music sweet and loud,   485  
And take two steeds with trappings proud,  
And take the youth whom thou lov’st best  
To bear thy harp, and learn thy song,  
And clothe you both in solemn vest,  
And over the [[settings/mountains|mountains]] haste along,   490  
Lest wandering folk, that are abroad,  
Detain you on the valley [[motifs/road|road]].  
‘And when he has crossed the Irthing flood,  
My merry bard! he hastes, he hastes  
Up Knorren Moor, through Halegarth Wood,   495  
And reaches soon that [[settings/castle|castle]] good  
Which stands and threatens Scotland’s wastes.  
‘Bard Bracy! bard Bracy! your horses are fleet,  
Ye must ride up the hall, your music so sweet,  
More loud than your horses’ echoing feet!   500  
And loud and loud to Lord Roland call,  
Thy daughter is safe in Langdale hall!  
Thy beautiful daughter is safe and free —  
Sir Leoline greets thee thus through me!  
He bids thee come without delay   505  
With all thy numerous array  
And take thy lovely daughter [[motifs/home|home]]:  
And he will meet thee on the way  
With all his numerous array  
White with their panting palfreys’ foam:   510  
And, by mine [[concepts/honour|honour]]! I will say,  
That I repent me of the day  
When I spake words of fierce disdain  
To Roland de Vaux of Tryermaine! —  
— For since that evil hour hath flown,   515  
Many a [[motifs/summer|summer]]’s sun hath shone;  
Yet ne’er found I a friend again  
Like Roland de Vaux of Tryermaine.’  
The lady fell, and clasped his knees,  
Her face upraised, her eyes o’erflowing;   520  
And Bracy replied, with faltering voice,  
His gracious Hail on all bestowing! —  
‘Thy words, thou sire of [[characters/christabel|Christabel]],  
Are sweeter than my harp can tell;  
Yet might I gain a boon of thee,   525  
This day my [[motifs/journey|journey]] should not be,  
So strange a dream hath come to me,  
That I had vowed with music loud  
To clear yon wood from thing unblest,  
Warned by a vision in my rest!   530  
For in my [[motifs/sleep|sleep]] I saw that dove,  
That gentle bird, whom thou dost [[motifs/love|love]],  
And call’st by thy own daughter’s name —  
Sir Leoline! I saw the same  
Fluttering, and uttering fearful moan,   535  
Among the green herbs in the forest alone.  
Which when I saw and when I heard,  
I [[concepts/wonder|wonder]]’d what might ail the bird;  
For nothing near it could I see,  
Save the grass and green herbs underneath the old tree.  
‘And in my dream methought I went   541  
To search out what might there be found;  
And what the sweet bird’s trouble meant,  
That thus lay fluttering on the ground.  
I went and peered, and could descry   545  
No cause for her distressful cry;  
But yet for her dear lady’s sake  
I stooped, methought, the dove to take,  
When lo! I saw a bright green snake  
Coiled around its wings and neck.   550  
Green as the herbs on which it couched,  
Close by the dove’s its head it crouched;  
And with the dove it heaves and stirs,  
Swelling its neck as she swelled hers!  
I woke; it was the midnight hour,   555  
The [[motifs/clock|clock]] was echoing in the tower;  
But though my slumber was gone by,  
This dream it would not pass away —  
It seems to live upon my eye!  
And thence I vowed this [[concepts/self|self]]-same day   560  
With music strong and saintly song  
To wander through the forest bare,  
Lest aught unholy loiter there.’  
Thus Bracy said: the Baron, the while,  
Half-listening heard him with a smile;   565  
Then turned to Lady [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]],  
His eyes made up of [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] and [[motifs/love|love]];  
And said in courtly accents fine,  
‘Sweet maid, Lord Roland’s beauteous dove,  
With arms more strong than harp or song,   570  
Thy sire and I will crush the snake!’  
He kissed her forehead as he spake,  
And [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] in maiden wise  
Casting down her large bright eyes,  
With blushing cheek and courtesy fine   575  
She turned her from Sir Leoline;  
Softly gathering up her train,  
That o’er her right arm fell again;  
And folded her arms across her chest,  
And couched her head upon her breast,   580  
And looked askance at [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] —  
Jesu, [[characters/maria|Maria]], shield her [[motifs/well|well]]!  
A snake’s small eye blinks dull and shy;  
And the lady’s eyes they shrunk in her head,  
Each shrunk up to a serpent’s eye,    585  
And with somewhat of malice, and more of dread,  
At [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] she looked askance! —  
One moment — and the sight was fled!  
But [[characters/christabel|Christabel]] in dizzy trance  
Stumbling on the unsteady ground   590  
Shuddered aloud, with a hissing sound;  
And [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]] again turned round,  
And like a thing, that sought relief,  
Full of [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] and full of grief,  
She rolled her large bright eyes divine   595  
Wildly on Sir Leoline.  
The maid, alas! her thoughts are gone,  
She nothing sees — no sight but one!  
The maid, devoid of guile and sin,  
I know not how, in fearful wise,   600  
So deeply had she drunken in  
That look, those shrunken serpent eyes,  
That all her features were resigned  
To this sole image in her mind:  
And passively did imitate    605  
That look of dull and treacherous hate!  
And thus she stood, in dizzy trance,  
Still picturing that look askance  
With forced unconscious sympathy  
Full before her father’s view —— 610  
As far as such a look could be  
In eyes so [[archetypes/innocent|innocent]] and blue!  
And when the trance was o’er, the maid  
Paused awhile, and inly prayed:  
Then falling at the Baron’s feet,   615  
‘By my mother’s [[motifs/soul|soul]] do I entreat  
That thou this woman send away!’  
She said: and more she could not say:  
For what she knew she could not tell,  
O’er-mastered by the mighty spell.    620  
Why is thy cheek so wan and wild,  
Sir Leoline? Thy only [[archetypes/child|child]]  
Lies at thy feet, thy joy, thy [[concepts/pride|pride]],  
So fair, so [[archetypes/innocent|innocent]], so mild;  
The same, for whom thy lady died!   625  
O by the pangs of her dear mother  
Think thou no evil of thy [[archetypes/child|child]]!  
For her, and thee, and for no other,  
She prayed the moment ere she died:  
Prayed that the babe for whom she died,   630  
Might prove her dear lord’s joy and [[concepts/pride|pride]]!  
That [[motifs/prayer|prayer]] her deadly pangs beguiled,  
Sir Leoline!  
And wouldst thou wrong thy only [[archetypes/child|child]],  
Her [[archetypes/child|child]] and thine?   635  
Within the Baron’s heart and brain  
If thoughts, like these, had any share,  
They only swelled his rage and [[motifs/pain|pain]],  
And did but work confusion there.  
His heart was cleft with [[motifs/pain|pain]] and rage,   640  
His cheeks they quivered, his eyes were wild,  
Dishonoured thus in his old age;  
Dishonoured by his only [[archetypes/child|child]],  
And all his hospitality  
To the wronged daughter of his friend   645  
By more than woman’s [[concepts/jealousy|jealousy]]  
Brought thus to a disgraceful end —  
He rolled his eye with stern regard  
Upon the gentle minstrel bard,  
And said in tones abrupt, austere — 650  
‘Why, Bracy! dost thou loiter here?  
I bade thee hence!’ The bard obeyed;  
And turning from his own sweet maid,  
The agéd knight, Sir Leoline,  
Led forth the lady [[characters/geraldine|Geraldine]]!   655

[[characters/christabel|CHRISTABEL]] THE CONCLUSION TO PART II  
A little [[archetypes/child|child]], a limber elf,  
Singing, dancing to itself,  
A fairy thing with red round cheeks,  
That always finds, and never seeks,  
Makes such a vision to the sight   660  
As fills a father’s eyes with light;  
And pleasures flow in so thick and fast  
Upon his heart, that he at last  
Must needs express his [[motifs/love|love]]’s excess  
With words of unmeant bitterness.   665  
Perhaps ‘tis pretty to force together  
Thoughts so all unlike each other;  
To mutter and mock a broken charm,  
To dally with wrong that does no harm.  
Perhaps ‘tis tender too and pretty    670  
At each wild word to feel within  
A sweet recoil of [[motifs/love|love]] and pity.  
And what, if in a world of sin  
(O sorrow and shame should this be true!)  
Such giddiness of heart and brain   675  
Comes seldom save from rage and [[motifs/pain|pain]],  
So talks as it’s most used to do.  
1801.
