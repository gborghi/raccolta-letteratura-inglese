THE RAPE OF [[characters/lucrece|LUCRECE]]  
DEDICATING [[characters/venus|Venus]] and [[characters/adonis|Adonis]] to the Earl of Southampton in 1953, Shakespeare promised, if the poem pleased, to ‘take advantage of all idle hours’ to [[concepts/honour|honour]] the Earl with ‘some graver labour’. The Rape of [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]], also dedicated to Southampton, was entered in the Stationers’ Register on May 1594, and printed in the same year. The warmth of the dedication suggests that the Earl was by then a friend as [[motifs/well|well]] as a patron.  
Like [[characters/venus|Venus]] and [[characters/adonis|Adonis]], The Rape of [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] is an erotic narrative based on Ovid, but this [[motifs/time|time]] the subject matter is historical, the tone tragic. The events took place in 509 BC, and were already legendary at the [[motifs/time|time]] of the first surviving account, by Livy in his [[concepts/history|history]] of Rome published between 27 and 27 5 BC. Shakespeare’s main source was Ovid’s Fasti, but he seems also to have known Livy’s and other accounts.  
Historically, Lucretia’s rape had political consequences. Her ravisher, [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]], was a member of the tyrannical ruling family of Rome. During the siege of Ardea, a group of noblemen boasted of their wives’ virtue, and rode [[motifs/home|home]] to test them; only [[characters/collatine|Collatine]]’s wife, Lucretia, lived up to her husband’s claims, and Sextus Tarquinius was attracted to her. Failing to seduce her, he raped her and returned to Rome. Lucretia committed [[concepts/suicide|suicide]], and her husband’s friend, [[characters/lucius|Lucius]] Junius [[characters/brutus|Brutus]], used the occasion as an opportunity to rouse the Roman people against Tarquinius’ rule and to constitute themselves a republic.  
Shakespeare concentrates on the private side of the story; [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] is lusting after [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] in the poem’s opening lines, and the ending devotes only a few lines to the consequence of her [[concepts/suicide|suicide]]. As in [[characters/venus|Venus]] and [[characters/adonis|Adonis]], Shakespeare makes a little narrative material go a long way. At first, the focus is on [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]]; after he has threatened [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]], it swings over to her. The opening sequence, with its marvellously dramatic account of [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]]’s tormented state of mind as he approaches [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]’s chamber, is the more intense. [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] disappears from the action soon after the rape, when [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] delivers herself of a long complaint, apostrophizing [[motifs/night|night]], opportunity, and [[motifs/time|time]] and cursing [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] with rhetorical fervour, before deciding to kill herself. After summoning her husband, she seeks consolation in a painting of Troy which is described (1373-1442) in lines indebted to the first and second books of Virgil’s Aeneid and to Book 13 of Ovid’s Metamorphoses. After she dies, her husband and father mourn, but [[characters/brutus|Brutus]] calls for deeds not words, and determines on [[concepts/revenge|revenge]]. The last lines of the poem look forward to the banishment of the Tarquins, but nothing is said of the establishment of a republic.  
Like [[characters/venus|Venus]] and [[characters/adonis|Adonis]], [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]], initially popular (with six editions in Shakespeare’s lifetime and another three by 1655), was later neglected. Coleridge admired it, and more recent criticism has recognized in it a profoundly dramatic quality combined with, if sometimes dissipated by, a remarkable force of rhetoric. The writing of the poem seems to have been a formative experience for Shakespeare. In it he not only laid the basis for his later plays on [[historical-references/roman-history|Roman history]], but also explored themes that were to figure prominently in his later work. This is especially apparent in the portrayal of a man who ‘still pursues his [[concepts/fear|fear]]’ (308), the relentless power of [[concepts/self|self]]-destructive evil that Shakespeare remembered when he made [[characters/macbeth|Macbeth]], on his way to murder [[characters/duncan|Duncan]], speak of ‘withered murder’ which, ‘With [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]]’s ravishing strides, towards his design ǀ Moves like a [[archetypes/ghost|ghost]]’.  
TO THE RIGHT HONOURABLE HENRY WRIOTHESLEY, EARL OF SOUTHAMPTON AND BARON OF TITCHFIELD  

The [[motifs/love|love]] I dedicate to your lordship is without end, whereof this pamphlet without beginning is but a superfluous moiety. The warrant I have of your honourable disposition, not the worth of my untutored lines, makes it assured of acceptance. What I have done is yours; what I have to do is yours, being part in all I have, devoted yours. Were my worth greater my duty would show greater, meantime, as it is, it is bound to your lordship, to whom I wish long life still lengthened with all happiness.  
Your lordship’s in all duty,  
William Shakespeare  

THE ARGUMENT  

[[characters/lucius|Lucius]] Tarquinius (for his excessive [[concepts/pride|pride]] surnamed Superbus), after he had caused his own father-in-law Servius Tullius to be cruelly murdered, and, contrary to the Roman laws and customs, not requiring or staying for the people’s suffrages had possessed himself of the kingdom, went accompanied with his sons and other noblemen of Rome to besiege Ardea, during which siege the principal men of the army meeting one evening at the tent of Sextus Tarquinius, the [[archetypes/king|King]]’s son, in their discourses after supper everyone commended the virtues of his own wife, among whom Collatinus extolled the incomparable chastity of his wife, Lucretia. In that pleasant [[Humour|humour]] they all posted to Rome, and, intending by their secret and sudden arrival to make trial of that which everyone had before avouched, only Collatinus finds his wife (though it were late in the [[motifs/night|night]]) spinning amongst her maids. The other ladies were all found dancing, and revelling, or in several disports. Whereupon the noblemen yielded Collatinus the victory and his wife the fame. At that [[motifs/time|time]] Sextus Tarquinius, being enflamed with [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]’ [[concepts/beauty|beauty]], yet smothering his passions for the present, departed with the rest back to the camp, from whence he shortly after privily withdrew himself and was, according to his estate, royally entertained and lodged by [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] at Collatium. The same [[motifs/night|night]] he treacherously stealeth into her chamber, violently ravished her, and early in the morning speedeth away. [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]], in this lamentable plight, hastily dispatcheth messengers—one to Rome for her father, another to the camp for [[characters/collatine|Collatine]]. They came, the one accompanied with Junius [[characters/brutus|Brutus]], the other with Publius Valerius, and, finding [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] attired in mourning habit, demanded the cause of her sorrow. She, first taking an oath of them for her [[concepts/revenge|revenge]], revealed the actor and whole manner of his dealing, and withal suddenly stabbed herself. Which done, with one consent they all vowed to root out the whole hated family of the Tarquins, and, bearing the dead [[concepts/body|body]] to Rome, [[characters/brutus|Brutus]] acquainted the people with the doer and manner of the vile deed, with a bitter invective against the tyranny of the [[archetypes/king|King]]; wherewith the people were so moved that with one consent and a general acclamation the Tarquins were all exiled and the state [[government]] changed from kings to consuls.  

The Rape of [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]  
From the besieged Ardea all in post,  
Borne by the trustless wings of false [[motifs/desire|desire]],  
Lust-breathèd [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] [[motifs/leaves|leaves]] the Roman host  
And to Collatium bears the lightless [[motifs/fire|fire]]  
Which, in pale embers hid, lurks to aspire  
And girdle with embracing flames the waist  
Of [[characters/collatine|Collatine]]’s fair [[motifs/love|love]], [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] the chaste.  
Haply that name of chaste unhapp’ly set  
This bateless edge on his keen appetite,  
When [[characters/collatine|Collatine]] unwisely did not let  
To praise the clear unmatched red and white  
Which triumphed in that sky of his delight,  
Where mortal [[motifs/stars|stars]] as bright as heaven’s beauties  
With pure aspects did him peculiar duties.  
For he the [[motifs/night|night]] before in [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]]’s tent  
Unlocked the treasure of his happy state,  
What priceless wealth the heavens had him lent  
In the possession of his beauteous mate,  
Reck’ning his fortune at such high-proud rate  
That kings might be espoused to more fame,  
But [[archetypes/king|king]] nor peer to such a peerless dame.  
O happiness enjoyed but of a few,  
And, if possessed, as soon decayed and done  
As is the morning’s silver melting dew  
Against the golden splendour of the sun,  
An expired date cancelled ere [[motifs/well|well]] begun!  
[[concepts/honour|Honour]] and [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] in the owner’s arms  
Are weakly fortressed from a world of harms.  
[[concepts/beauty|Beauty]] itself doth of itself persuade  
The eyes of men without an orator.  
What needeth then apology be made  
To set forth that which is so singular?  
Or why is [[characters/collatine|Collatine]] the publisher  
Of that rich [[motifs/jewel|jewel]] he should keep unknown  
From thievish ears, because it is his own?  
Perchance his boast of [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]’ sov’reignty  
Suggested this proud issue of a [[archetypes/king|king]],  
For by our ears our hearts oft tainted be.  
Perchance that envy of so rich a thing,  
Braving compare, disdainfully did sting  
His high-pitched thoughts, that meaner men should vaunt  
That golden hap which their superiors want.  
But some untimely thought did instigate  
His all-too-timeless [[characters/speed|speed]], if none of those.  
His [[concepts/honour|honour]], his affairs, his friends, his state  
Neglected all, with swift intent he goes  
To quench the coal which in his liver glows.  
O rash false heat, wrapped in repentant cold,  
Thy hasty [[motifs/spring|spring]] still blasts and ne’er grows old!  
When at Collatium this false lord arrived,  
[[motifs/well|Well]] was he welcomed by the Roman dame,  
Within whose face [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] and virtue strived  
Which of them both should underprop her fame.  
When virtue bragged, [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] would blush for shame;  
When [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] boasted blushes, in despite  
Virtue would stain that or with silver white.  
But [[concepts/beauty|beauty]], in that white entitulèd  
From [[characters/venus|Venus]]’ doves, doth challenge that fair field.  
Then virtue claims from [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] [[concepts/beauty|beauty]]’s red,  
Which virtue gave the [[topoi/golden-age|golden age]] to gild  
Their silver cheeks, and called it then their shield,  
Teaching them thus to use it in the fight:  
When shame assailed, the red should fence the  
white.  
This heraldry in [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]’ face was seen,  
Argued by [[concepts/beauty|beauty]]’s red and virtue’s white.  
Of either’s colour was the other [[characters/queen|queen]],  
Proving from world’s minority their right.  
Yet their [[concepts/ambition|ambition]] makes them still to fight,  
The sovereignty of either being so great  
That oft they interchange each other’s seat.  
This silent war of lilies and of roses  
Which [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] viewed in her fair face’s field  
In their pure ranks his traitor eye encloses,  
Where, lest between them both it should be killed,  
The coward captive vanquished doth yield  
To those two armies that would let him go  
Rather than triumph in so false a foe.  
Now thinks he that her husband’s shallow tongue,  
The niggard prodigal that praised her so,  
In that high task hath done her [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] wrong,  
Which far exceeds his barren skill to show.  
Therefore that praise which [[characters/collatine|Collatine]] doth owe  
Enchanted [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] answers with surmise  
In silent [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] of still-gazing eyes.  
This earthly saint adored by this devil  
Little suspecteth the false worshipper,  
For unstained thoughts do seldom dream on evil.  
[[motifs/birds|Birds]] never limed no secret bushes [[concepts/fear|fear]],  
So guiltless she securely gives good cheer  
And reverent welcome to her princely guest,  
Whose inward ill no outward harm expressed,  
For that he coloured with his high estate,  
Hiding base sin in pleats of majesty,  
That nothing in him seemed inordinate  
Save sometime too much [[concepts/wonder|wonder]] of his eye,  
Which, having all, all could not satisfy,  
But poorly rich so wanteth in his store  
That, cloyed with much, he pineth still for more.  
But she that never coped with stranger eyes  
Could pick no meaning from their parling looks,  
Nor read the subtle shining secrecies  
Writ in the glassy margins of such books.  
She touched no unknown baits nor feared no hooks,  
Nor could she moralize his wanton sight  
More than his eyes were opened to the light.  
He stories to her ears her husband’s fame  
Won in the fields of fruitful [[settings/italy|Italy]],  
And decks with praises [[characters/collatine|Collatine]]’s high name  
Made glorious by his manly chivalry  
With bruised arms and wreaths of victory.  
Her joy with heaved-up hand she doth express,  
And wordless so greets heaven for his [[success]].  
Far from the purpose of his coming thither  
He makes excuses for his being there.  
No cloudy show of stormy blust’ring weather  
Doth yet in his fair welkin once appear  
Till sable [[motifs/night|night]], mother of dread and [[concepts/fear|fear]],  
Upon the world dim darkness doth display  
And in her vaulty [[motifs/prison|prison]] stows the day.  
For then is [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] brought unto his bed,  
Intending weariness with heavy sprite;  
For after supper long he questioned  
With modest [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]], and wore out the [[motifs/night|night]].  
Now leaden slumber with life’s strength doth fight,  
And everyone to rest himself betakes  
Save thieves, and cares, and troubled minds that  
wakes.  
As one of which doth [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] lie revolving  
The sundry dangers of his will’s obtaining,  
Yet ever to obtain his will resolving,  
Though weak-built hopes persuade him to abstaining.  
[[concepts/despair|Despair]] to gain doth traffic oft for gaining,  
And when great treasure is the meed proposed,  
Though [[motifs/death|death]] be adjunct, there’s no [[motifs/death|death]] supposed.  
Those that much covet are with gain so fond  
That what they have not, that which they possess,  
They scatter and unloose it from their bond,  
And so by hoping more they have but less,  
Or, gaining more, the profit of excess  
Is but to surfeit and such griefs sustain  
That they prove bankrupt in this poor-rich gain.  
The aim of all is but to [[characters/nurse|nurse]] the life  
With [[concepts/honour|honour]], wealth, and ease in waning age,  
And in this aim there is such thwarting strife  
That one for all, or all for one, we gage,  
As life for [[concepts/honour|honour]] in fell battle’s rage,  
[[concepts/honour|Honour]] for wealth; and oft that wealth doth cost  
The [[motifs/death|death]] of all, and all together lost.  
So that, in vent’ring ill, we leave to be  
The things we are for that which we expect,  
And this ambitious foul infirmity  
In having much, torments us with defect  
Of that we have; so then we do neglect  
The thing we have, and all for want of wit  
Make something nothing by augmenting it.  
Such hazard now must doting [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]] make,  
Pawning his [[concepts/honour|honour]] to obtain his lust,  
And for himself himself he must forsake.  
Then where is [[concepts/truth|truth]] if there be no [[concepts/self|self]]-trust?  
When shall he think to find a stranger just  
When he himself himself confounds, betrays  
To sland’rous tongues and wretched hateful days?  
Now stole upon the [[motifs/time|time]] the dead of [[motifs/night|night]]  
When heavy [[motifs/sleep|sleep]] had closed up mortal eyes.  
No comfortable star did lend his light,  
No noise but owls’ and wolves’ [[motifs/death|death]]-boding cries  
Now serves the season, that they may surprise  
The silly lambs. Pure thoughts are dead and still,  
While lust and murder wakes to stain and kill.  
And now this lustful lord leapt from his bed,  
Throwing his mantle rudely o‘er his arm,  
Is madly tossed between [[motifs/desire|desire]] and dread.  
Th’one sweetly flatters, th’other feareth harm,  
But honest [[concepts/fear|fear]], bewitched with lust’s foul charm,  
Doth too-too oft betake him to retire,  
Beaten away by brainsick rude [[motifs/desire|desire]].  
His falchion on a flint he softly smiteth,  
That from the cold stone sparks of [[motifs/fire|fire]] do fly,  
Whereat a waxen torch forthwith he lighteth,  
Which must be lodestar to his lustful eye,  
And to the flame thus speaks advisedly:  
‘As from this cold flint I enforced this [[motifs/fire|fire]],  
So [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]] must I force to my [[motifs/desire|desire]].’  
Here pale with [[concepts/fear|fear]] he doth premeditate  
The dangers of his loathsome enterprise,  
And in his inward mind he doth [[debate]]  
What following sorrow may on this arise.  
Then, looking scornfully, he doth despise  
His naked armour of still-slaughtered lust,  
And justly thus controls his thoughts unjust:  
‘Fair torch, burn out thy light, and lend it not  
To darken her whose light excelleth thine;  
And die, unhallowed thoughts, before you blot  
With your uncleanness that which is divine.  
Offer pure incense to so pure a shrine.  
Let fair humanity abhor the deed  
That spots and stains [[motifs/love|love]]’s modest [[motifs/snow|snow]]-white weed.  
‘O shame to knighthood and to shining arms!  
O foul dishonour to my household’s [[motifs/grave|grave]]!  
O impious act including all foul harms!  
A martial man to be soft fancy’s slave!  
True valour still a true respect should have;  
Then my digression is so vile, so base,  
That it will live engraven in my face.  
‘Yea, though I die the scandal will survive  
And be an eyesore in my golden coat.  
Some loathsome dash the herald will contrive  
To cipher me how fondly I did dote,  
That my posterity, shamed with the note,  
Shall curse my bones and hold it for no sin  
To wish that I their father had not been.  
‘What win I if I gain the thing I seek?  
[[A Dream|A dream]], a breath, a froth of fleeting joy.  
Who buys a minute’s mirth to wail a week,  
Or sells [[concepts/eternity|eternity]] to get a toy?  
For one sweet grape who will the vine destroy?  
Or what fond beggar, but to touch the [[motifs/crown|crown]],  
Would with the sceptre straight be strucken down?  
‘If Collatinus dream of my intent  
Will he not wake, and in a desp’rate rage  
Post hither this vile purpose to prevent?—  
This siege that hath engirt his [[motifs/marriage|marriage]],  
This blur to youth, this sorrow to the sage,  
This dying virtue, this surviving shame,  
Whose crime will bear an ever-during blame.  
‘O what excuse can my invention make  
When thou shalt charge me with so black a deed?  
Will not my tongue be mute, my frail joints shake,  
Mine eyes forgo their light, my false heart bleed?  
The [[concepts/guilt|guilt]] being great, the [[concepts/fear|fear]] doth still exceed,  
And extreme [[concepts/fear|fear]] can neither fight nor fly,  
But coward-like with trembling terror die.  
‘Had Collatinus killed my son or sire,  
Or lain in ambush to betray my life,  
Or were he not my dear friend, this [[motifs/desire|desire]]  
Might have excuse to work upon his wife  
As in [[concepts/revenge|revenge]] or quittal of such strife.  
But as he is my kinsman, my dear friend,  
The shame and fault finds no excuse nor end.  
‘Shameful it is—ay, if the fact be known.  
Hateful it is—there is no hate in loving.  
I’ll beg her [[motifs/love|love]]—but she is not her own.  
The worst is but denial and reproving;  
My will is strong past reason’s weak removing.  
Who fears a sentence or an old man’s saw  
Shall by a painted cloth be kept in awe.’  
Thus graceless holds he disputation  
‘Tween frozen [[concepts/conscience|conscience]] and hot-burning will,  
And with good thoughts makes dispensation,  
Urging the worser sense for vantage still;  
Which in a moment doth confound and kill  
All pure effects, and doth so far proceed  
That what is vile shows like a virtuous. deed.  
Quoth he, ‘She took me kindly by the hand,  
And gazed for tidings in my eager eyes,  
Fearing some hard news from the warlike band  
Where her beloved Collatinus lies.  
O how her [[concepts/fear|fear]] did make her colour rise!  
First red as roses that on lawn we lay,  
Then white as lawn, the roses took away.  
‘And how her hand, in my hand being locked,  
Forced it to tremble with her loyal [[concepts/fear|fear]],  
Which struck her sad, and then it faster rocked  
Until her husband’s welfare she did hear,  
Whereat she smiled with so sweet a cheer  
That had Narcissus seen her as she stood  
[[concepts/self|Self]]-[[motifs/love|love]] had never drowned him in the flood.  
‘Why hunt I then for colour or excuses?  
All orators are dumb when [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] pleadeth.  
Poor wretches have remorse in poor abuses;  
[[motifs/love|Love]] thrives not in the heart that shadows dreadeth;  
Affection is my captain, and he leadeth,  
And when his gaudy banner is displayed,  
The coward fights, and will not be dismayed.  
‘Then childish [[concepts/fear|fear]] avaunt, debating die,  
Respect and reason wait on wrinkled age!  
My heart shall never countermand mine eye,  
Sad pause and deep regard beseems the sage.  
My part is youth, and beats these from the stage.  
[[motifs/desire|Desire]] my pilot is, [[concepts/beauty|beauty]] my prize.  
Then who fears sinking where such treasure lies?’  
As corn o‘ergrown by weeds, so heedful [[concepts/fear|fear]]  
Is almost choked by unresisted lust.  
Away he steals, with open list’ning ear,  
Full of foul [[concepts/hope|hope]] and full of fond mistrust,  
Both which as servitors to the unjust  
So [[motifs/cross|cross]] him with their opposite [[Persuasion|persuasion]]  
That now he vows a league, and now invasion.  
Within his thought her heavenly image sits,  
And in the selfsame seat sits [[characters/collatine|Collatine]].  
That eye which looks on her confounds his wits,  
That eye which him beholds, as more divine,  
Unto a view so false will not incline,  
But with a pure appeal seeks to the heart,  
Which once corrupted, takes the worser part,  
And therein heartens up his servile powers  
Who, flattered by their leader’s jocund show,  
Stuff up his lust as minutes fill up hours,  
And as their captain, so their [[concepts/pride|pride]] doth grow,  
Paying more slavish tribute than they owe.  
By reprobate [[motifs/desire|desire]] thus madly led  
The Roman lord marcheth to [[characters/lucrece|Lucrece]]’ bed.  
The locks between her chamber and his will,  
Each one by him enforced, retires his ward;  
But as they open they all rate his ill,  
Which drives the creeping thief to some regard.  
The threshold grates the door to have him heard,  
[[motifs/night|Night]]-wand’ring weasels shriek to see him there.  
They fright him, yet he still pursues his [[concepts/fear|fear]].  
As each unwilling portal yields him way,  
Through little vents and crannies of the place  
The [[motifs/wind|wind]] wars with his torch to make him stay,  
And blows the smoke of it into his face,  
Extinguishing his conduct in this case.  
But his hot heart, which fond [[motifs/desire|desire]] doth scorch,  
Puffs forth another [[motifs/wind|wind]] that fires the torch,  
And being lighted, by the light he spies  
Lucretia’s glove wherein her needle sticks.  
He takes it from the rushes where it lies,  
And gripping it, the needle his finger pricks,  
As who should say ‘This glove to wanton tricks  
Is not inured. Return again in haste.  
Thou seest our mistress’ ornaments are chaste.’  
But all these poor forbiddings could not stay him;  
He in the worst sense consters their denial.  
The doors, the [[motifs/wind|wind]], the glove that did delay him  
He takes for accidental things of trial,  
Or as those bars which stop the hourly dial,  
Who with a ling’ring stay his course doth let  
Till every minute pays the hour his debt.  
‘So, so,’ quoth he, ‘these lets attend the [[motifs/time|time]],  
Like little frosts that sometime threat the [[motifs/spring|spring]]  
To add a more rejoicing to the prime,  
And give the sneapèd [[motifs/birds|birds]] more cause to sing.  
[[motifs/pain|Pain]] pays the income of each precious thing.  
Huge rocks, high winds, strong pirates, shelves, and sands  
The merchant fears, ere rich at [[motifs/home|home]] he lands.’  
Now is he come unto the chamber door  
That shuts him from the heaven of his thought,  
Which with a yielding latch, and with no more,  
Hath barred him from the blessed thing he sought.  
So from himself impiety hath wrought  
That for his prey to pray he doth begin,  
As if the heavens should countenance his sin.  
But in the midst of his unfruitful [[motifs/prayer|prayer]]  
Having solicited th‘eternal power  
That his foul thoughts might compass his fair fair,  
And they would stand auspicious to the hour,  
Even there he starts. Quoth he, ‘I must deflower.  
The powers to whom I pray abhor this fact;  
How can they then assist me in the act?  
‘Then [[motifs/love|love]] and fortune be my gods, my guide!  
My will is backed with resolution.  
Thoughts are but [[motifs/dreams|dreams]] till their effects be tried;  
The blackest sin is cleared with absolution.

Of spirit, life, and bold audacity.  
Such harmless creatures have a true respect  
To talk in deeds, while others saucily  
Promise more [[characters/speed|speed]], but do it leisurely.  
Even so this pattern of the worn-out age  
Pawned honest looks, but laid no words to gage.  
His kindled duty kindled her mistrust,  
That two red fires in both their faces blazed.  
She thought he blushed as knowing [[characters/tarquin|Tarquin]]’s lust,  
And blushing with him, wistly on him gazed.  
Her earnest eye did make him more amazed.

At last she calls to mind where hangs a piece

Many a dry drop seemed a weeping tear

In youth, quick bearing and dexterity;

At last she sees a wretched image bound,

His face, though full of cares, yet showed content.  
Onward to Troy with the blunt swains he goes,  
So mild that patience seemed to scorn his woes.

To hide deceit and give the harmless show

A brow unbent that seemed to welcome woe;  
Cheeks neither red nor pale, but mingled so

Into so bright a day such blackfaced storms,  
Or blot with hell-born sin such saint-like forms.

The credulous old Priam after slew;

BY WILLIAM SHAKESPEARE AND OTHERS  
FIRST heard of in the Stationers’ Register for I December 1595, The Reign of [[archetypes/king|King]] Edward the Third was published anonymously in the following year, with the statement that it had been ‘sundry times played about the [[motifs/city|City]] of [[settings/london|London]]’. As was usual, there are no act and scene divisions; we divide it only into scenes. It could have been written at any [[motifs/time|time]] between the Armada of 1588 and 1595. Like other plays of this period, including Shakespeare’s I [[characters/henry-vi|Henry VI]], [[characters/richard-ii|Richard II]], and [[characters/king-john|King John]], it is composed entirely in verse, much of it formal and rhetorical in style. Shakespeare seems at least to have known the play, since a historical error placing [[archetypes/king|King]] David of Scotland among Edward’s prisoners at Calais (10.40-56, 18.63.1) occurs also in [[characters/henry-v|Henry V]] (1.2.160-2). The play’s omission from the First Folio is good presumptive evidence against Shakespeare’s sole authorship. It was, however, attributed to him in a totally unreliable catalogue of 1656; better worth taking seriously is the attribution to Shakespeare by Edward Capell, expressed in 1760. Since then various scholars have proposed that Shakespeare wrote at least the scenes involving the [[characters/countess-of-salisbury|Countess of Salisbury]] (Scene 2, Scene 3). When the [[settings/oxford|Oxford]] edition first appeared, its editors remarked that ‘if we had attempted a thorough reinvestigation of candidates for inclusion in the early dramatic canon, it would have begun with [[characters/edward-iii|Edward III]]’ (Textual Companion, p. 137). Since then intensive application of stylometric and other tests of authorship, along with an increased willingness to acknowledge that Shakespeare collaborated with other writers, especially early and late in his career, has strengthened the case for including it among the collected works. We believe, however, that Shakespeare was responsible only for Scene 2 (from the entrance of [[characters/edward-iii|Edward III]]) and Scene 3, and for Scene 12 (which includes a [[characters/hamlet|Hamlet]]-like meditation on the inevitability of [[motifs/death|death]]), and possibly Scene 13, and that one or more other authors wrote the rest of the play.  
The play’s treatment of [[concepts/history|history]], deriving principally from Lord Berners’s [[forms/translation|translation]] (1535) of Froissart’s Chronicles, is loose. As with [[characters/henry-v|Henry V]], the opening episode shows the English [[archetypes/king|king]] seeking reassurance about his claims to the throne of France. Lorraine’s subsequent demand that Edward swear allegiance to the French [[archetypes/king|king]] meets with derision. Attention turns to England’s relations with Scotland, where [[archetypes/king|King]] David, France’s ally, has besieged the [[settings/castle|castle]] of Roxburgh, imprisoning the [[characters/countess-of-salisbury|Countess of Salisbury]]. Edward instructs his son Edward (Ned) the Black Prince to raise troops against France; Edward himself will march against the Scots. At Roxburgh the [[archetypes/king|King]] rescues and attempts to seduce the Countess, who is also desired by [[archetypes/king|King]] David and Sir William Douglas. In the principal scenes ascribed to Shakespeare, the enraptured [[archetypes/king|King]] expresses his passion in attractively lyrical verse recalling that of The Two Gentlemen of [[settings/verona|Verona]]. He attempts to persuade the [[characters/earl-of-warwick|Earl of Warwick]], the Countess’s father, to further his suit, but the Countess, virtuous (and married), repudiates his adulterous desires, threatening to kill herself if he persists. Penitent, he reverts to the French conflict. This, presented in episodes of ambitious rhetoric rather than of violent action, reaches its first climax in young Edward’s conquest over the [[archetypes/king|King]] of Bohemia, for which his father knights him. Edward’s [[characters/queen|queen]], Philippa, who with her followers has overcome the Scots, joins him, and persuades him to show [[concepts/mercy|mercy]] to the burghers of the besieged town of Calais. Young Edward, believed dead, is revealed as the conqueror of the French, and the play ends with a jingoistic English triumph. IL RATTO DI [[characters/lucrece|LUCREZIA]]  
DEDICANDO [[characters/venus|Venere]] e [[characters/adonis|Adone]] al conte di Southampton nel 1593, Shakespeare promise, se il poema fosse piaciuto, di ‘approfittare di tutte le ore d’ozio’ per [[concepts/honour|onorare]] il conte con ‘qualche più grave fatica’. Il Ratto di [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], anch’esso dedicato a Southampton, fu registrato nello Stationers’ Register nel maggio 1594, e stampato nello stesso anno. Il calore della dedica lascia intendere che il conte era ormai un amico oltre [[motifs/well|che]] un patrono.  
Come [[characters/venus|Venere]] e [[characters/adonis|Adone]], Il Ratto di [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]] è una narrazione erotica fondata su Ovidio, ma questa [[motifs/time|volta]] la materia è storica, il tono tragico. Gli eventi ebbero luogo nel 509 a.C., ed erano già leggendari al [[motifs/time|tempo]] del primo resoconto superstite, quello di Livio nella sua [[concepts/history|storia]] di Roma pubblicata fra il 27 e il 25 a.C. La fonte principale di Shakespeare furono i Fasti d’Ovidio, ma pare che conoscesse anche il racconto di Livio e altri ancora.  
Storicamente, lo stupro di Lucrezia ebbe conseguenze politiche. Il suo violentatore, [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]], era membro della tirannica famiglia regnante di Roma. Durante l’assedio di Ardea, un gruppo di nobili si vantò della virtù delle proprie mogli, e cavalcò fino a [[motifs/home|casa]] per metterle alla prova; solo la moglie di [[characters/collatine|Collatino]], Lucrezia, fu all’altezza di quanto il marito affermava, e Sesto Tarquinio ne fu attratto. Non riuscendo a sedurla, la violentò e tornò a Roma. Lucrezia si diede la [[concepts/suicide|morte]], e l’amico del marito, [[characters/lucius|Lucio]] Giunio [[characters/brutus|Bruto]], si valse dell’occasione per sollevare il popolo romano contro il dominio dei Tarquini e costituirlo in repubblica.  
Shakespeare si concentra sul lato privato della vicenda; [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]] arde già di desiderio per [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]] nei versi d’apertura del poema, e la chiusa dedica pochi versi soltanto alle conseguenze del suo [[concepts/suicide|suicidio]]. Come in [[characters/venus|Venere]] e [[characters/adonis|Adone]], Shakespeare fa rendere moltissimo a pochissima materia narrativa. Dapprima il fuoco è su [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]]; dopo che egli ha minacciato [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], si sposta su di lei. La sequenza iniziale, col suo resoconto mirabilmente drammatico dello stato tormentato della mente di [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]] mentre s’avvicina alla camera di [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], è la più intensa. [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]] scompare dall’azione poco dopo lo stupro, quando [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]] si scioglie in un lungo lamento, apostrofando la [[motifs/night|notte]], l’occasione e il [[motifs/time|tempo]] e maledicendo [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]] con fervore retorico, prima di risolversi a uccidersi. Dopo aver chiamato il marito, cerca conforto in un dipinto di Troia che è descritto (1373-1442) in versi debitori dei libri primo e secondo dell’Eneide di Virgilio e del libro XIII delle Metamorfosi d’Ovidio. Dopo la sua morte, il marito e il padre la piangono, ma [[characters/brutus|Bruto]] chiede fatti e non parole, e si risolve alla [[concepts/revenge|vendetta]]. Gli ultimi versi del poema guardano all’esilio dei Tarquini, ma nulla si dice dell’istituzione d’una repubblica.  
Come [[characters/venus|Venere]] e [[characters/adonis|Adone]], anche [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], dapprima assai fortunata (con sei edizioni in vita di Shakespeare e altre tre entro il 1655), fu più tardi trascurata. Coleridge l’ammirò, e la critica più recente vi ha riconosciuto una qualità profondamente drammatica unita a una notevole forza retorica, se pure talvolta da questa dispersa. La composizione del poema pare essere stata per Shakespeare un’esperienza formativa. In esso egli non solo gettò le basi dei suoi drammi posteriori sulla [[historical-references/roman-history|storia romana]], ma esplorò anche temi destinati a figurare largamente nella sua opera più tarda. Ciò è particolarmente evidente nel ritratto d’un uomo che ‘ancora insegue la sua [[concepts/fear|paura]]’ (308), la spietata potenza del male distruttore di [[concepts/self|sé]] che Shakespeare ricordò quando fece parlare [[characters/macbeth|Macbeth]], in cammino verso l’assassinio di [[characters/duncan|Duncan]], dell’‘assassinio avvizzito’ che, ‘Coi passi rapinosi di [[characters/tarquin|Tarquinio]], verso il suo disegno ǀ Muove come un [[archetypes/ghost|fantasma]]’.  
ALL’ONOREVOLISSIMO HENRY WRIOTHESLEY, CONTE DI SOUTHAMPTON E BARONE DI TITCHFIELD

L’ARGOMENTO  

[[characters/lucius|Lucio]] Tarquinio (per il suo eccessivo [[concepts/pride|orgoglio]] soprannominato Superbo), dopo aver fatto crudelmente assassinare il proprio suocero Servio Tullio e, contro le leggi e i costumi romani, essersi impadronito del regno senza richiedere né attendere i suffragi del popolo, andò accompagnato dai suoi figli e da altri nobili di Roma ad assediare Ardea; durante il quale assedio, ritrovandosi una sera i principali uomini dell’esercito nella tenda di Sesto Tarquinio, figlio del [[archetypes/king|Re]], nei loro discorsi dopo cena ciascuno lodò le virtù della propria moglie, e fra costoro Collatino esaltò l’incomparabile castità della sua sposa, Lucrezia. In quel piacevole [[Humour|umore]] corsero tutti a Roma, e, intendendo col loro segreto e improvviso arrivo far prova di ciò che ciascuno aveva prima affermato, il solo Collatino trova la moglie (benché fosse tarda [[motifs/night|notte]]) a filare fra le sue ancelle. Le altre dame furono trovate tutte a danzare, a far festa, o in vari passatempi. Onde i nobili concessero a Collatino la vittoria e alla sua sposa la fama. In quel [[motifs/time|tempo]] Sesto Tarquinio, infiammato da [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], dalla [[concepts/beauty|bellezza]] di lei, pur soffocando per allora le sue passioni, partì con gli altri di ritorno al campo, donde poco dopo si ritrasse di nascosto e fu, secondo il suo grado, regalmente accolto e alloggiato da [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]] a Collazia. Quella stessa [[motifs/night|notte]] egli s’insinua a tradimento nella camera di lei, la violenta con la forza, e la mattina presto s’affretta via. [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]], in questo lamentevole stato, spedisce in fretta messaggeri—uno a Roma per suo padre, un altro al campo per [[characters/collatine|Collatino]]. Vennero, l’uno accompagnato da Giunio [[characters/brutus|Bruto]], l’altro da Publio Valerio, e, trovando [[characters/lucrece|Lucrezia]] vestita a lutto, domandarono la cagione del suo dolore. Ella, prendendo prima da loro un giuramento per la sua [[concepts/revenge|vendetta]], rivelò l’autore e tutto il modo del suo operare, e insieme d’un tratto si trafisse. Il che fatto, di comune accordo giurarono tutti di sradicare l’intera odiata famiglia dei Tarquini, e, portato il [[concepts/body|corpo]] morto a Roma, [[characters/brutus|Bruto]] fece noti al popolo l’autore e il modo del vile atto, con un’aspra invettiva contro la tirannia del [[archetypes/king|Re]]; onde il popolo fu tanto commosso che di comune accordo e con generale acclamazione i Tarquini furono tutti esiliati e il [[government|governo]] dello stato mutato dai re ai consoli.
