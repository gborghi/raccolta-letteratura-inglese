| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Valentine]] |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | How use doth breed a habit in a man!<br>This shadowy [[Desert|desert]], unfrequented woods<br>I better brook than flourishing peopled towns.<br>Here can I sit [[Alone|alone]], unseen of any,<br>And to the [[Nightingale|nightingale]]’s complaining notes<br>Tune my distresses and record my woes.<br>O thou that dost inhabit in my breast,<br>Leave not the mansion so long tenantless<br>Lest, growing ruinous, the building fall<br>And leave no [[Memory|memory]] of what it was.<br>Repair me with thy presence, [[Silvia]].<br>Thou gentle nymph, cherish thy forlorn swain.<br>What hallooing and what stir is this today?<br>These are my mates, that make their wills their law,<br>Have some unhappy passenger in chase.<br>They [[Love|love]] me [[Well|well]], yet I have much to do<br>To keep them from uncivil outrages.<br>Withdraw thee, [[Valentine]]. Who’s this comes here?<br>He stands aside. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Proteus]], [[Silvia]], and [[Julia]] dressed as a pageboy |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Madam, this service I have done for you—<br>Though you respect not aught your servant doth—<br>To hazard life, and rescue you from him<br>That would have forced your [[Honour|honour]] and your [[Love|love]].<br>Vouchsafe me for my meed but one fair look.<br>A smaller boon than this I cannot beg,<br>And less than this I am sure you cannot give. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | (aside)<br>How like [[A Dream|a dream]] is this I see and hear!<br>[[Love]] lend me patience to forbear awhile. |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | O miserable, unhappy that I am! |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Unhappy were you, madam, ere I came.<br>But by my coming I have made you happy. |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | By thy approach thou mak’st me most unhappy. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | (aside)<br>And me, when he approacheth to your presence. |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | Had I been seized by a hungry [[Lion|lion]]<br>I would have been a breakfast to the beast<br>Rather than have false [[Proteus]] rescue me.<br>O heaven be judge how I [[Love|love]] [[Valentine]],<br>Whose life’s as tender to me as my [[Soul|soul]].<br>And full as much, for more there cannot be,<br>I do detest false perjured [[Proteus]].<br>Therefore be gone, solicit me no more. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | What dangerous action, stood it next to [[Death|death]],<br>Would I not undergo for one calm look!<br>O, ’tis the curse in [[Love|love]], and still approved,<br>When women cannot [[Love|love]] where they’re beloved. |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | When [[Proteus]] cannot [[Love|love]] where he’s beloved.<br>Read over [[Julia]]’s heart, thy first, best [[Love|love]],<br>For whose dear sake thou didst then rend thy [[Faith|faith]]<br>Into a thousand oaths, and all those oaths<br>Descended into perjury to [[Love|love]] me.<br>Thou hast no [[Faith|faith]] left now, unless thou’dst two,<br>And that’s far worse than none. Better have none<br>Than plural [[Faith|faith]], which is too much by one,<br>Thou counterfeit to thy true friend. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | In [[Love|love]]<br>Who respects friend? |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | All men but [[Proteus]]. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Nay, if the gentle spirit of moving words<br>Can no way change you to a milder form<br>I’ll woo you like a soldier, at arm’s end,<br>And [[Love|love]] you ’gainst the [[Nature|nature]] of [[Love|love]]: force ye. |
| [[Silvia|SILVIA]] | O heaven! |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | (assailing her) I’ll force thee yield to my [[Desire|desire]]. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | (coming forward)<br>Ruffian, let go that rude uncivil touch,<br>Thou friend of an ill fashion. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | [[Valentine]]! |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Thou common friend, that’s without [[Faith|faith]] or [[Love|love]],<br>For such is a friend now. Treacherous man,<br>Thou hast beguiled my hopes. Naught but mine eye<br>Could have persuaded me. Now I dare not say<br>I have one friend alive. Thou wouldst disprove me.<br>Who should be trusted, when one’s right hand<br>Is perjured to the bosom? [[Proteus]],<br>I am sorry I must never trust thee more,<br>But count the world a stranger for thy sake.<br>The private wound is deepest. O [[Time|time]] most accursed,<br>’Mongst all foes that a friend should be the worst! |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | My shame and [[Guilt|guilt]] confounds me.<br>Forgive me, [[Valentine]]. If hearty sorrow<br>Be a sufficient ransom for offence,<br>I tender’t here. I do as truly suffer<br>As e’er I did commit. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Then I am paid,<br>And once again I do receive thee honest.<br>Who by repentance is not satisfied<br>Is nor of heaven nor earth. For these are pleased;<br>By penitence th’ Eternal’s wrath’s appeased.<br>And that my [[Love|love]] may appear plain and free,<br>All that was mine in [[Silvia]] I give thee. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | O me unhappy!<br>She faints |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Look to the boy. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Why, boy!<br>Why wag, how now? What’s the matter? Look up. Speak. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | O good sir, my master charged me to deliver a ring to Madam [[Silvia]], which out of my neglect was never done. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Where is that ring, boy? |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | Here ’tis. This is it.<br>She gives [[Proteus]] the ring |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | How, let me see!<br>Why, this is the ring I gave to [[Julia]]. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | O, cry you [[Mercy|mercy]], sir, I have mistook.<br>She offers [[Proteus]] another ring<br>This is the ring you sent to [[Silvia]]. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | But how cam’st thou by this ring? At my depart<br>I gave this unto [[Julia]]. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | And [[Julia]] herself did give it me,<br>And [[Julia]] herself hath brought it hither. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | How? [[Julia]]? |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | Behold her that gave aim to all thy oaths<br>And entertained ’em deeply in her heart.<br>How oft hast thou with perjury cleft the root?<br>O [[Proteus]], let this habit make thee blush.<br>Be thou ashamed that I have took upon me<br>Such an immodest raiment, if shame live<br>In a disguise of [[Love|love]].<br>It is the lesser blot, modesty finds,<br>Women to change their shapes than men their minds. |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Than men their minds! ‘Tis true. O heaven, were man<br>But constant, he were perfect. That one error<br>Fills him with faults, makes him run through all th’<br>sins;<br>Inconstancy falls off ere it begins.<br>What is in [[Silvia]]’s face but I may spy<br>More fresh in [[Julia]]’s, with a constant eye? |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Come, come, a hand from either.<br>Let me be blessed to make this happy close.<br>’Twere pity two such friends should be long foes.<br>[[Julia]] and [[Proteus]] join hands |
| [[Proteus|PROTEUS]] | Bear witness, heaven, I have my wish for ever. |
| [[Julia|JULIA]] | And I mine. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the Outlaws with the Duke and [[Thurio]] as captives |
| OUTLAWS | A prize, a prize, a prize! |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Forbear, forbear, I say. It is my lord the Duke.<br>The Outlaws release the Duke and [[Thurio]]<br><br>(To the Duke) Your grace is welcome to a man<br>disgraced,<br>Banished [[Valentine]]. |
| DUKE | Sir [[Valentine]]! |
| [[Thurio|THURIO]] | Yonder is [[Silvia]], and [[Silvia]]’s mine. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | [[Thurio]], give back, or else embrace thy [[Death|death]].<br>Come not within the measure of my wrath.<br>Do not name [[Silvia]] thine. If once again,<br>[[Verona]] shall not hold thee. Here she stands.<br>Take but possession of her with a touch—<br>I dare thee but to breathe upon my [[Love|love]]. |
| [[Thurio|THURIO]] | Sir [[Valentine]], I care not for her, I.<br>I hold him but a [[Fool|fool]] that will endanger<br>His [[Body|body]] for a girl that loves him not.<br>I claim her not, and therefore she is thine. |
| DUKE | The more degenerate and base art thou<br>To make such means for her as thou hast done,<br>And leave her on such slight conditions.<br>Now by the [[Honour|honour]] of my ancestry<br>I do applaud thy spirit, [[Valentine]],<br>And think thee worthy of an empress’ [[Love|love]].<br>Know then I here forget all former griefs,<br>Cancel all grudge, repeal thee [[Home|home]] again,<br>Plead a new state in thy unrivalled merit,<br>To which I thus subscribe: Sir [[Valentine]],<br>Thou art a gentleman, and [[Well|well]] derived.<br>Take thou thy [[Silvia]], for thou hast deserved her. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | I thank your grace. The gift hath made me happy.<br>I now beseech you, for your daughter’s sake,<br>To grant one boon that I shall ask of you. |
| DUKE | I grant it, for thine own, whate’er it be. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | These banished men that I have kept withal<br>Are men endowed with worthy qualities.<br>Forgive them what they have committed here,<br>And let them be recalled from their [[Exile|exile]].<br>They are reformed, civil, full of good,<br>And fit for great employment, worthy lord. |
| DUKE | Thou hast prevailed. I pardon them and thee.<br>Dispose of them as thou know’st their deserts.<br>Come, let us go. We will include all jars<br>With triumphs, mirth, and rare solemnity. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | And as we walk along I dare be bold<br>With our discourse to make your grace to smile.<br>What think you of this page, my lord? |
| DUKE | I think the boy hath grace in him. He blushes. |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | I warrant you, my lord, more grace than boy. |
| DUKE | What mean you by that saying? |
| [[Valentine|VALENTINE]] | Please you, I’ll tell you as we pass along,<br>That you will [[Wonder|wonder]] what hath fortunèd.<br>Come, [[Proteus]], ’tis your penance but to hear<br>The story of your loves discovered.<br>That done, our day of [[Marriage|marriage]] shall be yours,<br>One feast, one [[house]], one mutual happiness. |

| THE TAMING OF THE | SHREW<br>The Taming of the Shrew was first published in the 1623 Folio, but a related play, shorter and simpler, with the title The Taming of a Shrew, had appeared in print in 1594. The exact relationship of these plays is disputed. A Shrew has sometimes been regarded as the source for The Shrew; some scholars have believed that both plays derive independently from an earlier play, now lost; it has even been suggested that Shakespeare wrote both plays. In our view Shakespeare’s play was written first, not necessarily on the foundation of an earlier play, and A Shrew is an anonymous [[Imitation|imitation]], written in the [[Hope|hope]] of capitalizing on the [[success]] of Shakespeare’s play. The difference between the titles is probably no more significant than the fact that The [[Winter]]’s Tale is even now often loosely referred to as A [[Winter]]’s Tale, or The [[Comedy]] of Errors as A [[Comedy]] of Errors.<br>The plot of The Taming of the Shrew has three main strands. First comes the Induction showing how a drunken tinker, [[Christopher Sly]], is made to believe himself a lord for whose entertainment a play is to be presented. This resembles an episode in The Arabian Nights, in which Caliph Haroun al Raschid plays a similar trick on Abu Hassan. A Latin version of this story was known in Shakespeare’s England; it may also have circulated by word of mouth. Second comes the principal plot of the play performed for Sly, in which the shrewish Katherine is wooed, won, and tamed by the fortune-hunting Petruccio. This is a popular narrative theme; Shakespeare may have known a ballad called ‘A merry jest of a shrewd and curst wife lapped in morel’s skin for her good behaviour’, printed around 1550. The third strand of the play involves [[Lucentio]], Gremio, and [[Hortensio]], all of them suitors for the hand of Katherine’s sister, [[Bianca]]. This is based on the first English prose [[Comedy|comedy]], George Gascoigne’s Supposes, translated from Ludovico Ariosto’s I Suppositi (1509), acted in 1566, and published in 1573. In The Taming of the Shrew as printed in the 1623 Folio [[Christopher Sly]] fades out after Act 1, Scene 1, in A Shrew he makes other appearances, and rounds off the play. These episodes may derive from a version of Shakespeare’s play different from that preserved in the Folio; we print them as Additional Passages.<br>The adapting of Shakespeare’s play that seems to have occurred early in its career foreshadows its later [[history]] on the stage. Seven versions appeared during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, culminating in David Garrick’s Catharine and [[Petruchio]], first performed in 1754. This version, omitting [[Christopher Sly]] and concentrating on the taming story, held the stage almost unchallenged until late in the nineteenth century. In various incarnations The Taming of the Shrew has always been popular on the stage, but its reputation as a robust [[Comedy|comedy]] verging on farce has often obscured its more subtle and imaginative aspects, brutalizing Petruccio and trivializing Kate. The Induction, finely written, establishes a fundamentally serious concern with the powers of [[Persuasion|persuasion]] to change not merely appearance but reality, and this theme is acted out at different levels in both strands of the subsequent action. |
