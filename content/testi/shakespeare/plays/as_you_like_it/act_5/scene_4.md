| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Duke Senior]], Amiens, [[Jaques]], [[Orlando]], [[Oliver]], and [[Celia]] as Aliena |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | Dost thou believe, [[Orlando]], that the boy<br>Can do all this that he hath promised? |
| [[Orlando|ORLANDO]] | I sometimes do believe, and sometimes do not,<br>As those that [[Fear|fear]] they [[Hope|hope]], and know they [[Fear|fear]]. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Rosalind]] as Ganymede, with [[Silvius]] and |
| [[Orlando|ORLANDO]] | [[Phoebe]] |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | Patience once more, whiles our compact is urged.<br>(To the Duke) You say if I bring in your [[Rosalind]]<br>You will bestow her on [[Orlando]] here? |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | That would I, had I kingdoms to give with her. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to [[Orlando]])<br>And you say you will have her when I bring her? |
| [[Orlando|ORLANDO]] | That would I, were I of all kingdoms [[King|king]]. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to [[Phoebe]])<br>You say you’ll marry me if I be willing? |
| [[Phoebe|PHOEBE]] | That will I, should I die the hour after. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | But if you do refuse to marry me<br>You’ll give yourself to this most faithful shepherd? |
| [[Phoebe|PHOEBE]] | So is the bargain. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to [[Silvius]])<br>You say that you’ll have [[Phoebe]] if she will. |
| [[Silvius|SILVIUS]] | Though to have her and [[Death|death]] were both one thing. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | I have promised to make all this matter even.<br>Keep you your word, O Duke, to give your daughter.<br>You yours, [[Orlando]], to receive his daughter.<br>Keep your word, [[Phoebe]], that you’ll marry me,<br>Or else refusing me to wed this shepherd.<br>Keep your word, [[Silvius]], that you’ll marry her<br>If she refuse me; and from hence I go<br>To make these doubts all even. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt [[Rosalind]] and [[Celia]] |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | I do remember in this shepherd boy<br>Some lively touches of my daughter’s favour. |
| [[Orlando|ORLANDO]] | My lord, the first [[Time|time]] that I ever saw him,<br>Methought he was a brother to your daughter.<br>But, my good lord, this boy is forest-born,<br>And hath been tutored in the rudiments<br>Of many desperate studies by his uncle,<br>Whom he reports to be a great magician<br>Obscured in the circle of this forest. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Touchstone]] the clown and Audrey |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | There is sure another flood toward, and these couples are coming to the ark. Here comes a pair of very strange beasts, which in all tongues are called fools. |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | Salutation and greeting to you all. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | (to the Duke) Good my lord, bid him welcome. This is the motley-minded gentleman that I have so often met in the forest. He hath been a courtier, he swears. |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | If any man [[Doubt|doubt]] that, let him put me to my purgation. I have trod a measure, I have flattered a lady, I have been politic with my friend, smooth with mine enemy, I have undone three tailors, I have had four quarrels, and like to have fought one. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | And how was that ta’en up? |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | [[Faith]], we met, and found the quarrel was upon the seventh cause. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | How, seventh cause?—Good my lord, like this fellow. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | I like him very [[Well|well]]. |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | God’ield you, sir, I [[Desire|desire]] you of the like. I press in here, sir, amongst the rest of the country copulatives, to swear, and to forswear, according as [[Marriage|marriage]] binds and [[Blood|blood]] breaks. A poor virgin, sir, an ill-favoured thing, sir, but mine own. A poor [[Humour|humour]] of mine, sir, to take that that no man else will. Rich honesty dwells like a miser, sir, in a poor [[house]], as your pearl in your foul oyster. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | By my [[Faith|faith]], he is very swift and sententious. |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | According to the [[Fool|fool]]’s bolt, sir, and such dulcet diseases. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | But for the seventh cause. How did you find the quarrel on the seventh cause? |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | Upon a lie seven times removed.—Bear your [[Body|body]] more seeming, Audrey.—As thus, sir: I did dislike the cut of a certain courtier’s beard. He sent me word if I said his beard was not cut [[Well|well]], he was in the mind it was. This is called the Retort Courteous. If I sent him word again it was not [[Well|well]] cut, he would send me word he cut it to please himself. This is called the Quip Modest. If again it was not [[Well|well]] cut, he disabled my judgement. This is called the Reply Churlish. If again it was not [[Well|well]] cut, he would answer I spake not true. This is called the Reproof Valiant. If again it was not [[Well|well]] cut, he would say I lie. This is called the Countercheck Quarrelsome. And so to the Lie Circumstantial, and the Lie Direct. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | And how oft did you say his beard was not [[Well|well]] cut? |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | I durst go no further than the Lie Circumstantial, nor he durst not give me the Lie Direct; and so we measured swords, and parted. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | Can you nominate in order now the degrees of the lie? |
| [[Touchstone|TOUCHSTONE]] | O sir, we quarrel in print, by the book, as you have books for good manners. I will name you the degrees. The first, the Retort Courteous; the second, the Quip Modest; the third, the Reply Churlish; the fourth, the Reproof Valiant; the fifth, the Countercheck Quarrelsome; the sixth, the Lie with Circumstance; the seventh, the Lie Direct. All these you may avoid but the Lie Direct; and you may avoid that, too, with an ‘if’. I knew when seven justices could not take up a quarrel, but when the parties were met themselves, one of them thought but of an ‘if’, as ‘If you said so, then I said so’, and they shook hands and swore brothers. Your ‘if’ is the only peacemaker; much virtue in ‘if’. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | (to the Duke) Is not this a rare fellow, my lord? He’s as good at anything, and yet a [[Fool|fool]]. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | He uses his folly like a stalking-[[Horse|horse]], and under the presentation of that he shoots his wit. |
| *(didascalia)* | Still music Enter Hymen with [[Rosalind]] and [[Celia]] as themselves |
| HYMEN | Then is there mirth in heaven<br>When earthly things made even<br>Atone together.<br>Good Duke, receive thy daughter;<br>Hymen from heaven brought her,<br>Yea, brought her hither,<br>That thou mightst join her hand with his<br>Whose heart within his bosom is. |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to the Duke)<br>To you I give myself, for I am yours.<br>(To [[Orlando]]) To you I give myself, for I am yours. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | If there be [[Truth|truth]] in sight, you are my daughter. |
| [[Orlando|ORLANDO]] | If there be [[Truth|truth]] in sight, you are my [[Rosalind]]. |
| [[Phoebe|PHOEBE]] | If sight and shape be true,<br>Why then, my [[Love|love]] adieu! |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to the Duke)<br>I’ll have no father if you be not he.<br>(To [[Orlando]]) I’ll have no husband if you be not he,<br>(To [[Phoebe]]) Nor ne’er wed woman if you be not she. |
| HYMEN | Peace, ho, I bar confusion.<br>‘Tis I must make conclusion<br>Of these most strange events.<br>Here’s eight that must take hands<br>To join in Hymen’s bands,<br>If [[Truth|truth]] holds true contents.<br>(To [[Orlando]] and [[Rosalind]])<br>You and you no [[Cross|cross]] shall part.<br>(To [[Oliver]] and [[Celia]])<br>You and you are heart in heart.<br>(To [[Phoebe]])<br>You to his [[Love|love]] must accord,<br>Or have a woman to your lord.<br>(To [[Touchstone]] and Audrey)<br>You and you are sure together<br>As the [[Winter|winter]] to foul weather.—<br>Whiles a wedlock [[Hymn|hymn]] we sing,<br>Feed yourselves with questioning,<br>That reason [[Wonder|wonder]] may diminish<br>How thus we met, and these things finish. |
| *(didascalia)* | [[Song]] |
| HYMEN | Wedding is great Juno’s [[Crown|crown]],<br>O blessèd bond of board and bed.<br>‘Tis Hymen peoples every town.<br>High wedlock then be honoured.<br>[[Honour]], high [[Honour|honour]] and renown<br>To Hymen, god of every town. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | (to [[Celia]])<br>O my dear niece, welcome thou art to me,<br>Even daughter; welcome in no less degree. |
| [[Phoebe|PHOEBE]] | (to [[Silvius]].)<br>I will not eat my word. Now thou art mine,<br>Thy [[Faith|faith]] my fancy to thee doth combine. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Jaques]] de Bois, the second brother |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] DE BOIS | Let me have audience for a word or two.<br>I am the second son of old Sir Rowland,<br>That bring these tidings to this fair assembly.<br>[[Duke Frederick]], hearing how that every day<br>Men of great worth resorted to this forest,<br>Addressed a mighty power, which were on foot,<br>In his own conduct, purposely to take<br>His brother here, and put him to the [[Sword|sword]].<br>And to the skirts of this wild wood he came<br>Where, meeting with an old religious man,<br>After some question with him was converted<br>Both from his enterprise and from the world,<br>His [[Crown|crown]] bequeathing to his banished brother,<br>And all their lands restored to them again<br>That were with him exiled. This to be true<br>I do engage my life. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | Welcome, young man.<br>Thou offer’st fairly to thy brothers’ wedding:<br>To one his lands withheld, and to the other<br>A land itself at large, a potent dukedom.<br>First, in this forest let us do those ends<br>That here were [[Well|well]] begun, and [[Well|well]] begot.<br>And after, every of this happy number<br>That have endured shrewd days and nights with us<br>Shall share the good of our returned fortune<br>According to the measure of their states.<br>Meantime, forget this new-fallen dignity<br>And fall into our rustic revelry.<br>Play, music, and you brides and bridegrooms all,<br>With measure heaped in joy to th’ measures fall. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | Sir, by your patience. (To [[Jaques]] de Bois) If I heard you<br>rightly<br>The Duke hath put on a religious life<br>And thrown into neglect the pompous [[Court|court]]. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] DE BOIS | He hath. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | To him will I. Out of these convertites<br>There is much matter to be heard and learned.<br>(To the Duke)<br>You to your former [[Honour|honour]] I bequeath;<br>Your patience and your virtue [[Well|well]] deserves it.<br>(To [[Orlando]])<br>You to a [[Love|love]] that your true [[Faith|faith]] doth merit;<br>(To [[Oliver]])<br><br>You to your land, and [[Love|love]], and great allies;<br>(To [[Silvius]])<br><br>You to a long and [[Well|well]]-deserved bed;<br>(To [[Touchstone]])<br><br>And you to wrangling, for thy loving voyage<br>Is but for two months victualled.—So, to your<br>pleasures;<br>I am for other than for dancing measures. |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | Stay, [[Jaques]], stay. |
| [[Jaques|JAQUES]] | To see no pastime, I. What you would have<br>I’ll stay to know at your abandoned [[Cave|cave]]. Exit |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | Proceed, proceed. We’ll so begin these rites<br>As we do trust they’ll end, in true delights. |
| *(didascalia)* | They [[Dance|dance]]; then exeunt all but [[Rosalind]] |
| [[Duke Senior|DUKE SENIOR]] | Epilogue |
| [[Rosalind|ROSALIND]] | (to the audience) It is not the fashion to see the lady the epilogue; but it is no more unhandsome than to see the lord the prologue. If it be true that good wine needs no bush, ’tis true that a good play needs no epilogue. Yet to good wine they do use good bushes, and good plays prove the better by the help of good epilogues. What a case am I in then, that am neither a good epilogue nor cannot insinuate with you in the behalf of a good play! I am not furnished like a beggar, therefore to beg will not become me. My way is to conjure you; and I’ll begin with the women. I charge you, O women, for the [[Love|love]] you bear to men, to like as much of this play as please you. And I charge you, O men, for the [[Love|love]] you bear to women—as I perceive by your simpering none of you hates them—that between you and the women the play may please. If I were a woman I would kiss as many of you as had beards that pleased me, complexions that liked me, and breaths that I defied not. And I am sure, as many as have good beards, or good faces, or sweet breaths will for my kind offer, when I make curtsy, bid me farewell. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit |
| SEVERAL | references from 1589 onwards witness the existence of a play about [[Hamlet]], but Francis Meres did not attribute a play with this title to Shakespeare in 1598. The first clear reference to Shakespeare’s play is its entry in the Stationers’ Register on 26 July 1602 as The [[Revenge]] of [[Hamlet]] Prince [of] [[Denmark]], when it was said to have been ‘lately acted by the Lord Chamberlain his servants’. It survives in three versions; their relationship is a matter of dispute on which views about when Shakespeare wrote his play, and in what form, depend. In 1603 appeared an inferior text apparently assembled from actors’ memories; it has only about 2,200 lines. In the following year, as if to put the record straight, James Roberts (to whom the play had been entered in 1602) published it as ‘newly imprinted and enlarged to almost as much again as it was, according to the true and perfect copy’. At about 3,800 lines, this is the longest version. The 1623 Folio offers a still different text, some 230 lines shorter than the 1604 version, differing verbally from that at many points, and including about 70 additional lines. It is our belief that Shakespeare wrote [[Hamlet]] about 1600, and revised it later; that the 1604 edition was printed from his original papers; that the Folio represents the revised version; and that the 1603 edition represents a very imperfect report of an abridged version of the revision. So our text is based on the Folio; passages present in the 1604 quarto but absent from the Folio are printed as Additional Passages because we believe that, however fine they may be in themselves, Shakespeare decided that the play as a whole would be better without them.<br>The plot of [[Hamlet]] originates in a Scandinavian folk-tale told in the twelfth-century Danish [[history|History]] written in Latin by the Danish Saxo Grammaticus. François de Belleforest retold it in the fifth volume (1570) of his Histoires Tragiques, not translated into English until 1608. Saxo, through Belleforest, provided the basic story of a Prince of [[Denmark]] committed to [[Revenge|revenge]] his father’s murder by his own brother ([[Claudius]]) who has married the dead man’s widow ([[Gertrude]]). As in Shakespeare, [[Hamlet]] pretends to be mad, kills his uncle’s counsellor ([[Polonius]]) while he is eavesdropping, rebukes his mother, is sent to England under the escort of two retainers ([[Rosencrantz]] and [[Guildenstern]]) who bear orders that he be put to [[Death|death]] on arrival, finds the letter containing the orders and alters it so that it is the retainers who are executed, returns to [[Denmark]], and kills the [[King]].<br>Belleforest’s story differs at some points from Shakespeare’s, and Shakespeare elaborates it, adding, for example, the [[Ghost]] of [[Hamlet]]’s father, the coming of the actors to Elsinore, the performance of the play through which [[Hamlet]] tests his uncle’s [[Guilt|guilt]], [[Ophelia]]’s [[Madness|madness]] and [[Death|death]], [[Laertes]]’ plot to [[Revenge|revenge]] his father’s [[Death|death]], the [[Grave|grave]]-digger, [[Ophelia]]’s funeral, and the characters of Osric and Fortinbras. How much he owed to the lost [[Hamlet]] play we cannot tell; what is certain is that Shakespeare used his mastery of a wide range of diverse styles in both verse and prose, and his genius for dramatic effect, to create from these and other sources the most complex, varied, and exciting drama that had ever been seen on the English stage. Its popularity was instant and enduring. The play has had a profound influence on Western culture, and Shakespeare’s [[Hamlet]] has himself entered the world of myth. |
