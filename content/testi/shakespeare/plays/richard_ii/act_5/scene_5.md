| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Richard]], [[Alone|alone]] |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | I have been studying how I may compare<br>This [[Prison|prison]] where I live unto the world;<br>And for because the world is populous,<br>And here is not a creature but myself,<br>I cannot do it. Yet I’ll hammer it out.<br>My brain I’ll prove the female to my [[Soul|soul]],<br>My [[Soul|soul]] the father, and these two beget<br>A generation of still-breeding thoughts;<br>And these same thoughts people this little world<br>In humours like the people of this world.<br>For no thought is contented. The better sort,<br>As thoughts of things divine, are intermixed<br>With scruples, and do set the [[Faith|faith]] itself<br>Against the [[Faith|faith]], as thus: ‘Come, little ones’,<br>And then again,<br>‘It is as hard to come as for a camel<br>To thread the postern of a small needle’s eye.’<br>Thoughts tending to [[Ambition|ambition]], they do plot<br>Unlikely wonders: how these vain weak nails<br>May tear a passage through the flinty ribs<br>Of this hard world, my ragged [[Prison|prison]] walls;<br>And for they cannot, die in their own [[Pride|pride]].<br>Thoughts tending to content flatter themselves<br>That they are not the first of fortune’s slaves,<br>Nor shall not be the last—like seely beggars,<br>Who, sitting in the stocks, refuge their shame<br>That many have, and others must, set there;<br>And in this thought they find a kind of ease,<br>Bearing their own misfortunes on the back<br>Of such as have before endured the like.<br>Thus play I in one person many people,<br>And none contented. Sometimes am I [[King|king]];<br>Then treason makes me wish myself a beggar,<br>And so I am. Then crushing penury<br>Persuades me I was better when a [[King|king]].<br>Then am I kinged again, and by and by<br>Think that I am unkinged by Bolingbroke,<br>And straight am nothing. But whate’er I be,<br>Nor I, nor any man that but man is,<br>With nothing shall be pleased till he be eased<br>With being nothing.<br>The music plays |
| *(didascalia)* | Music do I hear. |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | Ha, ha; keep [[Time|time]]! How sour sweet music is<br>When [[Time|time]] is broke and no proportion kept.<br>So is it in the music of men’s lives.<br>And here have I the daintiness of ear<br>To check [[Time|time]] broke in a disordered string;<br>But for the concord of my state and [[Time|time]]<br>Had not an ear to hear my true [[Time|time]] broke.<br>I wasted [[Time|time]], and now doth [[Time|time]] waste me,<br>For now hath [[Time|time]] made me his numb‘ring [[Clock|clock]].<br>My thoughts are minutes, and with sighs they jar<br>Their watches on unto mine eyes, the outward watch<br>Whereto my finger, like a dial’s point,<br>Is pointing still in cleansing them from [[Tears|tears]].<br>Now, sir, the sounds that tell what hour it is<br>Are clamorous groans that strike upon my heart,<br>Which is the bell. So sighs, and [[Tears|tears]], and groans<br>Show minutes, hours, and times. But my [[Time|time]]<br>Runs posting on in Bolingbroke’s proud joy,<br>While I stand fooling here, his jack of the [[Clock|clock]].<br>This music mads me. Let it sound no more,<br>For though it have holp madmen to their wits,<br>In me it seems it will make wise men mad. |
| *(didascalia)* | The music ceases |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | Yet blessing on his heart that gives it me,<br>For ’tis a sign of [[Love|love]], and [[Love|love]] to [[Richard]]<br>Is a strange brooch in this all-hating world. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | a Groom<br>of<br>the<br>stable |
| GROOM | Hail, royal Prince! |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | Thanks, noble peer.<br>The cheapest of us is ten groats too dear.<br>What art thou, and how com’st thou hither,<br>Where no man never comes but that sad dog<br>That brings me food to make misfortune live? |
| GROOM | I was a poor groom of thy stable, [[King]],<br>When thou wert [[King|king]]; who, travelling towards York,<br>With much ado at length have gotten leave<br>To look upon my sometimes royal master’s face.<br>O, how it erned my heart when I beheld<br>In [[London]] streets, that coronation day,<br>When Bolingbroke rode on roan Barbary,<br>That [[Horse|horse]] that thou so often hast bestrid,<br>That [[Horse|horse]] that I so carefully have dressed! |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | Rode he on Barbary? Tell me, gentle friend,<br>How went he under him? |
| GROOM | So proudly as if he disdained the ground. |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | So proud that Bolingbroke was on his back.<br>That jade hath eat bread from my royal hand;<br>This hand hath made him proud with clapping him.<br>Would he not stumble, would he not fall down-<br>Since [[Pride|pride]] must have a fall—and break the neck<br>Of that proud man that did usurp his back?<br>[[Forgiveness]], [[Horse|horse]]! Why do I rail on thee,<br>Since thou, created to be awed by man,<br>Wast born to bear? I was not made a [[Horse|horse]],<br>And yet I bear a burden like an ass,<br>Spur-galled and tired by jauncing Bolingbroke. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Keeper to [[Richard]], with meat |
| KEEPER | (to Groom)<br>Fellow, give place. Here is no longer stay. |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | (to Groom)<br>If thou [[Love|love]] me, ’tis [[Time|time]] thou wert away. |
| GROOM | What my tongue dares not, that my heart shall say. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit |
| KEEPER | My lord, will’t please you to fall to? |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | Taste of it first, as thou art wont to do. |
| KEEPER | My lord, I dare not. Sir Piers of Exton,<br>Who lately came from the [[King]], commands the contrary. |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | (striking<br>the Keeper)<br>The devil take Henry of Lancaster and thee I<br>Patience is stale, and I am weary of it. |
| KEEPER | Help, help, help!<br>Exton and his men rush in |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | How now! What means [[Death|death]] in this rude assault?<br>He seizes a weapon from a man, and kills him<br>[[Villain]], thy own hand yields thy [[Death|death]]’s instrument.<br>He kills another<br>Go thou, and fill another room in hell.<br>Here Exton strikes him down |
| [[Richard|RICHARD]] | That hand shall burn in never-quenching [[Fire|fire]]<br>That staggers thus my person. Exton, thy fierce hand<br>Hath with the [[King]]’s [[Blood|blood]] stained the [[King]]’s own land.<br>Mount, mount, my [[Soul|soul]]; thy seat is up on high,<br>Whilst my gross flesh sinks downward, here to die.<br>He dies |
| EXTON | As full of valour as of royal [[Blood|blood]].<br>Both have I spilt. O, would the deed were good I<br>For now the devil that told me I did [[Well|well]]<br>Says that this deed is chronicled in hell.<br>This dead [[King]] to the living [[King]] I’ll bear.<br>Take hence the rest, and give them burial here. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Exton with [[Richard]]’s [[Body|body]] at one door, and his men with the other bodies at another door |
| *(didascalia)* | 5.6 Flourish. Enter [[King]] Henry and the [[Duke of York]], with other lords and attendants |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Kind uncle York, the latest news we hear<br>Is that the rebels have consumed with [[Fire|fire]]<br>Our town of Ci’cester in Gloucestershire;<br>But whether they be ta’en or slain we hear not. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the [[Earl of Northumberland]] |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Welcome, my lord. What is the news? |
| NORTHUMBERLAND | First, to thy sacred state wish I all happiness.<br>The next news is, I have to [[London]] sent<br>The heads of Salisbury, Spencer, Blunt, and [[Kent]].<br>The manner of their taking may appear<br>At large discoursed in this paper here.<br>He gives the paper to [[King]] Henry |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | We thank thee, gentle Percy, for thy pains,<br>And to thy worth will add right worthy gains. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Lord Fitzwalter |
| FITZWALTER | My lord, I have from [[Oxford]] sent to [[London]]<br>The heads of Brocas and Sir Bennet Seely,<br>Two of the dangerous consorted traitors<br>That sought at [[Oxford]] thy dire overthrow. |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Thy pains, Fitzwalter, shall not be forgot.<br>Right noble is thy merit, [[Well|well]] I wot. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Harry Percy, with the Bishop of Carlisle, guarded |
| HARRY PERCY | The grand conspirator Abbot of Westminster,<br>With clog of [[Conscience|conscience]] and sour [[Melancholy|melancholy]],<br>Hath yielded up his [[Body|body]] to the [[Grave|grave]].<br>But here is Carlisle living, to abide<br>Thy kingly doom and sentence of his [[Pride|pride]]. |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Carlisle, this is your doom.<br>Choose out some secret place, some reverent room<br>More than thou hast, and with it joy thy life.<br>So as thou liv’st in peace, die free from strife.<br>For though mine enemy thou hast ever been,<br>High sparks of [[Honour|honour]] in thee have I seen. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Exton with his men bearing the coffin |
| EXTON | Great [[King]], within this coffin I present<br>Thy buried [[Fear|fear]]. Herein all breathless lies<br>The mightiest of thy greatest enemies,<br>[[Richard]] of Bordeaux, by me hither brought. |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Exton, I thank thee not, for thou hast wrought<br>A deed of slander with thy fatal hand<br>Upon my head and all this famous land. |
| EXTON | From your own mouth, my lord, did I this deed. |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | They [[Love|love]] not [[Poison|poison]] that do [[Poison|poison]] need;<br>Nor do I thee. Though I did wish him dead,<br>I hate the murderer, [[Love|love]] him murdered.<br>The [[Guilt|guilt]] of [[Conscience|conscience]] take thou for thy labour,<br>But neither my good word nor princely favour.<br>With [[Cain]] go wander through the shades of [[Night|night]],<br>And never show thy head by day nor light. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Exton and his men |
| [[King|KING]] HENRY | Lords, I protest my [[Soul|soul]] is full of woe<br>That [[Blood|blood]] should sprinkle me to make me grow.<br>Come mourn with me for what I do lament,<br>And put on sullen black incontinent.<br>I’ll make a voyage to the Holy Land<br>To wash this [[Blood|blood]] off from my guilty hand.<br>March sadly after. Grace my mournings here<br>In weeping after this untimely bier. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt With the coffin |

| ON | its first appearance in print, in 1597, [[Romeo]] and [[Juliet]] was described as ‘An excellent conceited [[Tragedy|tragedy]]’ that had ‘been often (with great applause) played publicly’; its popularity is witnessed by the fact that this is a pirated version, put together from actors’ memories as a way of cashing in on its [[success]]. A second printing, two years later, offered a greatly superior text apparently printed from Shakespeare’s working papers. Probably he wrote it in 1594 or 1595.<br>The story was already [[Well|well]] known, in Italian, French, and English. Shakespeare owes most to [[arthur|Arthur]] Brooke’s long poem The Tragical [[history|History]] of Romeus and [[Juliet]] (1562), which had already supplied hints for The Two Gentlemen of [[Verona]]; he may also have looked at some of the other versions. In his address ‘To the Reader’, Brooke says that he has seen ‘the same argument lately set forth on stage with more commendation than I can look for’, but no earlier play survives.<br>Shakespeare’s Prologue neatly sketches the plot of the two star-crossed [[lovers]] born of feuding families whose deaths ‘bury their parents’ strife’; and the formal verse structure of the Prologue—a [[Sonnet|sonnet]]—is matched by the carefully patterned layout of the action. At the climax of the first scene, [[Prince Escalus]] stills a brawl between representatives of the houses of Montague ([[Romeo]]’s family) and [[Capulet]] ([[Juliet]]’s); at the end of Act 3, Scene 1, he passes judgement on another, more serious brawl, banishing [[Romeo]] for killing [[Juliet]]’s cousin [[Tybalt]] after [[Tybalt]] had killed [[Romeo]]’s friend and the Prince’s kinsman, [[Mercutio]]; and at the end of Act 5, the Prince presides over the [[Reconciliation|reconciliation]] of Montagues and Capulets. Within this framework of public life [[Romeo]] and [[Juliet]] act out their brief [[Tragedy|tragedy]]: in the first act, they meet and declare their [[Love|love]]—in another [[Sonnet|sonnet]]; in the second, they arrange to marry in secret; in the third, after [[Romeo]]’s banishment, they consummate their [[Marriage|marriage]] and part; in the fourth, [[Juliet]] drinks a sleeping draught prepared by [[Friar Laurence]] so that she may escape [[Marriage|marriage]] to [[Paris]] and, after waking in the family tomb, run off with [[Romeo]]; in the fifth, after [[Romeo]], believing her to be dead, has taken [[Poison|poison]], she stabs herself to [[Death|death]].<br>The play’s structural formality is offset by an astonishing fertility of linguistic invention, showing itself no less in the comic bawdiness of the servants, the [[Nurse]], and (on a more sophisticated level) [[Mercutio]] than in the rapt and impassioned poetry of the [[lovers]]. Shakespeare’s mastery over a wide range of verbal styles combines with his psychological perceptiveness to create a richer gallery of memorable characters than in any play written up to this [[Time|time]]; and his theatrical [[Imagination|imagination]] compresses Brooke’s leisurely narrative into a dramatic masterpiece. |
