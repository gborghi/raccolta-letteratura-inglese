| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[King]] Harry, the Dukes of [[Gloucester]], [[Clarence]], and Exeter, and the Earls of Warwick and Westmorland |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Where is my gracious lord of Canterbury? |
| EXETER | Not here in presence. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Send for him, good uncle. |
| WESTMORLAND | Shall we call in th’ambassador, my liege? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Not yet, my cousin. We would be resolved,<br>Before we hear him, of some things of weight<br>That task our thoughts, concerning us and France. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Bishop of Ely |
| CANTERBURY | God and his angels guard your sacred throne,<br>And make you long become it. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Sure we thank you.<br>My learnèd lord, we pray you to proceed,<br>And justly and religiously unfold<br>Why the law Salic that they have in France<br>Or should or should not bar us in our claim.<br>And God forbid, my dear and faithful lord,<br>That you should fashion, wrest, or bow your reading,<br>Or nicely charge your understanding [[Soul|soul]]<br>With opening titles miscreate, whose right<br>Suits not in native colours with the [[Truth|truth]];<br>For God doth know how many now in health<br>Shall drop their [[Blood|blood]] in approbation<br>Of what your reverence shall incite us to.<br>Therefore take heed how you impawn our person,<br>How you awake our sleeping [[Sword|sword]] of war;<br>We charge you in the name of God take heed.<br>For never two such kingdoms did contend<br>Without much fall of [[Blood|blood]], whose guiltless drops<br>Are every one a woe, a sore complaint<br>’Gainst him whose wrongs gives edge unto the swords<br>That makes such waste in brief [[Mortality|mortality]].<br>Under this conjuration speak, my lord,<br>For we will hear, note, and believe in heart<br>That what you speak is in your [[Conscience|conscience]] washed<br>As pure as sin with baptism. |
| CANTERBURY | Then hear me, gracious sovereign, and you peers<br>That owe your selves, your lives, and services<br>To this imperial throne. There is no bar<br>To make against your highness’ claim to France<br>But this, which they produce from Pharamond:<br>‘In terram Salicam mulieres ne succedant’—<br><br>’No woman shall succeed in Salic land’—<br>Which ‘Salic land’ the French unjustly gloss<br>To be the realm of France, and Pharamond<br>The founder of this law and female bar.<br>Yet their own authors faithfully affirm<br>That the land Salic is in [[Germany]],<br>Between the floods of Saale and of Elbe,<br>Where, Charles the Great having subdued the Saxons,<br>There left behind and settled certain French<br>Who, holding in disdain the German women<br>For some dishonest manners of their life,<br>Established there this law: to wit, no female<br>Should be inheritrix in Salic land—<br>Which Salic, as I said, ’twixt Elbe and Saale,<br>Is at this day in [[Germany]] called Meissen.<br>Then doth it [[Well|well]] appear the Salic Law<br>Was not devised for the realm of France.<br>Nor did the French possess the Salic land<br>Until four hundred one-and-twenty years<br>After defunction of [[King]] Pharamond,<br>Idly supposed the founder of this law,<br>Who died within the year of our redemption<br>Four hundred twenty-six; and Charles the Great<br>Subdued the Saxons, and did seat the French<br>Beyond the [[River|river]] Saale, in the year<br>Eight hundred five. Besides, their writers say,<br>[[King]] Pépin, which deposed Childéric,<br>Did, as heir general—being descended<br>Of Blithild, which was daughter to [[King]] Clotaire—<br>Make claim and title to the [[Crown|crown]] of France.<br>Hugh Capet also—who usurped the [[Crown|crown]]<br>Of Charles the Duke of Lorraine, sole heir male<br>Of the true line and stock of Charles the Great—<br>To fine his title with some shows of [[Truth|truth]],<br>Though in pure [[Truth|truth]] it was corrupt and naught,<br>Conveyed himself as heir to th’ Lady Lingard,<br>Daughter to Charlemain, who was the son<br>To Louis the Emperor, and Louis the son<br>Of Charles the Great. Also, [[King]] Louis the Ninth,<br>Who was sole heir to the [[Usurper|usurper]] Capet,<br>Could not keep quiet in his [[Conscience|conscience]],<br>Wearing the [[Crown|crown]] of France, till satisfied<br>That fair [[Queen Isabel]], his grandmother,<br>Was lineal of the Lady Ermengarde,<br>Daughter to Charles, the foresaid Duke of Lorraine;<br>By the which [[Marriage|marriage]], the line of Charles the Great<br>Was reunited to the [[Crown|crown]] of France.<br>So that, as clear as is the [[Summer|summer]]’s sun,<br>[[King]] Pépin’s title and Hugh Capet’s claim,<br>[[King]] Louis his satisfaction, all appear<br>To hold in right and title of the female;<br>So do the kings of France unto this day,<br>Howbeit they would hold up this Salic Law<br>To bar your highness claiming from the female,<br>And rather choose to hide them in a net<br>Than amply to embar their crooked titles,<br>Usurped from you and your progenitors. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | May I with right and [[Conscience|conscience]] make this claim? |
| CANTERBURY | The sin upon my head, dread sovereign.<br>For in the Book of Numbers is it writ,<br>‘When the son dies, let the inheritance<br>Descend unto the daughter.’ Gracious lord,<br>Stand for your own; unwind your bloody [[Flag|flag]];<br>Look back into your mighty ancestors.<br>Go, my dread lord, to your great-grandsire’s tomb,<br>From whom you claim; invoke his warlike spirit,<br>And your great-uncle’s, [[Edward the Black Prince]],<br>Who on the French ground played a [[Tragedy|tragedy]],<br>Making defeat on the full power of France,<br>Whiles his most mighty father on a [[Hill|hill]]<br>Stood smiling to behold his [[Lion|lion]]’s whelp<br>Forage in [[Blood|blood]] of French nobility. no<br>O noble English, that could entertain<br>With half their forces the full [[Pride|pride]] of France,<br>And let another half stand laughing by,<br>All out of work, and cold for action. |
| ELY | Awake remembrance of those valiant dead,<br>And with your puissant arm renew their feats.<br>You are their heir, you sit upon their throne,<br>The [[Blood|blood]] and [[Courage|courage]] that renowned them<br>Runs in your veins—and my thrice-puissant liege<br>Is in the very May-morn of his youth,<br>Ripe for exploits and mighty enterprises. |
| EXETER | Your brother kings and monarchs of the earth<br>Do all expect that you should rouse yourself<br>As did the former lions of your [[Blood|blood]]. |
| WESTMORLAND | They know your grace hath cause; and means and<br>might,<br>So hath your highness. Never [[King|king]] of England<br>Had nobles richer and more loyal subjects,<br>Whose hearts have left their bodies here in England<br>And lie pavilioned in the fields of France. |
| CANTERBURY | O let their bodies follow, my dear liege,<br>With [[Blood|blood]] and [[Sword|sword]] and [[Fire|fire]], to win your right.<br>In aid whereof, we of the spiritualty<br>Will raise your highness such a mighty sum<br>As never did the clergy at one [[Time|time]]<br>Bring in to any of your ancestors. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | We must not only arm t’invade the French,<br>But lay down our proportions to defend<br>Against the Scot, who will make raid upon us<br>With all advantages. |
| CANTERBURY | They of those marches, gracious sovereign,<br>Shall be a wall sufficient to defend<br>Our inland from the pilfering borderers. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | We do not mean the coursing snatchers only,<br>But [[Fear|fear]] the main intendment of the Scot,<br>Who hath been still a giddy neighbour to us.<br>For you shall read that my great-grandfather<br>Never unmasked his power unto France<br>But that the Scot on his unfurnished kingdom<br>Came pouring like the tide into a breach<br>With ample and brim fullness of his force<br>Galling the gleaned land with hot assays,<br>Girding with grievous siege castles and towns,<br>That England, being empty of defence,<br>Hath shook and trembled at the bruit thereof. |
| CANTERBURY | She hath been then more feared than harmed, my<br>liege. 155<br>For hear her but exampled by herself:<br>When all her chivalry hath been in France<br>And she a mourning widow of her nobles,<br>She hath herself not only [[Well|well]] defended<br>But taken and impounded as a stray<br>The [[King]] of Scots, whom she did send to France<br>To fill [[King]] Edward’s fame with prisoner kings<br>And make your chronicle as rich with praise<br>As is the ooze and [[Bottom|bottom]] of the sea<br>With sunken wrack and sumless treasuries. |
| *(didascalia)* | A LORD |
| CANTERBURY | But there’s a saying very old and true:<br>‘If that you will France win, Then with Scotland first begin.’<br>For once the eagle England being in prey,<br>To her unguarded nest the weasel Scot<br>Comes sneaking, and so sucks her princely eggs,<br>Playing the mouse in absence of the cat,<br>To ’tame and havoc more than she can eat. |
| EXETER | It follows then the cat must stay at [[Home|home]].<br>Yet that is but a crushed necessity,<br>Since we have locks to safeguard necessaries<br>And pretty traps to catch the petty thieves.<br>While that the armed hand doth fight abroad,<br>Th’advisèd head defends itself at [[Home|home]].<br>For [[government]], though high and low and lower,<br>Put into parts, doth keep in one consent,<br>Congreeing in a full and natural close,<br>Like music. |
| CANTERBURY | True. Therefore doth heaven divide<br>The state of man in divers functions,<br>Setting endeavour in continual motion;<br>To which is fixed, as an aim or butt,<br>Obedience. For so work the honey-[[Bees|bees]],<br>Creatures that by a rule in [[Nature|nature]] teach<br>The act of order to a peopled kingdom.<br>They have a [[King|king]], and officers of sorts,<br>Where some like magistrates correct at [[Home|home]];<br>Others like merchants venture trade abroad;<br>Others like soldiers, armed in their stings,<br>Make boot upon the [[Summer|summer]]’s velvet buds,<br>Which pillage they with merry march bring [[Home|home]]<br>To the tent royal of their emperor,<br>Who busied in his majesty surveys<br>The singing masons building roofs of gold,<br>The civil citizens lading up the honey,<br>The poor mechanic porters crowding in<br>Their heavy burdens at his narrow gate,<br>The sad-eyed justice with his surly hum<br>Delivering o’er to executors pale<br>The lazy yawning drone. I this infer:<br>That many things, having full reference<br>To one consent, may work contrariously.<br>As many arrows, loosed several ways,<br>Fly to one mark, as many ways meet in one town,<br>As many fresh streams meet in one salt sea,<br>As many lines close in the dial’s centre,<br>So may a thousand actions once afoot<br>End in one purpose, and be all [[Well|well]] borne<br>Without defect. Therefore to France, my liege.<br>Divide your happy England into four,<br>Whereof take you one quarter into France,<br>And you withal shall make all Gallia shake.<br>If we with thrice such powers left at [[Home|home]]<br>Cannot defend our own doors from the dog,<br>Let us be worried, and our nation lose<br>The name of hardiness and policy. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Call in the messengers sent from the [[Dauphin]]. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit one or more |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Now are we [[Well|well]] resolved, and by God’s help<br>And yours, the noble sinews of our power,<br>France being ours we’ll bend it to our awe,<br>Or break it all to pieces. Or there we’ll sit,<br>Ruling in large and ample empery<br>O’er France and all her almost kingly dukedoms,<br>Or lay these bones in an unworthy urn,<br>Tombless, with no remembrance over them.<br>Either our [[history]] shall with full mouth<br>Speak freely of our acts, or else our [[Grave|grave]],<br>Like Turkish mute, shall have a tongueless mouth,<br>Not worshipped with a waxen epitaph. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Ambassadors of France, with a tun |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Now are we [[Well|well]] prepared to know the pleasure<br>Of our fair cousin [[Dauphin]], for we hear<br>Your greeting is from him, not from the [[King]]. |
| AMBASSADOR | May’t please your majesty to give us leave<br>Freely to render what we have in charge,<br>Or shall we sparingly show you far off<br>The [[Dauphin]]’s meaning and our embassy? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | We are no [[Tyrant|tyrant]], but a Christian [[King|king]],<br>Unto whose grace our passion is as subject<br>As is our wretches fettered in our prisons.<br>Therefore with frank and with uncurbèd plainness<br>Tell us the [[Dauphin]]’s mind. |
| AMBASSADOR | Thus then in few:<br>Your highness lately sending into France<br>Did claim some certain dukedoms, in the right<br>Of your great predecessor, [[King]] Edward the Third.<br>In answer of which claim, the Prince our master<br>Says that you savour too much of your youth,<br>And bids you be advised, there’s naught in France<br>That can be with a nimble galliard won:<br>You cannot revel into dukedoms there.<br>He therefore sends you, meeter for your spirit,<br>This tun of treasure, and in lieu of this<br>Desires you let the dukedoms that you claim<br>Hear no more of you. This the [[Dauphin]] speaks. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | What treasure, uncle? |
| EXETER | (opening the tun) Tennis balls, my liege. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | We are glad the [[Dauphin]] is so pleasant with us.<br>His present and your pains we thank you for.<br>When we have matched our rackets to these balls,<br>We will in France, by God’s grace, play a set<br>Shall strike his father’s [[Crown|crown]] into the hazard.<br>Tell him he hath made a match with such a wrangler<br>That all the courts of France will be disturbed<br>With chases. And we understand him [[Well|well]],<br>How he comes o‘er us with our wilder days,<br>Not measuring what use we made of them.<br>We never valued this poor seat of England,<br>And therefore, living hence, did give ourself<br>To barbarous licence—as ’tis ever common<br>That men are merriest when they are from [[Home|home]].<br>But tell the [[Dauphin]] I will keep my state,<br>Be like a [[King|king]], and show my sail of [[Greatness|greatness]]<br>When I do rouse me in my throne of France.<br>For that have I laid by my majesty<br>And plodded like a man for working days,<br>But I will rise there with so full a glory<br>That I will dazzle all the eyes of France,<br>Yea strike the [[Dauphin]] blind to look on us.<br>And tell the pleasant Prince this mock of his<br>Hath turned his balls to gunstones, and his [[Soul|soul]]<br>Shall stand sore charged for the wasteful vengeance<br>That shall fly from them—for many a thousand<br>widows<br>Shall this his mock mock out of their dear husbands,<br>Mock mothers from their sons, mock castles down;<br>Ay, some are yet ungotten and [[unborn]]<br>That shall have cause to curse the [[Dauphin]]’s scorn.<br>But this lies all within the will of God,<br>To whom I do appeal, and in whose name<br>Tell you the [[Dauphin]] I am coming on<br>To venge me as I may, and to put forth<br>My rightful hand in a [[Well|well]]-hallowed cause.<br>So get you hence in peace. And tell the [[Dauphin]]<br>His jest will savour but of shallow wit<br>When thousands weep more than did laugh at it.—<br>Convey them with safe conduct.—Fare you [[Well|well]]. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Ambassadors |
| EXETER | This was a merry message. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | We [[Hope|hope]] to make the sender blush at it.<br>Therefore, my lords, omit no happy hour<br>That may give furth’rance to our expedition;<br>For we have now no thought in us but France,<br>Save those to God, that run before our business.<br>Therefore let our proportions for these wars<br>Be soon collected, and all things thought upon<br>That may with reasonable swiftness add<br>More feathers to our wings; for, God before,<br>We’ll chide this [[Dauphin]] at his father’s door.<br>Therefore let every man now task his thought,<br>That this fair action may on foot be brought. |
| *(didascalia)* | Flourish. Exeunt |
