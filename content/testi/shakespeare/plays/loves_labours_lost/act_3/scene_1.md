| *(didascalia)* | Enter Armado the braggart, and Mote his boy |
| ARMADO | Warble, [[Child|child]]; make passionate my sense of hearing. |
| MOTE | (sings) Concolinel. |
| ARMADO | Sweet air! Go, tenderness of years, take this key. Give enlargement to the swain. Bring him festinately hither. I must employ him in a letter to my [[Love|love]]. |
| MOTE | Master, will you win your [[Love|love]] with a French brawl? |
| ARMADO | How meanest thou—brawling in French? |
| MOTE | No, my complete master; but to jig off a tune at the tongue’s end, canary to it with your feet, [[Humour|humour]] it with turning up your eyelids, sigh a note and sing a note, sometime through the throat as if you swallowed [[Love|love]] with singing [[Love|love]], sometime through the nose as if you snuffed up [[Love|love]] by smelling [[Love|love]], with your hat penthouse-like o’er the shop of your eyes, with your arms crossed on your thin-belly doublet like a rabbit on a spit, or your hands in your pocket like a man after the old painting, and keep not too long in one tune, but a snip and away. These are complements, these are humours; these betray nice wenches that would be betrayed without these, and make them men of note—do you note? men—that most are affected to these. |
| ARMADO | How hast thou purchased this experience? |
| MOTE | By my penny of observation. |
| ARMADO | But O, but O- |
| MOTE | ‘The hobby-[[Horse|horse]] is forgot.’ |
| ARMADO | Call’st thou my [[Love|love]] hobby-[[Horse|horse]] ? |
| MOTE | No, master, the hobby-[[Horse|horse]] is but a colt, and your [[Love|love]] perhaps a hackney. But have you forgot your [[Love|love]]? |
| ARMADO | Almost I had. |
| MOTE | Negligent student, learn her by heart. |
| ARMADO | By heart and in heart, boy. |
| MOTE | And out of heart, master. All those three I will prove. 36 |
| ARMADO | What wilt thou prove? |
| MOTE | A man, if I live; and this, ‘by’, ‘in’, and ‘without’, upon the instant: ‘by’ heart you [[Love|love]] her because your heart cannot come by her; ‘in’ heart you [[Love|love]] her because your heart is in [[Love|love]] with her; and ‘out’ of heart you [[Love|love]] her, being out of heart that you cannot enjoy her. |
| ARMADO | I am all these three. |
| MOTE | (aside) And three times as much more, and yet nothing at all. |
| ARMADO | Fetch hither the swain. He must carry me a letter. |
| MOTE | (aside) A message [[Well|well]] sympathized—a [[Horse|horse]] to be ambassador for an ass. |
| ARMADO | Ha, ha! What sayst thou? |
| MOTE | Marry, sir, you must send the ass upon the [[Horse|horse]], for he is very slow-gaited. But I go. |
| ARMADO | The way is but short. Away! |
| MOTE | As swift as lead, sir. 55 |
| ARMADO | The meaning, pretty ingenious?<br>Is not lead a metal heavy, dull, and slow ? |
| MOTE | Minime, honest master—or rather, master, no. |
| ARMADO | I say lead is slow. |
| MOTE | You are too swift, sir, to say so.<br>Is that lead slow which is fired from a gun? |
| ARMADO | Sweet smoke of rhetoric!<br>He reputes me a cannon, and the bullet, that’s he.<br>I shoot thee at the swain. |
| MOTE | Thump, then, and I flee. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit |
| ARMADO | A most acute juvenal—voluble and free of grace.<br>By thy favour, sweet welkin, I must sigh in thy face.<br>Most rude [[Melancholy|melancholy]], valour gives thee place.<br>My herald is returned. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Mote the page, and [[Costard]] the clown |
| MOTE | A [[Wonder|wonder]], master—here’s a [[Costard|costard]] broken in a shin. |
| ARMADO | Some enigma, some [[Riddle|riddle]]; come, thy l’envoi. Begin. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | No egma, no [[Riddle|riddle]], no l‘envoi, no salve in the mail, sir. O sir, plantain, a plain plantain—no l’envoi, no l’envoi, no salve, sir, but a plantain. |
| ARMADO | By virtue, thou enforcest laughter—thy silly thought my spleen. The heaving of my lungs provokes me to ridiculous smiling. O pardon me, my [[Stars|stars]]! Doth the inconsiderate take salve for l‘envoi, and the word l’envoi for a salve? |
| MOTE | Do the wise think them other? Is not l’envoi a salve ? |
| ARMADO | No, page, it is an epilogue or discourse to make plain<br>Some obscure precedence that hath tofore been sain.<br>I will example it. so<br>The fox, the ape, and the humble-bee<br>Were still at odds, being but three.<br>There’s the moral. Now the /’envoi. |
| MOTE | I will add the l’envoi. Say the moral again. |
| ARMADO | The fox, the ape, and the humble-bee<br>Were still at odds, being but three. |
| MOTE | Until the goose came out of door<br>And stayed the odds by adding four.<br>Now will I begin your moral, and do you follow with my l’envoi.<br>The fox, the ape, and the humble-bee<br>Were still at odds, being but three. |
| ARMADO | Until the goose came out of door,<br>Staying the odds by adding four. |
| MOTE | A good l’envoi, ending in the goose. Would you [[Desire|desire]] more? |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | The boy hath sold him a bargain—a goose, that’s flat.<br>Sir, your pennyworth is good an your goose be fat.<br>To sell a bargain [[Well|well]] is as cunning as fast and loose.<br>Let me see, a fat l’envoi-ay, that’s a fat goose. |
| ARMADO | Come hither, come hither. How did this argument begin? |
| MOTE | By saying that a [[Costard|costard]] was broken in a shin.<br>Then called you for the l’envoi. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | True, and I for a plantain. Thus came your argument in. Then the boy’s fat l’envoi, the goose that you bought, and he ended the market. |
| ARMADO | But tell me, how was there a [[Costard|costard]] broken in a shin? |
| MOTE | I will tell you sensibly. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | Thou hast no feeling of it. Mote, I will speak that l’envoi.<br>I, [[Costard]], running out, that was safely within,<br>Fell over the threshold and broke my shin. |
| ARMADO | We will talk no more of this matter. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | Till there be more matter in the shin. |
| ARMADO | Sirrah [[Costard]], I will enfranchise thee. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | O, marry me to one Frances! I smell some l’envoi, some goose, in this. |
| ARMADO | By my sweet [[Soul|soul]], I mean setting thee at liberty, enfreedoming thy person. Thou wert immured, restrained, captivated, bound. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | True, true, and now you will be my purgation and let me loose. |
| ARMADO | I give thee thy liberty, set thee from durance, and in lieu thereof impose on thee nothing but this: bear this significant to the country maid, Jaquenetta. (Giving him a letter) There is remuneration (giving him money), for the best ward of mine [[Honour|honour]] is rewarding my dependants. Mote, follow. Exit |
| MOTE | Like the sequel, I. Signor [[Costard]], adieu. Exit |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | My sweet ounce of man’s flesh, my incony Jew!<br>Now will I look to his remuneration. Remuneration—<br>O, that’s the Latin word for three-farthings. Three<br>farthings—remuneration. ‘What’s the price of this<br>inkle?’ ‘One penny.’ ‘No, I’ll give you a remuneration.’<br>Why, it carries it! Remuneration! Why, it is a fairer<br>name than French [[Crown|crown]]. I will never buy and sell out<br>of this word. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Biron |
| BIRON | My good knave [[Costard]], exceedingly [[Well|well]] met. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | Pray you, sir, how much carnation ribbon may a man buy for a remuneration? |
| BIRON | What is a remuneration? |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | Marry, sir, halfpenny-farthing. |
| BIRON | Why, then, three-farthing-worth of silk. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | I thank your worship. God be wi’ you. |
| BIRON | Stay, slave, I must employ thee.<br>As thou wilt win my favour, good my knave,<br>Do one thing for me that I shall entreat. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | When would you have it done, sir? |
| BIRON | This afternoon. |
| CUSTARD | [[Well]], I will do it, sir. Fare you [[Well|well]]. |
| BIRON | Thou knowest not what it is. |
| CUSTARD | I shall know, sir, when I have done it. |
| BIRON | Why, [[Villain|villain]], thou must know first. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | I will come to your worship tomorrow morning. |
| BIRON | It must be done this afternoon. Hark, slave,<br>It is but this:<br>The Princess comes to hunt here in the park,<br>And in her train there is a gentle lady.<br>When tongues speak sweetly, then they name her<br>name,<br>And [[Rosaline]] they call her. Ask for her,<br>And to her white hand see thou do commend<br>This sealed-up counsel. There’s thy guerdon (giving<br>him a letter and money), go. |
| [[Costard|COSTARD]] | Guerdon! O sweet guerdon!—better than remuneration, elevenpence-farthing better—most sweet guerdon! I will do it, sir, in print. Guerdon—remuneration. Exit |
| BIRON | And I, forsooth, in [[Love|love]]—I that have been [[Love|love]]’s whip,<br>A very beadle to a humorous sigh,<br>A critic, nay, a [[Night|night]]-watch constable,<br>A domineering pedant o‘er the boy,<br>Than whom no mortal so magnificent.<br>This wimpled, whining, purblind, wayward boy,<br>This Signor Junior, giant [[Dwarf|dwarf]], Dan Cupid,<br>Regent of [[Love|love]]-rhymes, lord of folded arms,<br>Th’anointed sovereign of sighs and groans,<br>Liege of all loiterers and malcontents,<br>Dread prince of plackets, [[King|king]] of codpieces,<br>Sole imperator and great general<br>Of trotting paritors—O my little heart!<br>And I to be a corporal of his field,<br>And wear his colours like a tumbler’s hoop!<br>What? I [[Love|love]], I sue, I seek a wife?—<br>A woman, that is like a German [[Clock|clock]],<br>Still a-repairing, ever out of frame,<br>And never going aright, being a watch,<br>But being watched that it may still go right.<br>Nay, to be perjured, which is worst of all,<br>And among three to [[Love|love]] the worst of all—<br>A whitely wanton with a velvet brow,<br>With two pitch-balls stuck in her face for eyes—<br>Ay, and, by heaven, one that will do the deed<br>Though Argus were her eunuch and her guard.<br>And I to sigh for her, to watch for her,<br>To pray for her—go to, it is a plague<br>That Cupid will impose for my neglect<br>Of his almighty dreadful little might.<br>[[Well]], I will [[Love|love]], write, sigh, pray, sue, groan:<br>Some men must [[Love|love]] my lady, and some Joan. Exit |
