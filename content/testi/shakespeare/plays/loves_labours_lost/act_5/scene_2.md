| *(didascalia)* | Enter the Princess and her ladies: [[Rosaline]], [[Maria]], and Catherine |
| PRINCESS | Sweethearts, we shall be rich ere we depart,<br>If fairings come thus plentifully in.<br>A lady walled about with diamonds—<br>Look you what I have from the loving [[King]]. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | Madam, came nothing else along with that? |
| PRINCESS | Nothing but this?—yes, as much [[Love|love]] in rhyme<br>As would be crammed up in a sheet of paper<br>Writ o’ both sides the leaf, margin and all,<br>That he was fain to seal on Cupid’s name. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | That was the way to make his godhead wax,<br>For he hath been five thousand year a boy. |
| CATHERINE | Ay, and a shrewd unhappy gallows, too. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | You’ll ne’er be friends with him, a killed your sister. |
| CATHERINE | He made her [[Melancholy|melancholy]], sad, and heavy,<br>And so she died. Had she been light like you,<br>Of such a merry, nimble, stirring spirit,<br>She might ha’ been a grandam ere she died;<br>And so may you, for a light heart lives long. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | What’s your dark meaning, mouse, of this light word? |
| CATHERINE | A light condition in a [[Beauty|beauty]] dark. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | We need more light to find your meaning out. |
| CATHERINE | You’ll mar the light by taking it in snuff,<br>Therefore I’ll darkly end the argument. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | Look what you do, you do it still i’th’ dark. |
| CATHERINE | So do not you, for you are a light wench. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | Indeed I weigh not you, and therefore light. |

| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | Great reason, for past care is still past cure. |

| CATHERINE | Fair as a text B in a copy-book. |

| [[Maria|MARIA]] | This and these pearls to me sent [[Longueville]].<br>The letter is too long by half a mile. |
| PRINCESS | I think no less. Dost thou not wish in heart<br>The chain were longer and the letter short? |
| [[Maria|MARIA]] | Ay, or I would these hands might never part. |
| PRINCESS | We are wise girls to mock our [[lovers]] so. |
| [[Rosaline|ROSALINE]] | They are worse fools to purchase mocking so.<br>That same Biron I’ll torture ere I go.<br>O that I knew he were but in by th’ week!—<br>How I would make him fawn, and beg, and seek,<br>And wait the season, and observe the times,<br>And spend his prodigal wits in bootless rhymes,<br>And shape his service wholly to my hests,<br>And make him proud to make me proud that jests!<br>So pursuivant-like would I o’ersway his state<br>That he should be my [[Fool|fool]], and I his fate. |
| PRINCESS | None are so surely caught when they are catched<br>As wit turned [[Fool|fool]]. Folly in wisdom hatched<br>Hath wisdom’s warrant, and the help of school,<br>And wit’s own grace, to grace a learned [[Fool|fool]]. |

| [[Love|LOVE]]’S LABOUR’S WON | A BRIEF ACCOUNT |
| IN | 1598, Francis Meres called as witnesses to Shakespeare’s excellence in [[Comedy|comedy]] ‘his Gentlemen of [[Verona]], his Errors, his [[Love]] Labour’s Lost, his [[Love]] Labour’s Wone, his Midsummer’s [[Night]] Dream, and his Merchant of [[Venice]]’. This was the only evidence that Shakespeare wrote a play called [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won until the discovery in 1953 of a [[Fragment|fragment]] of a bookseller’s list that had been used in the binding of a volume published in 1637/8. The [[Fragment|fragment]] itself appears to record titles sold from 9 to 17 August 1603 by a book dealer in the south of England. Among items headed ‘[inte]rludes & tragedyes’ are<br>marchant of vennis<br>taming of a shrew<br>knak to know a knave<br>knak to know an honest man<br>loves labor lost<br>loves labor won<br>No author is named for any of the items. All the plays named in the list except [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won are known to have been printed by 1600; all were written by 1596-7. Taken together, Meres’s reference in 1598 and the 1603 [[Fragment|fragment]] appear to demonstrate that a play by Shakespeare called [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won had been performed by the [[Time|time]] Meres wrote and was in print by August 1603. Conceivably the phrase served as an alternative title for one of Shakespeare’s other comedies, though the only one believed to have been written by 1598 but not listed by Meres is The Taming of the Shrew, which is named (as The Taming of A Shrew) in the bookseller’s [[Fragment|fragment]]. Otherwise we must suppose that [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won is the title of a lost play by Shakespeare, that no copy of the edition mentioned in the bookseller’s list is extant, and that Heminges and Condell failed to include it in the 1623 Folio.<br>None of these suppositions is implausible. We know of at least one other lost play attributed to Shakespeare (see [[Cardenio]], below), and of many lost works by contemporary playwrights. No copy of the first edition of [[Titus Andronicus]] was known until 1904; for I [[Henry IV]] and The Passionate [[Pilgrim]] only a [[Fragment|fragment]] of the first edition survives. And we now know that [[Troilus]] and [[Cressida]] was almost omitted from the 1623 Folio (probably for copyright reasons) despite its evident authenticity. It is also possible that, like most of the early editions of Shakespeare’s plays, the lost edition of [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won did not name him on the title-page, and this omission might go some way to explaining the failure of the edition to survive, or (if it does) to be noticed. [[Love]]’s Labour’s Won stands a much better chance of having survived, somewhere, than [[Cardenio]]: because it was printed, between 500 and 1,500 copies were once in circulation, whereas for [[Cardenio]] we know of only a single manuscript.<br>The evidence for the existence of the lost play (unlike that for [[Cardenio]]) gives us little indication of its content. Meres explicitly states, and the title implies, that it was a [[Comedy|comedy]]. Its titular pairing with [[Love]]’s Labour’s Lost suggests that they may have been written at about the same [[Time|time]]. Both Meres and the bookseller’s catalogue place it after [[Love]]’s Labour’s Lost; although neither list is necessarily chronological, Meres’s does otherwise agree with our own view of the order of composition of Shakespeare’s comedies. |
