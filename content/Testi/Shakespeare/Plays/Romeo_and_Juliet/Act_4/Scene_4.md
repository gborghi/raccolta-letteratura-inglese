| *(didascalia)* | Enter Capulet’s Wife, and the Nurse With herbs |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | Hold, take these keys, and fetch more spices, Nurse. |
| NURSE | They call for dates and quinces in the pastry. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Capulet |
| CAPULET | Come, stir, stir, stir! The second cock hath crowed.<br>The curfew bell hath rung. ’Tis three o’clock.<br>Look to the baked meats, good Angelica.<br>Spare not for cost. |
| NURSE | Go, you cot-quean, go.<br>Get you to bed. Faith, you’ll be sick tomorrow<br>For this night’s watching. |
| CAPULET | No, not a whit. What, I have watched ere now<br>All night for lesser cause, and ne’er been sick. |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | Ay, you have been a mouse-hunt in your time,<br>But I will watch you from such watching now. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Capulet’s Wife and Nurse |
| CAPULET | A jealous-hood, a jealous-hood! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter three or four Servingmen, with spits and logs and baskets |
| CAPULET | Now, fellow, what is there? |
| FIRST SERVINGMAN | Things for the cook, sir, but I know not what. |
| CAPULET | Make haste, make haste. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit First Servingman and one or two others |
| CAPULET | Sirrah, fetch drier logs.<br>Call Peter. He will show thee where they are. |
| SECOND SERVINGMAN | I have a head, sir, that will find out logs<br>And never trouble Peter for the matter. |
| CAPULET | Mass, and well said! A merry whoreson, ha!<br>Thou shalt be loggerhead. Exit Second Servingman<br><br>Good faith, ’tis day.<br>The County will be here with music straight,<br>For so he said he would. |
| *(didascalia)* | Music plays within |
| CAPULET | I hear him near.<br>Nurse! Wife! What ho, what, Nurse, I say! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the Nurse |
| CAPULET | Go waken Juliet. Go and trim her up.<br>I’ll go and chat with Paris. Hie, make haste,<br>Make haste, the bridegroom he is come already.<br>Make haste, I say. Exit |
| NURSE | Mistress, what, mistress! Juliet! Fast, I warrant her, she.<br>Why, lamb, why, lady! Fie, you slug-abed!<br>Why, love, I say, madam, sweetheart, why, bride!<br>What, not a word? You take your pennyworths now.<br>Sleep for a week, for the next night, I warrant,<br>The County Paris hath set up his rest<br>That you shall rest but little. God forgive me!<br>Marry, and amen. How sound is she asleep!<br>I needs must wake her. Madam, madam, madam!<br>Ay, let the County take you in your bed.<br>He’ll fright you up, i’faith. Will it not be? |
| *(didascalia)* | She draws back the curtains |
| NURSE | What, dressed and in your clothes, and down again?<br>I must needs wake you. Lady, lady, lady!<br>Alas, alas! Help, help! My lady’s dead.<br>O welladay, that ever I was born!<br>Some aqua-vitae, ho! My lord, my lady! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Capulet’s Wife |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | What noise is here? |
| NURSE | O lamentable day! |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | What is the matter? |
| NURSE | Look, look. O heavy day! |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | O me, O me, my child, my only life!<br>Revive, look up, or I will die with thee.<br>Help, help, call help! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Capulet |
| CAPULET | For shame, bring Juliet forth. Her lord is come. |
| NURSE | She’s dead, deceased. She’s dead, alack the day! |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | Alack the day, she’s dead, she’s dead, she’s dead! |
| CAPULET | Ha, let me see her! Out, alas, she’s cold.<br>Her blood is settled, and her joints are stiff.<br>Life and these lips have long been separated.<br>Death lies on her like an untimely frost<br>Upon the sweetest flower of all the field. |
| NURSE | O lamentable day! |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | O woeful time! |
| CAPULET | Death, that hath ta’en her hence to make me wail,<br>Ties up my tongue, and will not let me speak. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Friar Laurence and Paris, with Musicians |
| FRIAR LAURENCE | Come, is the bride ready to go to church? |
| CAPULET | Ready to go, but never to return.<br>(To Paris) O son, the night before thy wedding day<br>Hath death lain with thy wife. See, there she lies,<br>Flower as she was, deflowered by him.<br>Death is my son-in-law, death is my heir.<br>My daughter he hath wedded. I will die,<br>And leave him all. Life, living, all is death’s. |
| *(didascalia)* | Paris, Capulet and his Wife, and the Nurse all at once wring their hands and cry out together: |
| PARIS | Have I thought long to see this morning’s face,<br>And doth it give me such a sight as this?<br>Beguiled, divorced, wronged, spited, slain!<br>Most detestable death, by thee beguiled,<br>By cruel, cruel thee quite overthrown.<br>O love, O life: not life, but love in death. |
| CAPULET’S WIFE | Accursed, unhappy, wretched, hateful day!<br>Most miserable hour that e’er time saw<br>In lasting labour of his pilgrimage!<br>But one, poor one, one poor and loving child,<br>But one thing to rejoice and solace in,<br>And cruel death hath catched it from my sight! |
| NURSE | O woe! O woeful, woeful, woeful day!<br>Most lamentable day! Most woeful day<br>That ever, ever, I did yet behold!<br>O day, O day, O day, O hateful day,<br>Never was seen so black a day as this I<br>O woeful day, O woeful day! 85 |
| CAPULET | Despised, distressed, hated, martyred, killed!<br>Uncomfortable time, why cam’st thou now<br>To murder, murder our solemnity?<br>O child, O child, my soul and not my child!<br>Dead art thou, alack, my child is dead,<br>And with my child my joys are buried. |
| FRIAR LAURENCE | Peace, ho, for shame! Confusion’s cure lives not<br>In these confusions. Heaven and yourself<br>Had part in this fair maid. Now heaven hath all,<br>And all the better is it for the maid.<br>Your part in her you could not keep from death,<br>But heaven keeps his part in eternal life.<br>The most you sought was her promotion,<br>For ’twas your heaven she should be advanced,<br>And weep ye now, seeing she is advanced<br>Above the clouds as high as heaven itself?<br>O, in this love you love your child so ill<br>That you run mad, seeing that she is well.<br>She’s not well married that lives married long,<br>But she’s best married that dies married young.<br>Dry up your tears, and stick your rosemary<br>On this fair corpse, and, as the custom is,<br>All in her best array bear her to church;<br>For though fond nature bids us all lament,<br>Yet nature’s tears are reason’s merriment. |
| CAPULET | All things that we ordained festival<br>Turn from their office to black funeral.<br>Our instruments to melancholy bells,<br>Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast,<br>Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change;<br>Our bridal flowers serve for a buried corpse,<br>And all things change them to the contrary. |
| FRIAR LAURENCE | Sir, go you in; and madam, go with him,<br>And go, Sir Paris. Everyone prepare<br>To follow this fair corpse unto her grave.<br>The heavens do lour upon you for some ill.<br>Move them no more by crossing their high will. |
| *(didascalia)* | They cast rosemary on Juliet, and shut the curtains. Exeunt all but the Nurse and Musicians |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST MUSICIAN Faith, we may put up our pipes and be gone. |
| NURSE | Honest good fellows, ah, put up, put up,<br>For well you know this is a pitiful case. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST MUSICIAN |
| NURSE | Ay, by my troth, the case may be amended. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Nurse Enter Peter |
| PETER | Musicians, O, musicians! ‘Heart’s ease’, ‘Heart’s ease’; O,an you will have me live, play ‘Heart’s ease’. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST MUSICIAN Why ‘Heart’s ease’? |
| PETER | O, musicians, because my heart itself plays ‘My heart is full of woe’. O, play me some merry dump to comfort me. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST MUSICIAN Not a dump, we. ’Tis no time to play now. |
| PETER | You will not then? |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | No. |
| PETER | I will then give it you soundly. |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | What will you give us? |
| PETER | No money, on my faith, but the gleek. I will give you the minstrel. |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | Then will I give you the serving-creature. |
| PETER | (drawing his dagger) Then will I lay the serving-creature’s dagger on your pate. I will carry no crochets. I’ll re you, I’ll fa you. Do you note me? |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | An you re us and fa us, you note us. |
| SECOND MUSICIAN | Pray you, put up your dagger and put out your wit. |
| *(didascalia)* | PETER Then have at you with my wit. I will dry-beat you with an iron wit, and put up my iron dagger. Answer me like men. |
| *(didascalia)* | Sings |
| SECOND MUSICIAN | When griping grief the heart doth wound,<br>And doleful dumps the mind oppress,<br>Then music with her silver sound—<br>Why ‘silver sound’, why ‘music with her silver sound’?<br>What say you, Matthew Minikin? |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | Marry, sir, because silver hath a sweet sound. |
| PETER | Prates! What say you, Hugh Rebec? |
| SECOND MUSICIAN | I say ’silver sound’ because musicians sound for silver. |
| PETER | Prates too! What say you, Simon Soundpost? |
| THIRD MUSICIAN | Faith, I know not what to say. |
| PETER | O, I cry you mercy, you are the singer. I will say for you. It is ’music with her silver sound’ because musicians have no gold for sounding. |
| *(didascalia)* | Sings |
| PETER | Then music with her silver sound<br>With speedy help doth lend redress. Exit |
| FIRST MUSICIAN | What a pestilent knave is this same! |
| SECOND MUSICIAN | Hang him, jack! Come, we’ll in here, tarry for the mourners, and stay dinner. Exeunt |
