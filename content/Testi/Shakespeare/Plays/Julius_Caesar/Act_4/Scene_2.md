| *(didascalia)* | Drum. Enter Brutus, Lucius, and the army. Lucillius, Titinius, and Pindarus meet them |
| BRUTUS | Stand, ho! |
| *(didascalia)* | SOLDIER Give the word ‘ho’, and stand. |
| BRUTUS | What now, Lucillius: is Cassius near? |
| LUCILLIUS | He is at hand, and Pindarus is come<br>To do you salutation from his master. |
| BRUTUS | He greets me well. Your master, Pindarus,<br>In his own change or by ill officers,<br>Hath given me some worthy cause to wish<br>Things done undone. But if he be at hand,<br>I shall be satisfied. |
| PINDARUS | I do not doubt<br>But that my noble master will appear<br>Such as he is, full of regard and honour. |
| BRUTUS | He is not doubted.—A word, Lucillius.<br>Brutus and Lucillius speak apart<br><br>How he received you let me be resolved. |
| LUCILLIUS | With courtesy and with respect enough,<br>But not with such familiar instances,<br>Nor with such free and friendly conference,<br>As he hath used of old. |
| BRUTUS | Thou hast described<br>A hot friend cooling. Ever note, Lucillius:<br>When love begins to sicken and decay<br>It useth an enforced ceremony.<br>There are no tricks in plain and simple faith;<br>But hollow men, like horses hot at hand,<br>Make gallant show and promise of their mettle;<br>Low march within<br>But when they should endure the bloody spur,<br>They fall their crests and, like deceitful jades,<br>Sink in the trial. Comes his army on? |
| LUCILLIUS | They mean this night in Sardis to be quartered.<br>The greater part, the horse in general,<br>Are come with Cassius. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Cassius and his powers |
| BRUTUS | Hark, he is arrived.<br>March gently on to meet him.<br>The armies march |
| CASSIUS | Stand, ho! |
| BRUTUS | Stand, ho! Speak the word along. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SOLDIER Stand! |
| *(didascalia)* | second SOLDIER Stand! |
| *(didascalia)* | THIRD SOLDIER Standl |
| CASSIUS | Most noble brother, you have done me wrong. |
| BRUTUS | Judge me, you gods: wrong I mine enemies?<br>And if not so, how should I wrong a brother? |
| CASSIUS | Brutus, this sober form of yours hides wrongs,<br>And when you do them— |
| BRUTUS | Cassius, be content.<br>Speak your griefs softly. I do know you well.<br>Before the eyes of both our armies here,<br>Which should perceive nothing but love from us,<br>Let us not wrangle. Bid them move away,<br>Then in my tent, Cassius, enlarge your griefs,<br>And I will give you audience. |
| CASSIUS | Pindarus,<br>Bid our commanders lead their charges off<br>A little from this ground. |
| BRUTUS | Lucillius, do you the like; and let no man<br>Come to our tent till we have done our conference.<br>Let Lucius and Titinius guard our door. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt the armies Brutus and Cassius remain, with Titinius, and Lucius guarding the door |
| CASSIUS | That you have wronged me doth appear in this:<br>You have condemned and noted Lucius Pella<br>For taking bribes here of the Sardians,<br>Wherein my letters praying on his side,<br>Because I knew the man, was slighted off. |
| BRUTUS | You wronged yourself to write in such a case. |
| CASSIUS | In such a time as this it is not meet<br>That every nice offence should bear his comment. |
| BRUTUS | Let me tell you, Cassius, you yourself<br>Are much condemned to have an itching palm,<br>To sell and mart your offices for gold<br>To undeservers. |
| CASSIUS | I, an itching palm?<br>You know that you are Brutus that speaks this, 65<br>Or, by the gods, this speech were else your last. |
| BRUTUS | The name of Cassius honours this corruption,<br>And chastisement doth therefore hide his head. |
| CASSIUS | Chastisement? |
| BRUTUS | Remember March, the ides of March, remember.<br>Did not great Julius bleed for justice’ sake?<br>What villain touched his body, that did stab,<br>And not for justice? What, shall one of us,<br>That struck the foremost man of all this world<br>But for supporting robbers, shall we now<br>Contaminate our fingers with base bribes,<br>And sell the mighty space of our large honours<br>For so much trash as may be grasped thus?<br>I had rather be a dog and bay the moon<br>Than such a Roman. |
| CASSIUS | Brutus, bay not me.<br>I’ll not endure it. You forget yourself<br>To hedge me in. I am a soldier, I,<br>Older in practice, abler than yourself<br>To make conditions. |
| BRUTUS | Go to, you are not, Cassius. |
| CASSIUS | I am. |
| BRUTUS | I say you are not. |
| CASSIUS | Urge me no more, I shall forget myself.<br>Have mind upon your health. Tempt me no farther. |
| BRUTUS | Away, slight man. |
| CASSIUS | Is’t possible? |
| BRUTUS | Hear me, for I will speak.<br>Must I give way and room to your rash choler?<br>Shall I be frighted when a madman stares? |
| CASSIUS | O ye gods, ye gods! Must I endure all this? |
| BRUTUS | All this? Ay, more. Fret till your proud heart break.<br>Go show your slaves how choleric you are,<br>And make your bondmen tremble. Must I budge?<br>Must I observe you? Must I stand and crouch<br>Under your testy humour? By the gods,<br>You shall digest the venom of your spleen,<br>Though it do split you. For from this day forth<br>I’ll use you for my mirth, yea for my laughter,<br>When you are waspish. |
| CASSIUS | Is it come to this? |
| BRUTUS | You say you are a better soldier.<br>Let it appear so, make your vaunting true,<br>And it shall please me well. For mine own part,<br>I shall be glad to learn of noble men. |
| CASSIUS | You wrong me every way, you wrong me, Brutus.<br>I said an elder soldier, not a better.<br>Did I say better? |
| BRUTUS | If you did, I care not. |
| CASSIUS | When Caesar lived he durst not thus have moved me. |
| BRUTUS | Peace, peace; you durst not so have tempted him. |
| CASSIUS | I durst not? |
| BRUTUS | No. |
| CASSIUS | What, durst not tempt him? |
| BRUTUS | For your life you durst not. |
| CASSIUS | Do not presume too much upon my love.<br>I may do that I shall be sorry for. |
| BRUTUS | You have done that you should be sorry for.<br>There is no terror, Cassius, in your threats,<br>For I am armed so strong in honesty<br>That they pass by me as the idle wind,<br>Which I respect not. I did send to you<br>For certain sums of gold, which you denied me;<br>For I can raise no money by vile means.<br>By heaven, I had rather coin my heart<br>And drop my blood for drachmas than to wring<br>From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash<br>By any indirection. I did send<br>To you for gold to pay my legions,<br>Which you denied me. Was that done like Cassius?<br>Should I have answered Caius Cassius so?<br>When Marcus Brutus grows so covetous<br>To lock such rascal counters from his friends,<br>Be ready, gods, with all your thunderbolts;<br>Dash him to pieces. |
| CASSIUS | I denied you not. |
| BRUTUS | You did. |
| CASSIUS | I did not. He was but a fool<br>That brought my answer back. Brutus hath rived my<br>heart.<br>A friend should bear his friend’s infirmities,<br>But Brutus makes mine greater than they are. |
| BRUTUS | I do not, till you practise them on me. |
| CASSIUS | You love me not. |
| BRUTUS | I do not like your faults. |
| CASSIUS | A friendly eye could never see such faults. |
| BRUTUS | A flatterer’s would not, though they do appear<br>As huge as high Olympus. |
| CASSIUS | Come, Antony and young Octavius, come,<br>Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius;<br>For Cassius is aweary of the world,<br>Hated by one he loves, braved by his brother,<br>Checked like a bondman; all his faults observed,<br>Set in a notebook, learned and conned by rote,<br>To cast into my teeth. O, I could weep<br>My spirit from mine eyes! There is my dagger,<br>And here my naked breast; within, a heart<br>Dearer than Pluto’s mine, richer than gold.<br>If that thou beest a Roman, take it forth.<br>I that denied thee gold will give my heart.<br>Strike as thou didst at Caesar; for I know<br>When thou didst hate him worst, thou loved‘st him<br>better<br>Than ever thou loved’st Cassius. |
| BRUTUS | Sheathe your dagger.<br>Be angry when you will; it shall have scope.<br>Do what you will; dishonour shall be humour.<br>O Cassius, you are yoked with a lamb<br>That carries anger as the flint bears fire,<br>Who, much enforced, shows a hasty spark<br>And straight is cold again. |
| CASSIUS | Hath Cassius lived<br>To be but mirth and laughter to his Brutus<br>When grief and blood ill-tempered vexeth him? |
| BRUTUS | When I spoke that, I was ill-tempered too. |
| CASSIUS | Do you confess so much? Give me your hand. |
| BRUTES | And my heart too. |
| *(didascalia)* | They embrace |
| CASSIUS | O Brutus! |
| BRUTUS | What’s the matter? |
| CASSIUS | Have not you love enough to bear with me<br>When that rash humour which my mother gave me<br>Makes me forgetful? |
| BRUTUS | Yes, Cassius, and from henceforth,<br>When you are over-earnest with your Brutus,<br>He’ll think your mother chides, and leave you so. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Lucillius and a Poet |
| POET | Let me go in to see the generals.<br>There is some grudge between ’em; ’tis not meet<br>They be alone. |
| LUCILLIUS | You shall not come to them. |
| POET | Nothing but death shall stay me. |
| CASSIUS | How now! What’s the matter? |
| POET | For shame, you generals, what do you mean?<br>Love and be friends, as two such men should be,<br>For I have seen more years, I’m sure, than ye. |
| CASSIUS | Ha, ha! How vilely doth this cynic rhyme! |
| BRUTUS | (to the Poet)<br>Get you hence, sirrah; saucy fellow, hence! |
| CASSIUS | Bear with him, Brutus, ’tis his fashion. |
| BRUTUS | I’ll know his humour when he knows his time.<br>What should the wars do with these jigging fools?<br>(To the Poet) Companion, hence! |
| CASSIUS | (to the Poet) Away, away, be gone! |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Poet |
| BRUTUS | Lucillius and Titinius, bid the commanders<br>Prepare to lodge their companies tonight. |
| CASSIUS | And come yourselves, and bring Messala with you<br>Immediately to us. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Lucillius and Titinius |
| BRUTUS | Lucius, a bowl of wine. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Lucius |
| CASSIUS | I did not think you could have been so angry. |
| BRUTUS | O Cassius, I am sick of many griefs. |
| CASSIUS | Of your philosophy you make no use,<br>If you give place to accidental evils. |
| BRUTUS | No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead. |
| CASSIUS | Ha! Portia? |
| BRUTUS | She is dead. |
| CASSIUS | How scaped I killing when I crossed you so?<br>O insupportable and touching loss!<br>Upon what sickness? |
| BRUTUS | Impatience of my absence,<br>And grief that young Octavius with Mark Antony<br>Have made themselves so strong—for with her death<br>That tidings came. With this, she fell distraught,<br>And, her attendants absent, swallowed fire. |
| CASSIUS | And died so? |
| BRUTUS | Even so. |
| CASSIUS | Oye immortal gods! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Lucius, with wine and tapers |
| BRUTUS | Speak no more of her. (To Lucius) Give me a bowl of<br>wine.<br>(To Cassius) In this I bury all unkindness, Cassius.<br>He drinks |
| CASSIUS | My heart is thirsty for that noble pledge.<br>Fill, Lucius, till the wine o’erswell the cup.<br>I cannot drink too much of Brutus’ love.<br>He drinks. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Lucius Enter Titinius and Messala |
| BRUTUS | Come in, Titinius; welcome, good Messala.<br>Now sit we close about this taper here,<br>And call in question our necessities. |
| CASSIUS | (aside)<br>Portia, art thou gone? |
| BRUTUS | No more, I pray you. |
| *(didascalia)* | They sit |
| BRUTUS | Messala, I have here received letters<br>That young Octavius and Mark Antony<br>Come down upon us with a mighty power,<br>Bending their expedition toward Philippi. |
| MESSALA | Myself have letters of the selfsame tenor. |
| BRUTUS | With what addition? |
| MESSALA | That by proscription and bills of outlawry<br>Octavius, Antony, and Lepidus<br>Have put to death an hundred senators. |
| BRUTUS | Therein our letters do not well agree.<br>Mine speak of seventy senators that died<br>By their proscriptions, Cicero being one. |
| CASSIUS | Cicero one? |
| MESSALA | Ay, Cicero is dead,<br>And by that order of proscription.<br>(To Brutus)<br>Had you your letters from your wife, my lord? |
| BRUTUS | No, Messala. |
| MESSALA | Nor nothing in your letters writ of her? |
| BRUTUS | Nothing, Messala. |
| MESSALA | That methinks is strange. |
| BRUTUS | Why ask you? Hear you aught of her in yours? |
| MESSALA | No, my lord. |
| BRUTUS | Now as you are a Roman, tell me true. |
| MESSALA | Then like a Roman bear the truth I tell;<br>For certain she is dead, and by strange manner. |
| BRUTUS | Why, farewell, Portia. We must die, Messala.<br>With meditating that she must die once,<br>I have the patience to endure it now. |
| MESSALA | Even so great men great losses should endure. |
| CASSIUS | I have as much of this in art as you,<br>But yet my nature could not bear it so. |
| BRUTUS | Well, to our work alive. What do you think<br>Of marching to Philippi presently? |
| CASSIUS | I do not think it good. |
| BRUTUS | Your reason? |
| CASSIUS | This it is:<br>’Tis better that the enemy seek us;<br>So shall he waste his means, weary his soldiers,<br>Doing himself offence; whilst we, lying still,<br>Are full of rest, defence, and nimbleness. |
| BRUTUS | Good reasons must of force give place to better.<br>The people ’twixt Philippi and this ground<br>Do stand but in a forced affection,<br>For they have grudged us contribution.<br>The enemy marching along by them<br>By them shall make a fuller number up,<br>Come on refreshed, new added, and encouraged;<br>From which advantage shall we cut him off,<br>If at Philippi we do face him there,<br>These people at our back. |
| CASSIUS | Hear me, good brother. |
| BRUTUS | Under your pardon. You must note beside<br>That we have tried the utmost of our friends;<br>Our legions are brim-full, our cause is ripe.<br>The enemy increaseth every day;<br>We at the height are ready to decline.<br>There is a tide in the affairs of men<br>Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune;<br>Omitted, all the voyage of their life<br>Is bound in shallows and in miseries.<br>On such a full sea are we now afloat,<br>And we must take the current when it serves,<br>Or lose our ventures. |
| CASSIUS | Then, with your will, go on.<br>We’ll along ourselves, and meet them at Philippi. |
| BRUTUS | The deep of night is crept upon our talk,<br>And nature must obey necessity,<br>Which we will niggard with a little rest.<br>There is no more to say. |
| CASSIUS | No more. Good night.<br>Early tomorrow will we rise and hence. |
| BRUTUS | Lucius. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Lucius |
| BRUTUS | My gown. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Lucius |
| BRUTUS | Farewell, good Messala.<br>Good night, Titinius. Noble, noble, Cassius,<br>Good night and good repose. |
| CASSIUS | O my dear brother,<br>This was an ill beginning of the night!<br>Never come such division ’tween our souls.<br>Let it not, Brutus. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Lucius with the gown |
| BRUTUS | Everything is well. |
| CASSIUS | Good night, my lord. |
| BRUTUS | Good night, good brother. |
| TITINIUS | and MESSALA<br>Good night, Lord Brutus. |
| BRUTUS | Farewell, every one. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Cassius, Titinius, and Messala |
| BRUTUS | Give me the gown. |
| *(didascalia)* | He puts on the gown |
| BRUTUS | Where is thy instrument? |
| LUCIUS | Here in the tent. |
| BRUTUS | What, thou speak’t drowsily.<br>Poor knave, I blame thee not; thou art o’erwatched.<br>Call Claudio and some other of my men.<br>I’ll have them sleep on cushions in my tent. |
| LUCIUS | Varrus and Claudio! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Varrus and Claudio |
| VARRUS | Calls my lord? |
| BRUTUS | I pray you, sirs, lie in my tent and sleep.<br>It may be I shall raise you by and by<br>On business to my brother Cassius. |
| VARRUS | So please you, we will stand and watch your pleasure. |
| BRUTUS | I will not have it so. Lie down, good sirs.<br>It may be I shall otherwise bethink me.<br>Varrus and Claudio lie down to sleep<br><br>Look, Lucius, here’s the book I sought for so.<br>I put it in the pocket of my gown. |
| LUCIUS | I was sure your lordship did not give it me. |
| BRUTUS | Bear with me, good boy, I am much forgetful.<br>Canst thou hold up thy heavy eyes a while,<br>And touch thy instrument a strain or two? |
| LUCIUS | Ay, my lord, an’t please you. |
| BRUTUS | It does, my boy.<br>I trouble thee too much, but thou art willing. |
| LUCIUS | It is my duty, sir. |
| BRUTUS | I should not urge thy duty past thy might.<br>I know young bloods look for a time of rest. |
| LUCIUS | I have slept, my lord, already. |
| BRUTUS | It was well done, and thou shalt sleep again.<br>I will not hold thee long. If I do live,<br>I will be good to thee.<br>Lucius plays music and sings a song, and so falls asleep<br>This is a sleepy tune. O murd‘rous slumber,<br>Lay’st thou thy leaden mace upon my boy<br>That plays thee music?—Gentle knave, good night.<br>I will not do thee so much wrong to wake thee.<br>If thou dost nod thou break’st thy instrument;<br>I’ll take it from thee, and, good boy, good night.<br>He takes away Lucius’ instrument, then opens the book<br>Let me see, let me see, is not the leaf turned down<br>Where I left reading? Here it is, I think. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the Ghost of Caesar |
| BRUTUS | How ill this taper burns! Ha! Who comes here?<br>I think it is the weakness of mine eyes<br>That shapes this monstrous apparition.<br>It comes upon me. Art thou any thing?<br>Art thou some god, some angel, or some devil,<br>That mak’st my blood cold and my hair to stare?<br>Speak to me what thou art. |
| GHOST | Thy evil spirit, Brutus. |
| BRUTUS | Why com’st thou? |
| GHOST | To tell thee thou shalt see me at Philippi. |
| BRUTUS | Well; then I shall see thee again? |
| GHOST | Ay, at Philippi. |
| BRUTUS | Why, I will see thee at Philippi then. Exit Ghost<br><br>Now I have taken heart, thou vanishest.<br>Ill spirit, I would hold more talk with thee.—<br>Boy, Lucius, Varrus, Claudio, sirs, awake!<br>Claudio! |
| LUCIUS | The strings, my lord, are false. |
| BRUTUS | He thinks he still is at his instrument.—<br>Lucius, awake! |
| LUCIUS | My lord. |
| BRUTUS | Didst thou dream, Lucius, that thou so cried’st out? |
| LUCIUS | My lord, I do not know that I did cry. |
| BRUTUS | Yes, that thou didst. Didst thou see anything? |
| LUCIUS | Nothing, my lord. |
| BRUTUS | Sleep again, Lucius.—Sirrah Claudio!<br>(To Varrus) Fellow,<br>Thou, awake! |
| VARRUS | My lord. |
| CLAUDIO | My lord. |
| BRUTUS | Why did you so cry out, sirs, in your sleep? |
| BOTH | Did we, my lord? |
| BRUTUS | Ay. Saw you anything? |
| VARRUS | No, my lord, I saw nothing. |
| CLAUDIO | Nor I, my lord. |
| BRUTUS | Go and commend me to my brother Cassius.<br>Bid him set on his powers betimes before,<br>And we will follow. |
| BOTH | It shall be done, my lord. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Varrus and Claudio at one door, Brutus and Lucius at another door |
