| *(didascalia)* | Enter Pandarus and Cressida |
| PANDARUS | Be moderate, be moderate. |
| CRESSIDA | Why tell you me of moderation?<br>The grief is fine, full, perfect that I taste,<br>And violenteth in a sense as strong<br>As that which causeth it. How can I moderate it?<br>If I could temporize with my affection<br>Or brew it to a weak and colder palate,<br>The like allayment could I give my grief.<br>My love admits no qualifying dross;<br>No more my grief, in such a precious loss. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Troilus |
| PANDARUS | Here, here, here he comes. Ah, sweet ducks! |
| CRESSIDA | (embracing him) O Troilus, Troilus! |
| PANDARUS | What a pair of spectacles is here! Let me<br>embrace you too. ‘O heart‘, as the goodly saying is,<br>‘O heart, heavy heart,<br>Why sigh’st thou without breaking?’<br>where he answers again<br>‘Because thou canst not ease thy smart<br>By friendship nor by speaking.’<br>There was never a truer rhyme. Let us cast away<br>nothing, for we may live to have need of such a verse.<br>We see it, we see it. How now, lambs? |
| TROILUS | Cressid, I love thee in so strained a purity<br>That the blest gods, as angry with my fancy—<br>More bright in zeal than the devotion which<br>Cold lips blow to their deities—take thee from me. |
| CRESSIDA | Have the gods envy? |
| PANDARUS | Ay, ay, ay, ay, ’tis too plain a case. |
| CRESSIDA | And is it true that I must go from Troy? |
| TROILUS | A hateful truth. |
| CRESSIDA | What, and from Troilus too? |
| TROILUS | From Troy and Troilus. |
| CRESSIDA | Is’t possible? |
| TROILUS | And suddenty—where injury of chance<br>Puts back leave-taking, jostles roughly by<br>All time of pause, rudely beguiles our lips<br>Of all rejoindure, forcibly prevents<br>Our locked embrasures, strangles our dear vows<br>Even in the birth of our own labouring breath.<br>We two, that with so many thousand sighs<br>Did buy each other, must poorly sell ourselves<br>With the rude brevity and discharge of one.<br>Injurious Time now with a robber’s haste<br>Crams his rich thiev’ry up, he knows not how.<br>As many farewells as be stars in heaven,<br>With distinct breath and consigned kisses to them,<br>He fumbles up into a loose adieu<br>And scants us with a single famished kiss,<br>Distasted with the salt of broken tears. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Aeneas |
| AENEAS | My lord, is the lady ready? |
| TROILUS | (to Cressida)<br>Hark, you are called. Some say the genius so<br>Cries ‘Come!’ to him that instantly must die. |
| *(didascalia)* | To Pandarus Bid them have patience. She shall come |
| TROILUS | anon. |
| PANDARUS | Where are my tears? Rain, to lay this wind, or my heart will be blown up by the root. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit with Aeneas |
| CRESSIDA | I must then to the Grecians. |
| TROILUS | No remedy. |
| CRESSIDA | A woeful Cressid ’mongst the merry Greeks!<br>When shall we see again? |
| TROILUS | Hear me, my love: be thou but true of heart— |
| CRESSIDA | I true? How now! What wicked deem is this? |
| TROILUS | Nay, we must use expostulation kindly,<br>For it is parting from us.<br>I speak not ‘Be thou true’ as fearing thee—<br>For I will throw my glove to Death himself<br>That there’s no maculation in thy heart—<br>But ‘Be thou true’ say I, to fashion in<br>My sequent protestation: ‘Be thou true,<br>And I will see thee’. |
| CRESSIDA | O you shall be exposed, my lord, to dangers<br>As infinite as imminent. But I’ll be true. |
| TROILUS | And I’ll grow friend with danger. Wear this sleeve. |
| CRESSIDA | And you this glove. When shall I see you? |
| TROILUS | I will corrupt the Grecian sentinels<br>To give thee nightly visitation.<br>But yet, be true. |
| CRESSIDA | O heavens! ’Be true’ again! |
| TROILUS | Hear why I speak it, love.<br>The Grecian youths are full of quality,<br>Their loving well composed, with gifts of nature<br>flowing,<br>And swelling o’er with arts and exercise.<br>How novelty may move, and parts with person,<br>Alas, a kind of godly jealousy—<br>Which I beseech you call a virtuous sin—<br>Makes me afeard. |
| CRESSIDA | O heavens, you love me not! |
| TROILUS | Die I a villain then!<br>In this I do not call your faith in question<br>So mainly as my merit. I cannot sing,<br>Nor heel the high lavolt, nor sweeten talk,<br>Nor play at subtle games—fair virtues all,<br>To which the Grecians are most prompt and<br>pregnant.<br>But I can tell that in each grace of these<br>There lurks a still and dumb-discoursive devil<br>That tempts most cunningly. But be not tempted. |
| CRESSIDA | Do you think I will? |
| TROILUS | No, but something may be done that we will not,<br>And sometimes we are devils to ourselves,<br>When we will tempt the frailty of our powers,<br>Presuming on their changeful potency. |
| AENEAS | (within)<br>Nay, good my lord! |
| TROILUS | Come, kiss, and let us part. |
| *(didascalia)* | PARIS art the door |
| TROILUS | Brother Troilus? |
| TROILUS | Good brother, come you hither,<br>And bring Aeneas and the Grecian with you. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Paris |
| CRESSIDA | My lord, will you be true? |
| TROILUS | Who, I? Alas, it is my vice, my fault.<br>Whiles others fish with craft for great opinion,<br>I with great truth catch mere simplicity;<br>Whilst some with cunning gild their copper crowns,<br>With truth and plainness I do wear mine bare. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Paris, Aeneas, Antenor, Deiphobus, and Diomedes |
| TROILUS | Fear not my truth. The moral of my wit<br>Is ‘plain and true!’; there’s all the reach of it.—<br>Welcome, Sir Diomed. Here is the lady<br>Which for Antenor we deliver you.<br>At the port, lord, I’ll give her to thy hand,<br>And by the way possess thee what she is.<br>Entreat her fair, and by my soul, fair Greek,<br>If e’er thou stand at mercy of my sword,<br>Name Cressid, and thy life shall be as safe<br>As Priam is in Ilium. |
| DIOMEDES | Fair Lady Cressid,<br>So please you, save the thanks this prince expects.<br>The lustre in your eye, heaven in your cheek,<br>Pleads your fair usage; and to Diomed<br>You shall be mistress, and command him wholly. |
| TROILUS | Grecian, thou dost not use me courteously,<br>To shame the zeal of my petition towards thee<br>In praising her. I tell thee, lord of Greece,<br>She is as far high-soaring o’er thy praises<br>As thou unworthy to be called her servant.<br>I charge thee use her well, even for my charge;<br>For, by the dreadful Pluto, if thou dost not,<br>Though the great bulk Achilles be thy guard<br>I’ll cut thy throat. |
| DIOMEDES | O be not moved, Prince Troilus.<br>Let me be privileged by my place and message<br>To be a speaker free. When I am hence<br>I’ll answer to my lust. And know you, lord,<br>I’ll nothing do on charge. To her own worth<br>She shall be prized; but that you say ‘Be’t so’,<br>I’ll speak it in my spirit and honour ‘No!’ |
| TROILUS | Come, to the port.—I’ll tell thee, Diomed,<br>This brave shall oft make thee to hide thy head.—<br>Lady, give me your hand, and as we walk<br>To our own selves bend we our needful talk. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Troilus, Cressida, and Diomedes |
| TROILUS | A trumpet sounds |
| PARIS | Hark, Hector’s trumpet. |
| AENEAS | How have we spent this morning?<br>The Prince must think me tardy and remiss,<br>That swore to ride before him in the field. |
| PARIS | ‘Tis Troilus’ fault. Come, come to field with him. |
| DEIPHOBUS | Let us make ready straight. |
| AENEAS | Yea, with a bridegroom’s fresh alacrity<br>Let us address to tend on Hector’s heels.<br>The glory of our Troy doth this day lie<br>On his fair worth and single chivalry. Exeunt |
