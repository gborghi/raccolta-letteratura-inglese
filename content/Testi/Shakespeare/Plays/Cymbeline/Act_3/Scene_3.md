| *(didascalia)* | Enter Belarius, followed by Guiderius and Arviragus, from a cave in the woods |
| BELARIUS | A goodly day not to keep house with such<br>Whose roof’s as low as ours. Stoop, boys; this gate<br>Instructs you how t‘adore the heavens, and bows you<br>To a morning’s holy office. The gates of monarchs<br>Are arched so high that giants may jet through<br>And keep their impious turbans on without<br>Good morrow to the sun. Hail, thou fair heaven!<br>We house i’th’ rock, yet use thee not so hardly<br>As prouder livers do. |
| GUIDERIUS | Hail, heaven! |
| ARVIRAGUS | Hail, heaven! |
| BELARIUS | Now for our mountain sport. Up to yon hill,<br>Your legs are young; I’ll tread these flats. Consider,<br>When you above perceive me like a crow,<br>That it is place which lessens and sets off,<br>And you may then revolve what tales I have told you<br>Of courts, of princes, of the tricks in war;<br>That service is not service, so being done,<br>But being so allowed. To apprehend thus<br>Draws us a profit from all things we see,<br>And often to our comfort shall we find<br>The sharded beetle in a safer hold<br>Than is the full-winged eagle. O, this life<br>Is nobler than attending for a check,<br>Richer than doing nothing for a bauble,<br>Prouder than rustling in unpaid-for silk;<br>Such gain the cap of him that makes ’em fine,<br>Yet keeps his book uncrossed. No life to ours. |
| GUIDERIUS | Out of your proof you speak. We, poor unfledged,<br>Have never winged from view o’th’ nest, nor know<br>not<br>What air’s from home. Haply this life is best,<br>If quiet life be best; sweeter to you<br>That have a sharper known; well corresponding<br>With your stiff age, but unto us it is<br>A cell of ignorance, travelling abed,<br>A prison for a debtor, that not dares<br>To stride a limit. |
| ARVIRAGUS | (to Belarius) What should we speak of<br>When we are old as you? When we shall hear<br>The rain and wind beat dark December, how,<br>In this our pinching cave, shall we discourse<br>The freezing hours away? We have seen nothing.<br>We are beastly: subtle as the fox for prey,<br>Like warlike as the wolf for what we eat.<br>Our valour is to chase what flies; our cage<br>We make a choir, as doth the prisoned bird,<br>And sing our bondage freely. |
| BELARIUS | How you speak!<br>Did you but know the city’s usuries,<br>And felt them knowingly; the art o‘th’ court,<br>As hard to leave as keep, whose top to climb<br>Is certain falling, or so slipp’ry that<br>The fear’s as bad as falling; the toil o‘th’ war,<br>A pain that only seems to seek out danger<br>I’th’ name of fame and honour, which dies i‘th’ search<br>And hath as oft a sland’rous epitaph<br>As record of fair act; nay, many times<br>Doth ill deserve by doing well; what’s worse,<br>Must curtsy at the censure. O boys, this story<br>The world may read in me. My body’s marked<br>With Roman swords, and my report was once<br>First with the best of note. Cymbeline loved me,<br>And when a soldier was the theme my name<br>Was not far off. Then was I as a tree<br>Whose boughs did bend with fruit; but in one night<br>A storm or robbery, call it what you will,<br>Shook down my mellow hangings, nay, my leaves,<br>And left me bare to weather. |
| GUIDERIUS | Uncertain favour! |
| BELARIUS | My fault being nothing, as I have told you oft,<br>But that two villains, whose false oaths prevailed<br>Before my perfect honour, swore to Cymbeline<br>I was confederate with the Romans. So<br>Followed my banishment, and this twenty years<br>This rock and these demesnes have been my world,<br>Where I have lived at honest freedom, paid<br>More pious debts to heaven than in all<br>The fore-end of my time. But up to th’ mountains!<br>This is not hunter’s language. He that strikes<br>The venison first shall be the lord o’th’ feast,<br>To him the other two shall minister,<br>And we will fear no poison which attends<br>In place of greater state. I’ll meet you in the valleys. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Guiderius and Arviragus |
| BELARIUS | How hard it is to hide the sparks of nature!<br>These boys know little they are sons to th’ King,<br>Nor Cymbeline dreams that they are alive.<br>They think they are mine, and though trained up<br>thus meanly<br>I‘th’ cave wherein they bow, their thoughts do hit<br>The roofs of palaces, and nature prompts them<br>In simple and low things to prince it much<br>Beyond the trick of others. This Polydore,<br>The heir of Cymbeline and Britain, who<br>The King his father called Guiderius—Jove,<br>When on my three-foot stool I sit and tell<br>The warlike feats I have done, his spirits fly out<br>Into my story: say ‘Thus mine enemy fell,<br>And thus I set my foot on ’s neck’, even then<br>The princely blood flows in his cheek, he sweats,<br>Strains his young nerves, and puts himself in posture<br>That acts my words. The younger brother, Cadwal,<br>Once Arviragus, in as like a figure<br>Strikes life into my speech, and shows much more<br>His own conceiving. |
| *(didascalia)* | A hunting-horn sounds |
| BELARIUS | Hark, the game is roused!<br>O Cymbeline, heaven and my conscience knows<br>Thou didst unjustly banish me, whereon<br>At three and two years old I stole these babes,<br>Thinking to bar thee of succession as<br>Thou reft’st me of my lands. Euriphile,<br>Thou wast their nurse; they took thee for their<br>mother,<br>And every day do honour to her grave.<br>Myself, Belarius, that am Morgan called,<br>They take for natural father. |
| *(didascalia)* | A hunting-horn sounds |
| BELARIUS | The game is up. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit |
