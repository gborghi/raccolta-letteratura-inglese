| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[King]] Harry and the [[Duke of Gloucester]], then the Duke of [[Clarence]] |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | [[Gloucester]], ’tis true that we are in great danger;<br>The greater therefore should our [[Courage|courage]] be.<br>Good morrow, brother [[Clarence]]. God Almighty!<br>There is some [[Soul|soul]] of goodness in things evil,<br>Would men observingly distil it out—<br>For our bad neighbour makes us early stirrers,<br>Which is both healthful and good husbandry.<br>Besides, they are our outward consciences,<br>And preachers to us all, admonishing<br>That we should dress us fairly for our end.<br>Thus may we gather honey from the weed<br>And make a moral of the devil himself. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Sir Thomas Erpingham |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Good morrow, old Sir Thomas Erpingham.<br>A good soft pillow for that good white head<br>Were better than a churlish turf of France. |
| ERPINGHAM | Not so, my liege. This lodging likes me better,<br>Since I may say, ‘Now lie I like a [[King|king]].’ |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | ’Tis good for men to [[Love|love]] their present pains<br>Upon example. So the spirit is eased,<br>And when the mind is quickened, out of [[Doubt|doubt]]<br>The organs, though defunct and dead before,<br>Break up their drowsy [[Grave|grave]] and newly move<br>With casted slough and fresh legerity.<br>Lend me thy cloak, Sir Thomas.<br>He puts on Erpingham’s cloak<br>Brothers both,<br>Commend me to the princes in our camp.<br>Do my good morrow to them, and anon<br>[[Desire]] them all to my pavilion. |
| [[Gloucester|GLOUCESTER]] | We shall, my liege. |
| ERPINGHAM | Shall I attend your grace? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | No, my good knight. Go with my brothers to my lords of England. I and my bosom must [[debate]] awhile, And then I would no other company. |
| ERPINGHAM | The Lord in heaven bless thee, noble Harry. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | God-a-[[Mercy|mercy]], old heart, thou speak’st cheerfully. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt all but [[King]] Harry |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter [[Pistol]] to him |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Qui vous là? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | A friend. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Discuss unto me: art thou officer,<br>Or art thou base, common, and popular? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | I am a gentleman of a company. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Trail’st thou the puissant pike? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Even so. What are you? |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | As good a gentleman as the Emperor. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Then you are a better than the [[King]]. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | The [[King]]’s a bawcock and a heart-of-gold,<br>A lad of life, an imp of fame,<br>Of parents good, of fist most valiant.<br>I kiss his dirty shoe, and from heartstring<br>I [[Love|love]] the lovely bully. What is thy name? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Harry le roi. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Leroi? A Cornish name. Art thou of Cornish crew? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | No, I am a Welshman. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Know’st thou [[Fluellen]]? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Yes. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Tell him I’ll knock his leek about his pate<br>Upon Saint Davy’s day. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Do not you wear your dagger in your cap that day, lest he knock that about yours. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | Art thou his friend? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | And his kinsman too. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | The fico for thee then. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | I thank you. God be with you. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | My name is [[Pistol]] called. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | It sorts [[Well|well]] with your fierceness. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit [[Pistol]] Enter Captains [[Fluellen]] and [[Gower]] severally. [[King]] Harry stands apart |
| [[Gower|GOWER]] | Captain [[Fluellen]]! |
| [[Fluellen|FLUELLEN]] | So! In the name of Jesu [[Christ]], speak fewer. It is the greatest admiration in the universal world, when the true and ancient prerogatifs and laws of the wars is not kept. If you would take the pains but to examine the wars of [[Pompey]] the Great, you shall find, I warrant you, that there is no tiddle-taddle nor pibble-babble in [[Pompey]]’s camp. I warrant you, you shall find the ceremonies of the wars, and the cares of it, and the forms of it, and the sobriety of it, and the modesty of it, to be otherwise. |
| [[Gower|GOWER]] | Why, the enemy is loud. You hear him all [[Night|night]]. |
| [[Fluellen|FLUELLEN]] | If the enemy is an ass and a [[Fool|fool]] and a prating coxcomb, is it meet, think you, that we should also, look you, be an ass and a [[Fool|fool]] and a prating coxcomb? In your own [[Conscience|conscience]] now? |
| [[Gower|GOWER]] | I will speak lower. |
| [[Fluellen|FLUELLEN]] | I pray you and beseech you that you will. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt [[Fluellen]] and [[Gower]] |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Though it appear a little out of fashion,<br>There is much care and valour in this Welshman. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter three soldiers: John Bates, Alexander [[Court]], and [[Michael]] Williams |
| [[Court|COURT]] | Brother John Bates, is not that the morning which breaks yonder? |
| BATES | I think it be. But we have no great cause to [[Desire|desire]] the approach of day. |
| WILLIAM | We see yonder the beginning of the day, but I think we shall never see the end of it.—Who goes there? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | A friend. |
| WILLIAM | Under what captain serve you? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Under Sir Thomas Erpingham. |
| WILLIAM | A good old commander and a most kind gentleman. I pray you, what thinks he of our estate? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Even as men wrecked upon a sand, that look to be washed off the next tide. |
| BATES | He hath not told his thought to the [[King]]? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | No, nor it is not meet he should. For though I speak it to you, I think the [[King]] is but a man, as I am. The violet smells to him as it doth to me; the element shows to him as it doth to me. All his senses have but human conditions. His ceremonies laid by, in his [[Nakedness|nakedness]] he appears but a man, and though his affections are higher mounted than ours, yet when they stoop, they stoop with the like wing. Therefore, when he sees reason of fears, as we do, his fears, out of [[Doubt|doubt]], be of the same relish as ours are. Yet, in reason, no man should possess him with any appearance of [[Fear|fear]], lest he, by showing it, should dishearten his army. |
| BATES | He may show what outward [[Courage|courage]] he will, but I believe, as cold a [[Night|night]] as ’tis, he could wish himself in Thames up to the neck. And so I would he were, and I by him, at all adventures, so we were quit here. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | By my troth, I will speak my [[Conscience|conscience]] of the [[King]]. I think he would not wish himself anywhere but where he is. |
| BATES | Then I would he were here [[Alone|alone]]. So should he be sure to be ransomed, and a many poor men’s lives saved. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | I dare say you [[Love|love]] him not so ill to wish him here [[Alone|alone]], howsoever you speak this to feel other men’s minds. Methinks I could not die anywhere so contented as in the [[King]]’s company, his cause being just and his quarrel honourable. |
| WILLIAMS | That’s more than we know. |
| BATES | Ay, or more than we should seek after. For we know enough if we know we are the [[King]]’s subjects. If his cause be wrong, our obedience to the [[King]] wipes the crime of it out of us. |
| WILLIAMS | But if the cause be not good, the [[King]] himself hath a heavy reckoning to make, when all those legs and arms and heads chopped off in a battle shall join together at the latter day, and cry all, ‘We died at such a ptace’—some swearing, some crying for a surgeon, some upon their wives left poor behind them, some upon the debts they owe, some upon their children rawly left. I am afeard there are few die [[Well|well]] that die in a battle, for how can they charitably dispose of anything, when [[Blood|blood]] is their argument? Now, if these men do not die [[Well|well]], it will be a black matter for the [[King]] that led them to it—who to disobey were against all proportion of subjection. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | So, if a son that is by his father sent about merchandise do sinfully miscarry upon the sea, the imputation of his wickedness, by your rule, should be imposed upon his father, that sent him. Or if a servant, under his master’s command transporting a sum of money, be assailed by robbers, and die in many irreconciled iniquities, you may call the business of the master the author of the servant’s damnation. But this is not so. The [[King]] is not bound to answer the particular endings of his soldiers, the father of his son, nor the master of his servant, for they purpose not their deaths when they propose their services. Besides, there is no [[King|king]], be his cause never so spotless, if it come to the arbitrament of swords, can try it out with all unspotted soldiers. Some, peradventure, have on them the [[Guilt|guilt]] of premeditated and contrived murder; some, of beguiling virgins with the broken seals of perjury; some, making the wars their bulwark, that have before gored the gentle bosom of peace with pillage and robbery. Now, if these men have defeated the law and outrun native punishment, though they can outstrip men, they have no wings to fly from God. War is his beadle. War is his vengeance. So that here men are punished for before-breach of the [[King]]’s laws, in now the [[King]]’s quarrel. Where they feared the [[Death|death]], they have borne life away; and where they would be safe, they perish. Then if they die unprovided, no more is the [[King]] guilty of their damnation than he was before guilty of those impieties for the which they are now visited. Every subject’s duty is the [[King]]’s, but every subject’s [[Soul|soul]] is his own. Therefore should every soldier in the wars do as every sick man in his bed: wash every mote out of his [[Conscience|conscience]]. And dying so, [[Death|death]] is to him advantage; or not dying, the [[Time|time]] was blessedly lost wherein such preparation was gained. And in him that escapes, it were not sin to think that, making God so free an offer, he let him outlive that day to see his [[Greatness|greatness]] and to teach others how they should prepare. |
| *(didascalia)* | BATES ’Tis certain, every man that dies ill, the ill upon his own head. The [[King]] is not to answer it. I do not [[Desire|desire]] he should answer for me, and yet I determine to fight lustily for him. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | I myself heard the [[King]] say he would not be ransomed. |
| WILLIAMS | Ay, he said so, to make us fight cheerfully, but when our throats are cut he may be ransomed, and we ne’er the wiser. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | If I live to see it, I will never trust his word after. |
| WILLIAMS | You pay him then! That’s a perilous shot out of an elder-gun, that a poor and a private displeasure can do against a monarch. You may as [[Well|well]] go about to turn the sun to ice with fanning in his face with a peacock’s feather. You’ll never trust his word after! Come, ’tis a foolish saying. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Your reproof is something too round. I should be angry with you, if the [[Time|time]] were convenient. |
| WILLIAMS | Let it be a quarrel between us, if you live. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | I embrace it. |
| WILLIAM | How shall I know thee again? |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Give me any gage of thine, and I will wear it in my bonnet. Then if ever thou darest acknowledge it, I will make it my quarrel. |
| WILLIAMS | Here’s my glove. Give me another of thine. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | There. They exchange gloves |
| WILLIAM | This will I also wear in my cap. If ever thou come to me and say, after tomorrow, ‘This is my glove’, by this hand I will take thee a box on the ear. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | If ever I live to see it, I will challenge it. |
| WILLIAM | Thou darest as [[Well|well]] be hanged. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | [[Well]], I will do it, though I take thee in the [[King]]’s company. |
| WILLIAMS | Keep thy word. Fare thee [[Well|well]]. |
| BATES | Be friends, you English fools, be friends. We have French quarrels enough, if you could tell how to reckon. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Indeed, the French may lay twenty French crowns to one they will beat us, for they bear them on their shoulders. But it is no English treason to cut French crowns, and tomorrow the [[King]] himself will be a clipper. Exeunt soldiers Upon the [[King]]. ‘Let us our lives, our souls, our debts, our care-full wives, Our children, and our sins, lay on the [[King]].’ We must bear all. O hard condition, Twin-born with [[Greatness|greatness]]: subject to the breath Of every [[Fool|fool]], whose sense no more can feel But his own wringing. What infinite heartsease Must kings neglect that private men enjoy? And what have kings that privates have not too, Save ceremony, save general ceremony? And what art thou, thou idol ceremony? What kind of god art thou, that suffer‘st more Of mortal griefs than do thy worshippers? What are thy rents? What are thy comings-in? O ceremony, show me but thy worth. What is thy [[Soul|soul]] of adoration? Art thou aught else but place, degree, and form, Creating awe and [[Fear|fear]] in other men? Wherein thou art less happy, being feared, Than they in fearing. What drink’st thou oft, instead of homage sweet, But poisoned flattery? O be sick, great [[Greatness|greatness]], And bid thy ceremony give thee cure. Think‘st thou the fiery fever will go out With titles blown from adulation? Will it give place to flexure and low bending? Canst thou, when thou command’st the beggar’s knee, Command the health of it? No, thou proud dream That play‘st so subtly with a [[King|king]]’s repose; I am a [[King|king]] that find thee, and I know ’Tis not the balm, the sceptre, and the ball, The [[Sword|sword]], the mace, the [[Crown|crown]] imperial, The intertissued robe of gold and pearl, The farced title running fore the [[King|king]], The throne he sits on, nor the tide of pomp That beats upon the high shore of this world—No, not all these, thrice-gorgeous ceremony, Not all these, laid in bed majestical, Can [[Sleep|sleep]] so soundly as the wretched slave Who with a [[Body|body]] filled and vacant mind Gets him to rest, crammed with distressful bread; Never sees horrid [[Night|night]], the [[Child|child]] of hell, But like a lackey from the rise to set Sweats in the eye of Phoebus, and all [[Night|night]] Sleeps in Elysium; next day, after [[Dawn|dawn]] Doth rise and help [[Hyperion]] to his [[Horse|horse]], And follows so the ever-running year With profitable labour to his [[Grave|grave]]. And but for ceremony such a wretch, Winding up days with toil and nights with [[Sleep|sleep]], Had the forehand and vantage of a [[King|king]]. The slave, a member of the country’s peace, Enjoys it, but in gross brain little wots What watch the [[King]] keeps to maintain the peace, Whose hours the peasant best advantages. Enter Sir Thomas Erpingham |
| ERPINGHAM | My lord, your nobles, jealous of your absence,<br>Seek through your camp to find you. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | Good old knight,<br>Collect them all together at my tent.<br>I’ll be before thee. |
| ERPINGHAM | I shall do’t, my lord. Exit |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | O God of battles, steel my soldiers’ hearts.<br>Possess them not with [[Fear|fear]]. Take from them now<br>The sense of reck‘ning, ere th’opposèd numbers<br>Pluck their hearts from them. Not today, O Lord,<br>O not today, think not upon the fault<br>My father made in compassing the [[Crown|crown]].<br>I [[Richard]]’s [[Body|body]] have interred new,<br>And on it have bestowed more contrite [[Tears|tears]]<br>Than from it issued forced drops of [[Blood|blood]].<br>Five hundred poor have I in yearly pay<br>Who twice a day their withered hands hold up<br>Toward heaven to pardon [[Blood|blood]]. And I have built<br>Two chantries, where the sad and solemn priests<br>Sing still for [[Richard]]’s [[Soul|soul]]. More will I do,<br>Though all that I can do is nothing worth,<br>Since that my penitence comes after ill,<br>Imploring pardon. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter the [[Duke of Gloucester]] |
| [[Gloucester|GLOUCESTER]] | My liege. |
| [[King|KING]] HARRY | My brother [[Gloucester]]’s voice? Ay.<br>I know thy errand, I will go with thee.<br>The day, my friends, and all things stay for me. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt |
