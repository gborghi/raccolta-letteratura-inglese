When your head did but ache<br>I knit my handkerchief about your brows,<br>The best I had—a princess wrought it me,<br>And I did never ask it you again—<br>And with my hand at midnight held your head,<br>And like the watchful minutes to the hour<br>Still and anon cheered up the heavy time,<br>Saying ‘What lack you?’ and ‘Where lies your grief?’<br>Or ‘What good love may I perform for you?’<br>Many a poor man’s son would have lain still<br>And ne’er have spoke a loving word to you,<br>But you at your sick service had a prince.<br>Nay, you may think my love was crafty love,<br>And call it cunning. | ARTHUR | Alas, I then have chid away my friend!<br>He hath a stern look, but a gentle heart.<br>Let him come back, that his compassion may<br>Give life to yours. |
| HUBERT | Come, boy, prepare yourself. |

| ARTHUR | O God, that there were but a mote in yours,<br>A grain, a dust, a gnat, a wandering hair,<br>Any annoyance in that precious sense,<br>Then, feeling what small things are boisterous there,<br>Your vile intent must needs seem horrible. 95 |
| HUBERT | Is this your promise? Go to, hold your tongue! |
| ARTHUR | Hubert, the utterance of a brace of tongues<br>Must needs want pleading for a pair of eyes.<br>Let me not hold my tongue, let me not, Hubert;<br>Or, Hubert, if you will, cut out my tongue, 100<br>So I may keep mine eyes. | HUBERT | I can heat it, boy. |
| ARTHUR | No, in good sooth: the fire is dead with grief, 105<br>Being create for comfort, to be used<br>In undeserved extremes. See else yourself.<br>There is no malice in this burning coal;<br>The breath of heaven hath blown his spirit out,<br>And strewed repentant ashes on his head. II0 |
| HUBERT | But with my breath I can revive it, boy. |
| ARTHUR | An if you do, you will but make it blush<br>And glow with shame of your proceedings, Hubert.<br>Nay, it perchance will sparkle in your eyes,<br>And like a dog that is compelled to fight,<br>Snatch at his master that doth tarre him on.<br>All things that you should use to do me wrong<br>Deny their office; only you do lack<br>That mercy which fierce fire and iron extends,<br>Creatures of note for mercy-lacking uses. |
| HUBERT | Well, see to live. I will not touch thine eye<br>For all the treasure that thine uncle owes.<br>Yet am I sworn, and I did purpose, boy,<br>With this same very iron to burn them out. |

| ARTHUR | O God! I thank you, Hubert. |

| *(didascalia)* | 4.2 flourish. Enter King John, the Earls of Pembroke and Salisbury, and other lords. King John ascends the throne |
| KING JOHN | Here once again we sit, once again crowned,<br>And looked upon, I hope, with cheerful eyes. |
| PEMBROKE | This ‘once again’, but that your highness pleased,<br>Was once superfluous. You were crowned before,<br>And that high royalty was ne‘er plucked off,<br>The faiths of men ne’er stained with revolt;<br>Fresh expectation troubled not the land<br>With any longed-for change or better state. |
| SALISBURY | Therefore to be possessed with double pomp,<br>To guard a title that was rich before, 10<br>To gild refined gold, to paint the lily,<br>To throw a perfume on the violet,<br>To smooth the ice, or add another hue<br>Unto the rainbow, or with taper-light<br>To seek the beauteous eye of heaven to garnish,<br>Is wasteful and ridiculous excess. |
| PEMBROKE | But that your royal pleasure must be done,<br>This act is as an ancient tale new-told,<br>And in the last repeating troublesome,<br>Being urged at a time unseasonable. |
| SALISBURY | In this the antique and well-noted face<br>Of plain old form is much disfigured,<br>And like a shifted wind unto a sail,<br>It makes the course of thoughts to fetch about,<br>Startles and frights consideration,<br>Makes sound opinion sick, and truth suspected<br>For putting on so new a fashioned robe. |
| PEMBROKE | When workmen strive to do better than well,<br>They do confound their skill in covetousness;<br>And oftentimes excusing of a fault<br>Doth make the fault the worser by th’excuse;<br>As patches set upon a little breach<br>Discredit more in hiding of the fault<br>Than did the fault before it was so patched. |
| SALISBURY | To this effect: before you were new-crowned<br>We breathed our counsel, but it pleased your<br>highness<br>To overbear it; and we are all well pleased,<br>Since all and every part of what we would<br>Doth make a stand at what your highness will. |
| KING JOHN | Some reasons of this double coronation<br>I have possessed you with, and think them strong.<br>And more, more strong, when lesser is my fear<br>I shall endue you with. | PEMBROKE | Then I, as one that am the tongue of these<br>To sound the purposes of all their hearts,<br>Both for myself and them, but chief of all<br>Your safety, for the which myself and them<br>Bend their best studies, heartily request<br>Th’enfranchisement of Arthur, whose restraint<br>Doth move the murmuring lips of discontent<br>To break into this dangerous argument:<br>If what in rest you have, in right you hold, 55<br>Why then your fears—which, as they say, attend<br>The steps of wrong—should move you to mew up<br>Your tender kinsman, and to choke his days<br>With barbarous ignorance, and deny his youth<br>The rich advantage of good exercise?<br>That the time’s enemies may not have this<br>To grace occasions, let it be our suit<br>That you have bid us ask, his liberty;<br>Which for our goods we do no further ask<br>Than whereupon our weal, on you depending, 65<br>Counts it your weal he have his liberty. |
