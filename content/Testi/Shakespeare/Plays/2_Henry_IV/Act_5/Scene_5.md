| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | God bless thy lungs, good knight. |
| SIR JOHN | Come here, [[Pistol]]; stand behind me. (To Shallow) O, if I had had [[Time|time]] to have made new liveries, I would have bestowed the thousand pound I borrowed of you! But ’tis no matter; this poor show doth better; this doth infer the zeal I had to see him. |
| *(didascalia)* | SHALLOW It doth so. |
| SIR JOHN | It shows my earnestness of affection— |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | It doth so. |
| SIR JOHN | My devotion— |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | It doth, it doth, it doth. |
| SIR JOHN | As it were, to ride day and [[Night|night]], and not to deliberate, not to remember, not to have patience to shift me— |
| SHALLOW | It is most certain. |
| *(didascalia)* | SIR JOHN But to stand stained with travel and sweating with [[Desire|desire]] to see him, thinking of nothing else, putting all affairs in oblivion, as if there were nothing else to be done but to see him. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | ’Tis semper idem, for absque hoc nihil est: ’tis all in every part. |
| SHALLOW | ’Tis so indeed. |
| [[Pistol|PISTOL]] | My knight, I will inflame thy noble liver,<br>And make thee rage.<br>Thy Doll, and [[Helen]] of thy noble thoughts,<br>Is in base durance and contagious [[Prison|prison]],<br>Haled thither<br>By most mechanical and dirty hand.<br>Rouse up [[Revenge]] from ebon den with fell Alecto’s<br>snake,<br>For Doll is in. [[Pistol]] speaks naught but [[Truth|truth]]. |

Fall to thy prayers.<br>How ill white hairs becomes a [[Fool|fool]] and jester!<br>I have long dreamt of such a kind of man,<br>So surfeit-swelled, so old, and so profane;<br>But being awake, I do despise my dream.<br>Make less thy [[Body|body]] hence, and more thy grace.<br>Leave gormandizing; know the [[Grave|grave]] doth gape<br>For thee thrice wider than for other men.<br>Reply not to me with a [[Fool|fool]]-born jest.<br>Presume not that I am the thing I was,<br>For God doth know, so shall the world perceive,<br>That I have turned away my former [[Self|self]];<br>So will I those that kept me company.<br>When thou dost hear I am as I have been,<br>Approach me, and thou shalt be as thou wast,<br>The tutor and the feeder of my riots.<br>Till then I banish thee, on [[Pain|pain]] of [[Death|death]],<br>As I have done the rest of my misleaders,<br>Not to come near our person by ten mile.<br>For competence of life I will allow you,<br>That lack of means enforce you not to evils;<br>And as we hear you do [[Reform|reform]] yourselves,<br>We will, according to your strengths and qualities,<br>Give you advancement. | *(didascalia)* | Enter Epilogue |
| EPILOGUE | First my [[Fear|fear]], then my curtsy, last my speech.<br>My [[Fear|fear]] is your displeasure; my curtsy, my duty; and my speech to beg your pardons. If you look for a good speech now, you undo me; for what I have to say is of mine own making, and what indeed I should say will, I [[Doubt|doubt]], prove mine own marring. But to the purpose, and so to the venture. Be it known to you, as it is very [[Well|well]], I was lately here in the end of a displeasing play, to pray your patience for it, and to promise you a better. I did mean indeed to pay you with this; which, if like an ill venture it come unluckily [[Home|home]], I break, and you, my gentle creditors, lose. Here I promised you I would be, and here I commit my [[Body|body]] to your mercies. Bate me some, and I will pay you some, and, as most debtors do, promise you infinitely.<br>If my tongue cannot entreat you to acquit me, will you command me to use my legs? And yet that were but light payment, to [[Dance|dance]] out of your debt. But a good [[Conscience|conscience]] will make any possible satisfaction, and so would I. All the gentlewomen here have forgiven me; if the gentlemen will not, then the gentlemen do not agree with the gentlewomen, which was never seen before in such an assembly.<br>One word more, I beseech you. If you be not too much cloyed with fat meat, our humble author will continue the story with Sir John in it, and make you merry with fair Catherine of France; where, for anything I know, [[Falstaff]] shall die of a sweat—unless already a be killed with your hard opinions. For Oldcastle died a martyr, and this is not the man. My tongue is weary; when my legs are too, I will bid you good [[Night|night]], and so kneel down before you—but, indeed, to pray for the [[Queen]]. |
| *(didascalia)* | He dances, then kneels for applause. Exit |

| MUCH ADO ABOUT NOTHING | Much Ado About Nothing is not mentioned in the list of plays by Shakespeare given by Francis Meres in his Palladis Tamia, published in the [[Autumn|autumn]] of 1598. Certain speech-prefixes of the first edition, published in 1600, suggest that as Shakespeare wrote he had in mind for the role of [[Dogberry]] the comic actor Will Kemp, who is believed to have left the Lord Chamberlain’s Men during 1599. Probably Shakespeare wrote the play between [[Summer|summer]] 1598 and [[Spring|spring]] 1599.<br>The action is set in Sicily, where [[Don Pedro]], Prince of Aragon, has recently defeated his half-brother, the bastard [[Don John]], in a military engagement. Apparently reconciled, they return to the capital, Messina, as guests of the Governor, [[Leonato]]. There Count [[Claudio]], a young nobleman serving in [[Don Pedro]]’s army, falls in [[Love|love]] with [[Hero]], [[Leonato]]’s daughter, whom [[Don Pedro]] woos on his behalf. The play’s central plot, written mainly in verse, shows how [[Don John]] maliciously deceives [[Claudio]] into believing that [[Hero]] has taken a [[Lover|lover]] on the eve of her [[Marriage|marriage]], causing [[Claudio]] to repudiate her publicly, at the altar. This is a variation on an old tale that existed in many versions; it had been told in Italian verse by Ariosto, in his [[Orlando]] Furioso (1516, translated into English verse by Sir John Harington, 1591), in Italian prose by Matteo Bandello in his Novelle (1554, adapted into French by P. de Belleforest, 1569), in English prose by George Whetstone (The Rock of Regard, 1576), in English verse by [[Edmund]] Spenser (The Faerie Queene, Book 2, canto 4, 1590), and in a number of plays including Luigi Pasqualigo’s II Fedele (1579), adapted into English—perhaps by Anthony Munday—as Fedele and Fortunio (published in 1583). Shakespeare, whose plot is an independent reworking of the traditional story, seems to owe most to Ariosto and Bandello, perhaps indirectly.<br>[[Don John]]’s [[Deception|deception]], with its tragicomical resolution, is offset by a parallel plot written mainly in prose, portraying another, more light-hearted [[Deception|deception]], by which [[Hero]]’s cousin, [[Beatrice]], and [[Benedick]]—friend of [[Don Pedro]] and [[Claudio]]—are tricked into acknowledging, first to themselves and then to each other, that they are in [[Love|love]]. This part of the play seems to be of Shakespeare’s invention: the juxtaposition of this clever, sophisticated, apparently unillusioned pair with the more naive [[Claudio]] and [[Hero]] recalls Shakespeare’s earlier contrast of romantic and antiromantic attitudes to [[Love|love]] and [[Marriage|marriage]] in The Taming of the Shrew. The play’s third main strand is provided by Constable [[Dogberry]], his partner Verges, and the Watchmen, clearly English rather than Sicilian in origin. Although [[Benedick]] and [[Beatrice]] are, technically, subordinate characters, they have dominated the [[Imagination|imagination]] of both readers and playgoers. |
