| *(didascalia)* | Cornetts. Enter Coriolanus, Menenius, all the gentry; Cominius, Lartius, and other Senators |
| CORIOLANUS | Tullus Aufidius then had made new head? |
| LARTIUS | He had, my lord, and that it was which caused<br>Our swifter composition. |
| CORIOLANUS | So then the Volsces stand but as at first,<br>Ready when time shall prompt them to make raid<br>Upon’s again. |
| COMINIUS | They are worn, lord consul, so<br>That we shall hardly in our ages see<br>Their banners wave again. |
| CORIOLANUS | (to Lartius) Saw you Aufidius? |
| LARTIUS | On safeguard he came to me, and did curse<br>Against the Volsces for they had so vilely<br>Yielded the town. He is retired to Antium. |
| CORIOLANUS | Spoke he of me? |
| LARTIUS | He did, my lord. |
| CORIOLANUS | How? What? |
| LARTIUS | How often he had met you sword to sword;<br>That of all things upon the earth he hated<br>Your person most; that he would pawn his fortunes<br>To hopeless restitution, so he might<br>Be called your vanquisher. |
| CORIOLANUS | At Antium lives he? |
| LARTIUS | At Antium. |
| CORIOLANUS | I wish I had a cause to seek him there,<br>To oppose his hatred fully. Welcome home. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Sicinius and Brutus |
| CORIOLANUS | Behold, these are the tribunes of the people,<br>The tongues o’th’ common mouth. I do despise them,<br>For they do prank them in authority<br>Against all noble sufferance. |
| SICINIUS | Pass no further. |
| CORIOLANUS | Ha, what is that? |
| BRUTUS | It will be dangerous to go on. No further. |
| CORIOLANUS | What makes this change? |
| MENENIUS | The matter? |
| COMINIUS | Hath he not passed the noble and the common? |
| BRUTUS | Cominius, no. |
| CORIOLANUS | Have I had children’s voices? |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR |
| CORIOLANUS | Tribunes, give way. He shall to th’ market-place. |
| BRUTUS | The people are incensed against him. |
| SICINIUS | Stop,<br>Or all will fall in broil. |
| CORIOLANUS | Are these your herd?<br>Must these have voices, that can yield them now<br>And straight disclaim their tongues? What are your<br>offices?<br>You being their mouths, why rule you not their<br>teeth?<br>Have you not set them on? |
| MENENIUS | Be calm, be calm. |
| CORIOLANUS | It is a purposed thing, and grows by plot<br>To curb the will of the nobility.<br>Suffer’t, and live with such as cannot rule<br>Nor ever will be ruled. |
| BRUTUS | Call’t not a plot.<br>The people cry you mocked them, and of late<br>When corn was given them gratis, you repined,<br>Scandalled the suppliants for the people, called them<br>Time-pleasers, flatterers, foes to nobleness. |
| CORIOLANUS | Why, this was known before. |
| BRUTUS | Not to them all. |
| CORIOLANUS | Have you informed them sithence? |
| BRUTUS | How, I inform them? |
| *(didascalia)* | CORIOLANUS |
| BRUTUS | You are like to do such business. |
| BRUTUS | Not unlike<br>Each way to better yours. |
| CORIOLANUS | Why then should I be consul? By yon clouds,<br>Let me deserve so ill as you, and make me<br>Your fellow tribune. |
| SICINIUS | You show too much of that<br>For which the people stir. If you will pass<br>To where you are bound, you must enquire your way,<br>Which you are out of, with a gentler spirit,<br>Or never be so noble as a consul,<br>Nor yoke with him for tribune. |
| MENENIUS | Let’s be calm. |
| COMINIUS | The people are abused, set on. This palt‘ring<br>Becomes not Rome, nor has Coriolanus<br>Deserved this so dishonoured rub, laid falsely<br>I’th’ plain way of his merit. |
| CORIOLANUS | Tell me of corn?<br>This was my speech, and I will speak’t again. |
| MENENIUS | Not now, not now. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR Not in this heat, sir, now. |
| CORIOLANUS | Now as I live,<br>I will. My nobler friends, I crave their pardons.<br>For the mutable rank-scented meinie,<br>Let them regard me, as I do not flatter,<br>And therein behold themselves. I say again,<br>In soothing them we nourish ’gainst our Senate<br>The cockle of rebellion, insolence, sedition,<br>Which we ourselves have ploughed for, sowed, and<br>scattered<br>By mingling them with us, the honoured number<br>Who lack not virtue, no, nor power, but that<br>Which they have given to beggars. |
| MENENIUS | Well, no more. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR |
| MENENIUS | No more words, we beseech you. |
| CORIOLANUS | How, no more?<br>As for my country I have shed my blood,<br>Not fearing outward force, so shall my lungs<br>Coin words till their decay against those measles<br>Which we disdain should tetter us, yet sought<br>The very way to catch them. |
| BRUTUS | You speak o’th’ people as if you were a god<br>To punish, not a man of their infirmity. |
| SICINIUS | ’Twere well we let the people know’t. |
| MENENIUS | What, what, his choler? |
| CORIOLANUS | Choler? Were I as patient as the midnight sleep,<br>By Jove, ’twould be my mind. |
| SICINIUS | It is a mind<br>That shall remain a poison where it is,<br>Not poison any further. |
| CORIOLANUS | ‘Shall remain’?<br>Hear you this Triton of the minnows? Mark you<br>His absolute ‘shall’? |
| COMINIUS | ’Twas from the canon. |
| CORIOLANUS | ‘Shall’?<br>O good but most unwise patricians, why,<br>You grave but reckless senators, have you thus<br>Given Hydra here to choose an officer<br>That, with his peremptory ‘shall’, being but<br>The horn and noise o‘th’ monster’s, wants not spirit<br>To say he’ll turn your current in a ditch<br>And make your channel his? If he have power,<br>Then vail your impotence; if none, awake<br>Your dangerous lenity. If you are learned,<br>Be not as common fools; if you are not,<br>Let them have cushions by you. You are plebeians<br>If they be senators, and they are no less<br>When, both your voices blended, the great‘st taste<br>Most palates theirs. They choose their magistrate,<br>And such a one as he, who puts his ‘shall’,<br>His popular ‘shall’, against a graver bench<br>Than ever frowned in Greece. By Jove himself, no<br>It makes the consuls base, and my soul aches<br>To know, when two authorities are up,<br>Neither supreme, how soon confusion<br>May enter ’twixt the gap of both and take<br>The one by th’ other. |
| COMINIUS | Well, on to th’ market-place. |
| CORIOLANUS | Whoever gave that counsel to give forth<br>The corn o‘th’ storehouse gratis, as ’twas used<br>Sometime in Greece— |
| MENENIUS | Well, well, no more of that. |
| CORIOLANUS | Though there the people had more absolute power—<br>I say they nourished disobedience, fed<br>The ruin of the state. |
| BRUTUS | Why shall the people give<br>One that speaks thus their voice? |
| CORIOLANUS | I’ll give my reasons,<br>More worthier than their voices. They know the corn<br>Was not our recompense, resting well assured<br>They ne‘er did service for’t. Being pressed to th’ war,<br>Even when the navel of the state was touched,<br>They would not thread the gates. This kind of service<br>Did not deserve corn gratis. Being i’th’ war,<br>Their mutinies and revolts, wherein they showed<br>Most valour, spoke not for them. Th‘accusation<br>Which they have often made against the senate,<br>All cause unborn, could never be the native<br>Of our so frank donation. Well, what then?<br>How shall this bosom multiplied digest<br>The senate’s courtesy? Let deeds express<br>What’s like to be their words: ‘We did request it,<br>We are the greater poll, and in true fear<br>They gave us our demands.’ Thus we debase<br>The nature of our seats, and make the rabble<br>Call our cares fears, which will in time<br>Break ope the locks o’th’ senate and bring in<br>The crows to peck the eagles. |
| MENENIUS | Come, enough. |
| BRUTUS | Enough with over-measure. |
| CORIOLANUS | No, take more.<br>What may be sworn by, both divine and human,<br>Seal what I end withal! This double worship,<br>Where one part does disdain with cause, the other<br>Insult without all reason, where gentry, title, wisdom<br>Cannot conclude but by the yea and no<br>Of general ignorance, it must omit<br>Real necessities, and give way the while<br>To unstable slightness. Purpose so barred, it follows<br>Nothing is done to purpose. Therefore beseech you—<br>You that will be less fearful than discreet,<br>That love the fundamental part of state<br>More than you doubt the change on‘t, that prefer<br>A noble life before a long, and wish<br>To jump a body with a dangerous physic<br>That’s sure of death without it—at once pluck out<br>The multitudinous tongue; let them not lick<br>The sweet which is their poison. Your dishonour<br>Mangles true judgement, and bereaves the state<br>Of that integrity which should become’t,<br>Not having the power to do the good it would<br>For th’ill which doth control’t. |
| BRUTUS | He’s said enough. |
| SICINIUS | He’s spoken like a traitor, and shall answer<br>As traitors do. |
| CORIOLANUS | Thou wretch, despite o’erwhelm thee!<br>What should the people do with these bald tribunes,<br>On whom depending, their obedience fails<br>To th’ greater bench? In a rebellion,<br>When what’s not meet but what must be was law,<br>Then were they chosen. In a better hour<br>Let what is meet be said it must be meet,<br>And throw their power i’th’ dust. |
| BRUTUS | Manifest treason. |
| SICINIUS | This a consul? No. |
| BRUTUS | The aediles, hot |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter an Aedile |
| BRUTUS | Let him be apprehended. |
| SICINIUS | Go call the people, |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit Aedile |
| SICINIUS | (To Coriolanus) in whose name myself<br>Attach thee as a traitorous innovator,<br>A foe to th’ public weal. Obey, I charge thee,<br>And follow to thine answer. |
| CORIOLANUS | Hence, old goat! |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE PATRICIANS |
| CORIOLANUS | We’ll surety him. |
| COMINIUS | (to Sicinius) Aged sir, hands off. |
| CORIOLANUS | (to Sicinius)<br>Hence, rotten thing, or I shall shake thy bones<br>Out of thy garments. |
| SICINIUS | Help, ye citizens! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter a rabble of Plebeians, with the Aediles |
| MENENIUS | On both sides more respect. |
| SICINIUS | Here’s he<br>That would take from you all your power. |
| BRUTUS | Seize him, aediles. |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS |
| BRUTUS | Down with him, down with him! |
| SECOND SENATOR | Weapons, weapons, weapons!<br>They all bustle about Coriolanus |
| *(didascalia)* | CITIZENS and PATRICIANS in dispersed cries |
| SECOND SENATOR | Tribunes! Patricians! Citizens! What ho!<br>Siciniusl Brutus! Coriolanusl Citizens! |
| *(didascalia)* | SOME CITIZENS and PATRICIANS |
| SECOND SENATOR | Peace, peace, peace! Stay! Hold! Peace! |
| MENENIUS | What is about to be? I am out of breath.<br>Confusion’s near; I cannot speak. You tribunes<br>To th’ people, Coriolanus, patience!<br>Speak, good Sicinius. |
| SICINIUS | Hear me, people, peace. |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS |
| SICINIUS | Let’s hear our tribune! Peace! Speak, speak, speak! |
| SICINIUS | You are at point to lose your liberties.<br>Martius would have all from you—Martius<br>Whom late you have named for consul. |
| MENENIUS | Fie, fie, fie,<br>This is the way to kindle, not to quench. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR |
| MENENIUS | To unbuild the city, and to lay all flat. |
| SICINIUS | What is the city but the people? |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS |
| SICINIUS | True,<br>The people are the city. |
| BRUTUS | By the consent of all<br>We were established the people’s magistrates. |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS |
| BRUTUS | You so remain. |
| MENENIUS | And so are like to do. |
| *(didascalia)* | CORIOLANUS |
| MENENIUS | That is the way to lay the city flat,<br>To bring the roof to the foundation,<br>And bury all which yet distinctly ranges<br>In heaps and piles of ruin. |
| SICINIUS | This deserves death. |
| BRUTUS | Or let us stand to our authority,<br>Or let us lose it. We do here pronounce,<br>Upon the part o’th’ people in whose power<br>We were elected theirs, Martius is worthy<br>Of present death. |
| SICINIUS | Therefore lay hold of him,<br>Bear him to th’ rock Tarpeian; and from thence<br>Into destruction cast him. |
| BRUTUS | Aediles, seize him. |
| ALL THE CITIZENS | Yield, Martius, yield. |
| MENENIUS | Hear me one word.<br>Beseech you, tribunes, hear me but a word. |
| AEDILES | Peace, peace! |
| MENENIUS | (to the tribunes)<br>Be that you seem, truly your country’s friend,<br>And temp’rately proceed to what you would<br>Thus violently redress. |
| BRUTUS | Sir, those cold ways<br>That seem like prudent helps are very poisons<br>Where the disease is violent. Lay hands upon him,<br>And bear him to the rock.<br>Coriolanus draws his sword |
| CORIOLANUS | No, I’ll die here.<br>There’s some among you have beheld me fighting.<br>Come, try upon yourselves what you have seen me. |
| MENENIUS | Down with that sword. Tribunes, withdraw a while. |
| BRUTUS | Lay hands upon him. |
| MENENIUS | Help Martius, help!<br>You that be noble, help him, young and old. |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS Down with him, down with him! |
| MENENIUS | In this mutiny the tribunes, the Aediles, and the people are beat in |
| MENENIUS | (to Coriolanus)<br>Go get you to your house. Be gone, away!<br>All will be naught else. |
| *(didascalia)* | SECOND SENATOR (to Coriolanus) Get you gone. CORIOLANUS |
| MENENIUS | Stand fast; we have as many friends as enemies. |
| MENENIUS | Shall it be put to that? |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR The gods forbid! |
| MENENIUS | (To Coriolanus) I prithee, noble friend, home to thy house.<br>Leave us to cure this cause. |
| MENENIUS | For ’tis a sore upon us<br>You cannot tent yourself. Be gone, beseech you. |
| *(didascalia)* | COMINIUS Come, sir, along with us. |
| *(didascalia)* | CORIOLANUS |
| MENENIUS | I would they were barbarians, as they are,<br>Though in Rome littered; not Romans, as they are<br>not,<br>Though calved i‘th’ porch o’th’ Capitol. |
| *(didascalia)* | MENENIUS Be gone. |
| MENENIUS | Put not your worthy rage into your tongue.<br>One time will owe another. |
| CORIOLANUS | On fair ground<br>I could beat forty of them. |
| MENENIUS | I could myself<br>Take up a brace o’th’ best of them, yea, the two<br>tribunes. |
| COMINIUS | But now ‘tis odds beyond arithmetic,<br>And manhood is called foolery when it stands<br>Against a falling fabric.<br>(To Coriolanus) Will you hence<br>Before the tag return, whose rage doth rend<br>Like interrupted waters, and o’erbear<br>What they are used to bear? |
| MENENIUS | (to Coriolanus) Pray you be gone.<br>I’ll try whether my old wit be in request<br>With those that have but little. This must be patched<br>With cloth of any colour. |
| COMINIUS | Nay, come away. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Coriolanus and Cominius |
| COMINIUS | A PATRICIAN This man has marred his fortune. |
| MENENIUS | His nature is too noble for the world.<br>He would not flatter Neptune for his trident<br>Or Jove for’s power to thunder. His heart’s his mouth.<br>What his breast forges, that his tongue must vent,<br>And, being angry, does forget that ever<br>He heard the name of death. |
| *(didascalia)* | A noise within |
| MENENIUS | Here’s goodly work.<br>A PATRICIAN<br>I would they were abed. |
| MENENIUS | I would they were in Tiber.<br>What the vengeance, could he not speak ’em fair? |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Brutus and Sicinius, with the rabble again |
| SICINIUS | Where is this viper<br>That would depopulate the city and<br>Be every man himself? |
| MENENIUS | You worthy tribunes— |
| SICINIUS | He shall be thrown down the Tarpeian rock<br>With rigorous hands. He hath resisted law,<br>And therefore law shall scorn him further trial<br>Than the severity of the public power,<br>Which he so sets at naught. |
| FIRST CITIZEN | He shall well know<br>The noble tribunes are the people’s mouths,<br>And we their hands. |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS |
| FIRST CITIZEN | He shall, sure on’t. |
| MENENIUS | Sir, sir. |
| SICINIUS | Peace! |
| MENENIUS | Do not cry havoc where you should but hunt<br>With modest warrant. |
| SICINIUS | Sir, how comes’t that you<br>Have holp to make this rescue? |
| MENENIUS | Hear me speak.<br>As I do know the consul’s worthiness,<br>So can I name his faults. |
| SICINIUS | Consul? What consul? |
| MENENIUS | The consul Coriolanus. |
| BRUTUS | He consul? |
| *(didascalia)* | ALL THE CITIZENS No, no, no, no, no! |
| MENENIUS | If, by the tribunes’ leave and yours, good people,<br>I may be heard, I would crave a word or two,<br>The which shall turn you to no further harm<br>Than so much loss of time. |
| SICINIUS | Speak briefly, then,<br>For we are peremptory to dispatch<br>This viperous traitor. To eject him hence<br>Were but our danger, and to keep him here<br>Our certain death. Therefore it is decreed<br>He dies tonight. |
| MENENIUS | Now the good gods forbid<br>That our renowned Rome, whose gratitude<br>Towards her deserved children is enrolled<br>In Jove’s own book, like an unnatural dam<br>Should now eat up her own! |
| SICINIUS | He’s a disease that must be cut away. |
| MENENIUS | O, he’s a limb that has but a disease—<br>Mortal to cut it off, to cure it easy.<br>What has he done to Rome that’s worthy death?<br>Killing our enemies, the blood he hath lost—<br>Which I dare vouch is more than that he hath<br>By many an ounce—he dropped it for his country;<br>And what is left, to lose it by his country<br>Were to us all that do’t and suffer it<br>A brand to th’ end o’th’ world. |
| SICINIUS | This is clean cam. |
| BRUTUS | Merely awry. When he did love his country<br>It honoured him. |
| *(didascalia)* | SICINIUS S |
| BRUTUS | The service of the foot,<br>Being once gangrened, is not then respected<br>For what before it was. |
| BRUTUS | We’ll hear no more.<br>Pursue him to his house and pluck him thence,<br>Lest his infection, being of catching nature,<br>Spread further. |
| MENENIUS | One word more, one word!<br>This tiger-footed rage, when it shall find<br>The harm of unscanned swiftness, will too late<br>Tie leaden pounds to’s heels. Proceed by process,<br>Lest parties—as he is beloved—break out<br>And sack great Rome with Romans. |
| BRUTUS | If it were so? |
| SICINIUS | (to Menenius) What do ye talk?<br>Have we not had a taste of his obedience:<br>Our aediles smote, ourselves resisted? Come. |
| MENENIUS | Consider this: he has been bred i’th’ wars<br>Since a could draw a sword, and is ill-schooled<br>In bolted language. Meal and bran together<br>He throws without distinction. Give me leave,<br>I’ll go to him and undertake to bring him<br>Where he shall answer by a lawful form,<br>In peace, to his utmost peril. |
| FIRST SENATOR | Noble tribunes,<br>It is the humane way. The other course<br>Will prove too bloody, and the end of it<br>Unknown to the beginning. |
| SICINIUS | Noble Menenius,<br>Be you then as the people’s officer.<br>(To the Citizens) Masters, lay down your weapons. |
| BRUTUS | Go not home. |
| SICINIUS | Meet on the market-place. (To Menenius) We’ll attend<br>you there,<br>Where if you bring not Martius, we’ll proceed<br>In our first way. |
| MENENIUS | I’ll bring him to you.<br>(To the Senators) Let me desire your company. He must<br>come,<br>Or what is worst will follow. |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST SENATOR |
| MENENIUS | Pray you, let’s to him. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt tribunes and Citizens at one door, |
| *(didascalia)* | Patricians at another door |
