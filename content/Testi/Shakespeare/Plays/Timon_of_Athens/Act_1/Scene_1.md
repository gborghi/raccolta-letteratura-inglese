| *(didascalia)* | Enter Poet at one door, Painter carrying a picture [at another door], [followed by] Jeweller, Merchant, and Mercer, at several doors |
| POET | Good day, sir. |
| PAINTER | I am glad you’re well. |
| POET | I have not seen you long. How goes the world? |
| PAINTER | It wears, sir, as it grows. |
| POET | Ay, that’s well known.<br>But what particular rarity, what strange,<br>Which manifold record not matches?—See,<br>Magic of bounty, all these spirits thy power<br>Hath conjured to attend. |
| *(didascalia)* | Merchant and Jeweller meet. Mercer passes over |
| *(didascalia)* | the stage, and exits |
| POET | I know the merchant. |
| PAINTER | I know them both. Th’other’s a jeweller. |
| MERCHANT | (to Jeweller)<br>O, ’tis a worthy lord! |
| JEWELLER | Nay, that’s most fixed. |
| MERCHANT | A most incomparable man, breathed, as it were,<br>To an untirable and continuate goodness.<br>He passes. |
| JEWELLER | (showing a jewel) I have a jewel here. |
| MERCHANT | O, pray, let’s see’t. For the Lord Timon, sir? |
| JEWELLER | If he will touch the estimate. But for that— |
| POET | (to himself)<br>‘When we for recompense have praised the vile,<br>It stains the glory in that happy verse<br>Which aptly sings the good.’ |
| MERCHANT | (to Jeweller) ’Tis a good form. |
| JEWELLER | And rich. Here is a water, look ye. |
| PAINTER | (to Poet)<br>You are rapt, sir, in some work, some dedication<br>To the great lord. |
| POET | A thing slipped idly from me.<br>Our poesy is as a gum which oozes<br>From whence ‘tis nourished. The fire i’th’ flint<br>Shows not till it be struck; our gentle flame<br>Provokes itself, and like the current flies<br>Each bound it chafes. What have you there? |
| PAINTER | A picture, sir. When comes your book forth? |
| POET | Upon the heels of my presentment, sir.<br>Let’s see your piece. |
| PAINTER | (showing the picture) ’Tis a good piece. |
| POET | So ’tis. This comes off well and excellent. |
| PAINTER | Indifferent. |
| POET | Admirable. How this grace<br>Speaks his own standing! What a mental power<br>This eye shoots forth! How big imagination<br>Moves in this lip! To th’ dumbness of the gesture<br>One might interpret. |
| PAINTER | It is a pretty mocking of the life.<br>Here is a touch; is’t good? |
| POET | I will say of it,<br>It tutors nature. Artificial strife<br>Lives in these touches livelier than life. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter certain Senators |
| PAINTER | How this lord is followed! |
| POET | The senators of Athens. Happy man! |
| PAINTER | Look, more. |
| *(didascalia)* | The Senators pass over the stage, and exeunt] |
| POET | You see this confluence, this great flood of visitors.<br>I have in this rough work shaped out a man<br>Whom this beneath world doth embrace and hug<br>With amplest entertainment. My free drift<br>Halts not particularly, but moves itself<br>In a wide sea of tax. No levelled malice<br>Infects one comma in the course I hold,<br>But flies an eagle flight, bold and forth on,<br>Leaving no tract behind. |
| PAINTER | How shall I understand you? |
| POET | I will unbolt to you.<br>You see how all conditions, how all minds,<br>As well of glib and slipp’ry creatures as<br>Of grave and austere quality, tender down<br>Their service to Lord Timon. His large fortune,<br>Upon his good and gracious nature hanging,<br>Subdues and properties to his love and tendance<br>All sorts of hearts; yea, from the glass-faced flatterer<br>To Apemantus, that few things loves better<br>Than to abhor himself; even he drops down<br>The knee before him, and returns in peace,<br>Most rich in Timon’s nod. |
| PAINTER | I saw them speak together. |
| POET | Sir, I have upon a high and pleasant hill<br>Feigned Fortune to be throned. The base o’th’ mount<br>Is ranked with all deserts, all kind of natures<br>That labour on the bosom of this sphere<br>To propagate their states. Amongst them all<br>Whose eyes are on this sovereign lady fixed<br>One do I personate of Lord Timon’s frame,<br>Whom Fortune with her ivory hand wafts to her,<br>Whose present grace to present slaves and servants<br>Translates his rivals. |
| PAINTER | ’Tis conceived to scope.<br>This throne, this Fortune, and this hill, methinks,<br>With one man beckoned from the rest below,<br>Bowing his head against the steepy mount<br>To climb his happiness, would be well expressed<br>In our condition. |
| POET | Nay, sir, but hear me on.<br>All those which were his fellows but of late,<br>Some better than his value, on the moment<br>Follow his strides, his lobbies fill with tendance,<br>Rain sacrificial whisperings in his ear,<br>Make sacred even his stirrup, and through him<br>Drink the free air. |
| PAINTER | Ay, marry, what of these? |
| POET | When Fortune in her shift and change of mood<br>Spurns down her late beloved, all his dependants,<br>Which laboured after him to the mountain’s top<br>Even on their knees and hands, let him fall down,<br>Not one accompanying his declining foot. |
| PAINTER | ’Tis common.<br>A thousand moral paintings I can show<br>That shall demonstrate these quick blows of Fortune’s<br>More pregnantly than words. Yet you do well<br>To show Lord Timon that mean eyes have seen<br>The foot above the head. |
| *(didascalia)* | Trumpets sound. Enter Timon [wearing a rich |
| PAINTER | jewell, with a Messenger from Ventidius; Lucilius |
| *(didascalia)* | and other Servants] attending. Timon addresses |
| PAINTER | himself courteously to every suitor, then speaks to<br><br>the Messenger |
| TIMON | Imprisoned is he, say you? |
| MESSENGER | Ay, my good lord. Five talents is his debt,<br>His means most short, his creditors most strait.<br>Your honourable letter he desires<br>To those have shut him up, which failing,<br>Periods his comfort. |
| TIMON | Noble Ventidius! Well,<br>I am not of that feather to shake off<br>My friend when he must need me. I do know him<br>A gentleman that well deserves a help,<br>Which he shall have. I’ll pay the debt and free him. |
| MESSENGER | Your lordship ever binds him. |
| TIMON | Commend me to him. I will send his ransom;<br>And, being enfranchised, bid him come to me.<br>’Tis not enough to help the feeble up,<br>But to support him after. Fare you well. |
| MESSENGER | All happiness to your honour. Exit |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter an Old Athenian |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Lord Timon, hear me speak. |
| TIMON | Freely, good father. |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Thou hast a servant named Lucilius. |
| TIMON | I have so. What of him? |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Most noble Timon, call the man before thee. |
| TIMON | Attends he here or no? Lucilius! |
| LUCILIUS | (coming forward) Here at your lordship’s service. |
| OLD ATHENIAN | This fellow here, Lord Timon, this thy creature,<br>By night frequents my house. I am a man<br>That from my first have been inclined to thrift,<br>And my estate deserves an heir more raised<br>Than one which holds a trencher. |
| TIMON | Well, what further? |
| OLD ATHENIAN | One only daughter have I, no kin else<br>On whom I may confer what I have got.<br>The maid is fair, o’th’ youngest for a bride,<br>And I have bred her at my dearest cost<br>In qualities of the best. This man of thine<br>Attempts her love. I prithee, noble lord,<br>Join with me to forbid him her resort.<br>Myself have spoke in vain. |
| TIMON | The man is honest. |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Therefore he will be, Timon.<br>His honesty rewards him in itself;<br>It must not bear my daughter. |
| TIMON | Does she love him? |
| OLD ATHENIAN | She is young and apt.<br>Our own precedent passions do instruct us<br>What levity’s in youth. |
| TIMON | (to Lucilius) Love you the maid? |
| LUCILIUS | Ay, my good lord, and she accepts of it. |
| OLD ATHENIAN | If in her marriage my consent be missing,<br>I call the gods to witness, I will choose<br>Mine heir from forth the beggars of the world,<br>And dispossess her all. |
| TIMON | How shall she be endowed<br>If she be mated with an equal husband? |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Three talents on the present; in future, all. |
| TIMON | This gentleman of mine hath served me long.<br>To build his fortune I will strain a little,<br>For ’tis a bond in men. Give him thy daughter.<br>What you bestow in him I’ll counterpoise,<br>And make him weigh with her. |
| OLD ATHENIAN | Most noble lord,<br>Pawn me to this your honour, she is his. |
| TIMON | My hand to thee; mine honour on my promise. |
| LUCILIUS | Humbly I thank your lordship. Never may<br>That state or fortune fall into my keeping<br>Which is not owed to you. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt Lucilius and Old Athenian |
| POET | (presenting a poem to Timon)<br>Vouchsafe my labour, and long live your lordship! |
| TIMON | I thank you. You shall hear from me anon.<br>Go not away. (To Painter) What have you there, my<br>friend? |
| PAINTER | A piece of painting, which I do beseech<br>Your lordship to accept. |
| TIMON | Painting is welcome.<br>The painting is almost the natural man;<br>For since dishonour traffics with man’s nature,<br>He is but outside; these pencilled figures are<br>Even such as they give out. I like your work,<br>And you shall find I like it. Wait attendance<br>Till you hear further from me. |
| PAINTER | The gods preserve ye! |
| TIMON | Well fare you, gentleman. Give me your hand.<br>We must needs dine together. (To Jeweller) Sir, your jewel<br>Hath suffered under praise. |
| JEWELLER | What, my lord, dispraise? |
| TIMON | A mere satiety of commendations.<br>If I should pay you for’t as ’tis extolled<br>It would unclew me quite. |
| JEWELLER | My lord, ’tis rated<br>As those which sell would give; but you well know<br>Things of like value differing in the owners<br>Are prized by their masters. Believe’t, dear lord,<br>You mend the jewel by the wearing it. |
| TIMON | Well mocked. |
| MERCHANT | No, my good lord, he speaks the common tongue<br>Which all men speak with him. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Apemantus |
| TIMON | Look who comes here.<br>Will you be chid? |
| JEWELLER | We will bear, with your lordship. |
| MERCHANT | He’ll spare none. |
| TIMON | Good morrow to thee, gentle Apemantus. |
| APEMANTUS | Till I be gentle, stay thou for thy good morrow—<br>When thou art Timon’s dog, and these knaves honest. |
| TIMON | Why dost thou call them knaves? Thou know’st them<br>not. |
| APEMANTUS | Are they not Athenians? |
| TIMON | Yes. |
| APEMANTUS | Then I repent not. |
| JEWELLER | You know me, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | Thou know’st I do. I called thee by thy name. |
| TIMON | Thou art proud, Apemantus! |
| APEMANTUS | Of nothing so much as that I am not like Timon. |
| TIMON | Whither art going? |
| APEMANTUS | To knock out an honest Athenian’s brains. |
| TIMON | That’s a deed thou’It die for. |
| APEMANTUS | Right, if doing nothing be death by th’ law. |
| TIMON | How likest thou this picture, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | The best for the innocence. |
| TIMON | Wrought he not well that painted it? |
| APEMANTUS | He wrought better that made the painter, and yet he’s but a filthy piece of work. |
| PAINTER | You’re a dog. |
| APEMANTUS | Thy mother’s of my generation. What’s she, if I be a dog? |
| TIMON | Wilt dine with me, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | No, I eat not lords. |
| TIMON | An thou shouldst, thou’dst anger ladies. |
| APEMANTUS | O, they eat lords. So they come by great bellies. |
| TIMON | That’s a lascivious apprehension. |
| APEMANTUS | So thou apprehend’st it; take it for thy labour. |
| TIMON | How dost thou like this jewel, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | Not so well as plain dealing, which will not cost a man a doit. |
| TIMON | What dost thou think ’tis worth? |
| APEMANTUS | Not worth my thinking.<br>How now, poet? |
| POET | How now, philosopher? |
| APEMANTUS | Thou liest. |
| POET | Art not one? |
| APEMANTUS | Yes. |
| POET | Then I lie not. |
| APEMANTUS | Art not a poet? |
| POET | Yes. |
| APEMANTUS | Then thou liest. Look in thy last work, where thou hast feigned him a worthy fellow. |
| POET | That’s not feigned, he is so. |
| APEMANTUS | Yes, he is worthy of thee, and to pay thee for thy labour. He that loves to be flattered is worthy o’th’ flatterer. Heavens, that I were a lord! |
| TIMON | What wouldst do then, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | E’en as Apemantus does now: hate a lord with my heart. |
| TIMON | What, thyself? |
| APEMANTUS | Ay. |
| TIMON | Wherefore? |
| APEMANTUS | That I had no augury but to be a lord.-Art not thou a merchant? |
| MERCHANT | Ay, Apemantus. |
| APEMANTUS | Traffic confound thee, if the gods will not! |
| MERCHANT | If traffic do it, the gods do it. |
| APEMANTUS | Traffic’s thy god, and thy god confound thee! |
| *(didascalia)* | Trumpet sounds. Enter a Messenger |
| TIMON | What trumpet’s that? |
| MESSENGER | ’Tis Alcibiades, and some twenty horse<br>All of companionship. |
| TIMON | (to Servants)<br>Pray entertain them. Give them guide to us.<br>[Exit one or more Servants]<br>[To Jeweller] You must needs dine with me.<br>[To Poet] Go not you hence<br>Till I have thanked you. [To Painter] When dinner’s done<br>Show me this piece. [To all] I am joyful of your sights. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter Alcibiades with [his horsemen] |
| TIMON | Most welcome, sir! |
| APEMANTUS | [aside] So, so, there.<br>Aches contract and starve your supple joints!<br>That there should be small love ’mongst these sweet<br>knaves,<br>And all this courtesy! The strain of man’s bred out<br>Into baboon and monkey. |
| ALCIBIADES | (to Timon)<br>Sir, you have saved my longing, and I feed<br>Most hungrily on your sight. |
| TIMON | Right welcome, sir!<br>Ere we depart, we’ll share a bounteous time<br>In different pleasures. Pray you, let us in. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt all but Apemantus |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter two Lords |
| FIRST LORD | What time o’ day is’t, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | Time to be honest. |
| FIRST LORD | That time serves still. |
| APEMANTUS | The most accursed thou, that still omitt’st it. |
| SECOND LORD | Thou art going to Lord Timon’s feast? |
| APEMANTUS | Ay, to see meat fill knaves, and wine heat fools. |
| SECOND LORD | Fare thee well, fare thee well. |
| APEMANTUS | Thou art a fool to bid me farewell twice. |
| SECOND LORD | Why, Apemantus? |
| APEMANTUS | Shouldst have kept one to thyself, for I mean to give thee none. |
| FIRST LORD | Hang thyself |
| APEMANTUS | No, I will do nothing at thy bidding. Make thy requests to thy friend. |
| SECOND LORD | Away, unpeaceable dog, or I’ll spurn thee hence. |
| APEMANTUS | I will fly, like a dog, the heels o’th’ ass. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exit |
| FIRST LORD | He’s opposite to humanity. Come, shall we in,<br>And taste Lord Timon’s bounty? He outgoes<br>The very heart of kindness. |
| SECOND LORD | He pours it out. Plutus the god of gold<br>Is but his steward; no meed but he repays<br>Sevenfold above itself; no gift to him<br>But breeds the giver a return exceeding<br>All use of quittance. |
| FIRST LORD | The noblest mind he carries<br>That ever governed man. |
| SECOND LORD | Long may he live in fortunes! Shall we in? |
| *(didascalia)* | FIRST LORD I’ll keep you company. Exeunt |
