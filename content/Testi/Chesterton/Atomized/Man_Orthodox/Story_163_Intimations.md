INTIMATIONS

Matthew Arnold did not often say a thoroughly silly thing, but he did
when he criticized Wordsworth for talking of the wonderland of
[[Childhood|childhood]] and objected that a man of thirty had a better appreciation
of landscape, as of course he has in the sense of landscape gardening.
But the white light which Wordsworth meant was not in the sky the [[Child|child]]
looked at, but in the [[Child|child]].  He happened to be a country [[Child|child]];
but it was really only a geographical accident that it was meadow,
grove and stream, and not post, pavement and policeman that were
apparelled in celestial light, the glory and freshness of [[A Dream|a dream]].
Nor is it the mere [[Memory|memory]] of vigour and adventure which really is
stronger at sixteen than at six; it is quite a different sensation.
And to this day I cannot see certain things such as a white [[Horse|horse]]
or wood painted white, with out a pang of pleasure going through me
stronger than sex or the [[Fear|fear]] of [[Death|death]].  And unlike sex and [[Death|death]],
it does not concentrate; for an instant, like a lost [[Lightning|lightning]] flash,
it has spread over the whole world, full of too many wonders to
be wondered at.

Here enters the psychological educationist with his subconsciousness
and his first impressions, to remark shrewdly, `This is because
somebody in your childhood gave you a wooden horse or painted it
white for you'.  To which I reply respectfully, `Yes, [[Fool|fool]], yes.
Admirable and astonishing idiot, quite so.  But has it ever
occurred to you to ask why a pot of paint and a piece of wood
should blaze with divine [[Beauty|beauty]] to anybody at any [[Time|time]]; or why they
should do it to a [[Child|child]] of four any more than to a man of forty?
When you have retired for a few years to reflect on the point,
we will resume this conversation.'

                                          The New Witness, June 17th, 1921
