### SCENE I.

| Chi parla | Battuta |
|---|---|
| *(didascalia)* | An Apartment in the Castle. Enter CONRAD. |
| Conrad | So, I am safe emerged from these broils!<br>Amid the wreck of thousands I am whole;<br>For every crime I have a laurel-wreath,<br>For every lie a lordship. Nor yet has<br>My ship of fortune furl’d her silken sails,<br>Let her glide on! I have news for her<br>Shall- |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter AURANTHE. |
| Auranthe | Conrad! what tidings? Good, if I may guess<br>From your alert eyes and high-lifted brows.<br>What tidings of the battle? Albert? Ludolph? Otho? |
| Conrad | You guess aright. And, sister, slurring o’er<br>Our by-gone quarrels, I confess my heart<br>Is beating with a child’s anxiety,<br>To make our golden fortune known to you. |
| Auranthe | So serious? |
| Conrad | Yes, so serious, that before<br>I utter even the shadow of a hint<br>Concerning what will make that sin-worn cheek<br>Blush joyous blood through every lineament,<br>You must make here a solemn vow to me. |
| Auranthe | I prythee, Conrad, do not overact<br>The hypocrite what vow would you impose? |
| Conrad | Trust me for once, that you may be assured<br>’Tis not confiding to a broken reed,<br>A poor Court-bankrupt, outwitted and lost,<br>Revolve these facts in your acutest mood,<br>In such a mood as now you listen to me:<br>A few days since, I was an open rebel<br>Against the Emperor, had suborn’d his son,<br>Drawn off his nobles to revolt, and shown<br>Contented fools causes for discontent<br>Fresh hatch’d in my ambition’s eagle nest<br>So thrived I as a rebel, and behold<br>Now I am Otho’s favourite, his dear friend,<br>His right hand, his brave Conrad. |
| Auranthe | I confess<br>You have intrigued with these unsteady times<br>To admiration; but to be a favourite |
| Conrad | I saw my moment. The Hungarians,<br>Collected silently in holes and corners,<br>Appeared, a sudden host, in the open day.<br>I should have perish’d in our empire’s wreck,<br>But, calling interest loyalty, swore faith<br>To most believing Otho; and so helped<br>His blood-stained ensigns to the victory<br>In yesterday’s hard fight, that it has turn’d<br>The edge of his sharp wrath to eager kindness. |
| Auranthe | So far yourself. But what is this to me<br>More than that I am glad? I gratulate you. |
| Conrad | Yes, sister, but it does regard you greatly,<br>Nearly, momentously, aye, painfully!<br>Make me this vow |
| Auranthe | Concerning whom or what? |
| Conrad | Albert! |
| Auranthe | I would inquire somewhat of him:<br>You had a letter from me touching him?<br>No treason ‘gainst his head in deed or word!<br>Surely you spar’d him at my earnest prayer?<br>Give me the letter it should not exist! |
| Conrad | At one pernicious charge of the enemy,<br>I, for a moment-whiles, was prisoner ta’en<br>And rifled, stuff! the horses’ hoofs have minc’d it! |
| Auranthe | He is alive? |
| Conrad | He is! but here make oath<br>To alienate him from your scheming brain,<br>Divorce him from your solitary thoughts,<br>And cloud him in such utter banishment,<br>That when his person meets again your eye,<br>Your vision shall quite lose its memory,<br>And wander past him as through vacancy. |
| Auranthe | I’ll not be perjured. |
| Conrad | No, nor great, nor mighty;<br>You would not wear a crown, or rule a kingdom.<br>To you it is indifferent. |
| Auranthe | What means this? |
| Conrad | You’ll not be perjured! Go to Albert then,<br>That camp-mushroom dishonour of our house.<br>Go, page his dusty heels upon a march,<br>Furbish his jingling baldric while he sleeps,<br>And share his mouldy ration in a siege.<br>Yet stay, perhaps a charm may call you back,<br>And make the widening circlets of your eyes<br>Sparkle with healthy fevers. The Emperor<br>Hath given consent that you should marry Ludolph! |
| Auranthe | Can it be, brother? For a golden crown<br>With a queen’s awful lips I doubly thank you!<br>This is to wake in Paradise ! Farewell<br>Thou clod of yesterday ’twas not myself!<br>Not till this moment did I ever feel<br>My spirit’s faculties! I’ll flatter you<br>For this, and be you ever proud of it;<br>Thou, Jove-like, struck’dst thy forehead,<br>And from the teeming marrow of thy brain<br>I spring complete Minerva! But the prince<br>His highness Ludolph where is he? |
| Conrad | I know not:<br>When, lackeying my counsel at a beck,<br>The rebel lords, on bended knees, received<br>The Emperor’s pardon, Ludolph kept aloof,<br>Sole, in a stiff, fool-hardy, sulky pride;<br>Yet, for all this, I never saw a father<br>In such a sickly longing for his son.<br>We shall soon see him, for the Emperor<br>He will be here this morning. |
| Auranthe | That I heard<br>Among the midnight rumours from the camp. |
| Conrad | You give up Albert to me? |
| Auranthe | Harm him not!<br>E’en for his highness Ludolph’s sceptry hand,<br>I would not Albert suffer any wrong. |
| Conrad | Have I not laboured, plotted ? |
| Auranthe | See you spare him:<br>Nor be pathetic, my kind benefactor,<br>On all the many bounties of your hand,<br>’Twas for yourself you laboured not for me!<br>Do you not count, when I am queen, to take<br>Advantage of your chance discoveries<br>Of my poor secrets, and so hold a rod<br>Over my life? |
| Conrad | Let not this slave this villain<br>Be cause of feud between us. See! he comes!<br>Look, woman, look, your Albert is quite safe!<br>In haste it seems. Now shall I be in the way,<br>And wish’d with silent curses in my grave,<br>Or side by side with ‘whelmed mariners. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter ALBERT. |
| Albert | Fair on your graces fall this early morrow!<br>So it is like to do, without my prayers,<br>For your right noble names, like favourite tunes,<br>Have fallen full frequent from our Emperor’s lips,<br>High commented with smiles. |
| Auranthe | Noble Albert! |
| Conrad | Noble! |
| Auranthe | Such salutation argues a glad heart<br>In our prosperity. We thank you, sir. |
| Albert | Lady! O, would to Heaven your poor servant<br>Could do you better service than mere words!<br>But I have other greeting than mine own,<br>From no less man than Otho, who has sent<br>This ring as pledge of dearest amity;<br>’Tis chosen I hear from Hymen’s jewel’ry,<br>And you will prize it, lady, I doubt not,<br>Beyond all pleasures past, and all to come.<br>To you great duke |
| Conrad | To me! What of me, ha? |
| Albert | What pleas’d your grace to say? |
| Conrad | Your message, sir! |
| Albert | You mean not this to me? |
| Conrad | Sister, this way;<br>For there shall be no ‘‘‘gentle Alberts” now, [Aside.<br>No “sweet Auranthes!”<br>[Exeunt CONRAD and AURANTHE. |
| Albert | The duke is out of temper; if he knows<br>More than a brother of a sister ought,<br>I should not quarrel with his peevishness.<br>Auranthe Heaven preserve her always fair!<br>Is in the heady, proud, ambitious vein;<br>I bicker not with her, bid her farewell!<br>She has taken flight from me, then let her soar,<br>He is a fool who stands at pining gaze!<br>But for poor Ludolph, he is food for sorrow:<br>No levelling bluster of my licens’d thoughts,<br>No military swagger of my mind,<br>Can smother from myself the wrong I’ve done him,<br>Without design, indeed, yet it is so,<br>And opiate for the conscience have I none! [Exit. |

### SCENE II.

| Chi parla | Battuta |
|---|---|
| *(didascalia)* | The Court-yard of the Castle. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter, from the outer gate, OTHO, Nobles, Knights, and |
| Attendants | The Soldiers halt at the gate, with Banners in sight. |
| Otho | Where is my noble herald? |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter CONRAD, from the Castle, attended by two Knights and |
| Servants | ALBERT following.<br>Well, hast told<br>Auranthe our intent imperial?<br>Lest our rent banners, too o’ the sudden shown,<br>Should fright her silken casements, and dismay<br>Her household to our lack of entertainment.<br>A victory! |
| Conrad | God save illustrious Otho! |
| Otho | Aye, Conrad, it will pluck out all grey hairs;<br>It is the best physician for the spleen;<br>The courtliest inviter to a feast;<br>The subtlest excuser of small faults;<br>And a nice judge in the age and smack of wine. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter, from the Castle, AURANTHE, followed by Pages holdingup her robes, and a tram of Women. She kneels. |
| Otho | Hail my sweet hostess! I do thank the stars,<br>Or my good soldiers, or their ladies’ eyes,<br>That, after such a merry battle fought,<br>I can, all safe in body and in soul,<br>Kiss your fair hand and lady fortune’s too.<br>My ring! now, on my life, it doth rejoice<br>These lips to feel ‘t on this soft ivory!<br>Keep it, my brightest daughter; it may prove<br>The little prologue to a line of kings.<br>I strove against thee and my hot-blood son,<br>Dull blockhead that I was to be so blind,<br>But now my sight is clear; forgive me, lady. |
| Auranthe | My lord, I was a vassal to your frown,<br>And now your favour makes me but more humble;<br>In wintry winds the simple snow is safe,<br>But fadeth at the greeting of the sun:<br>Unto thine anger I might well have spoken,<br>Taking on me a woman’s privilege,<br>But this so sudden kindness makes me dumb. |
| Otho | What need of this? Enough, if you will be<br>A potent tutoress to my wayward boy,<br>And teach him, what it seems his nurse could not<br>To say, for once, I thank you. Sigifred! |
| Albert | He has not yet return’d, my gracious liege. |
| Otho | What then! No tidings of my friendly Arab? |
| Conrad | None, mighty Otho.<br>[To one of his Knights, who goes out.<br>Send forth instantly<br>An hundred horsemen from my honoured gates,<br>To scour the plains and search the cottages.<br>Cry a reward, to him who shall first bring<br>News of that vanished Arabian,<br>A full-heap’d helmet of the purest gold. |
| Otho | More thanks, good Conrad; for, except my son’s,<br>There is no face I rather would behold<br>Than that same quick-eyed pagan’s. By the saints,<br>This coming night of banquets must not light<br>Her dazzling torches; nor the music breathe<br>Smooth, without clashing cymbal, tones of peace<br>And in-door melodies; nor the ruddy wine<br>Ebb spouting to the lees; if I pledge not<br>In my first cup, that Arab! |
| Albert | Mighty Monarch,<br>I wonder not this stranger’s victor-deeds<br>So, hang upon your spirit. Twice in the fight<br>It was my chance to meet his olive brow,<br>Triumphant in the enemy’s shatter ‘d rhomb;<br>And, to say truth, in any Christian arm<br>I never saw such prowess. |
| Otho | Did you ever?<br>O, ’tis a noble boy! tut! what do I say?<br>I mean a triple Saladin, whose eyes,<br>When in the glorious scuffle they met mine,<br>Seem’d to say “Sleep, old man, in safety sleep;<br>I am the victory!” |
| Conrad | Pity he’s not here. |
| Otho | And my son too, pity he is not here.<br>Lady Auranthe, I would not make you blush,<br>But can you give a guess where Ludolph is?<br>Know you not of him? |
| Auranthe | Indeed, my liege, no secret |
| Otho | Nay, nay, without more words, dost know of him? |
| Auranthe | I would I were so over-fortunate,<br>Both for his sake and mine, and to make glad<br>A father’s ears with tidings of his son. |
| Otho | I see ’tis like to be a tedious day.<br>Were Theodore and Gonfred and the rest<br>Sent forth with my commands? |
| Albert | Aye, my lord. |
| Otho | And no news! No news! ‘Faith! ’tis very strange<br>He thus avoids us. Lady, is’t not strange?<br>Will he be truant to you too? It is a shame. |
| Conrad | Will ‘t please your highness enter, and accept<br>The unworthy welcome of your servant’s house?<br>Leaving your cares to one whose diligence<br>May in few hours make pleasures of them all. |
| Otho | Not so tedious, Conrad. No, no, no,<br>I must see Ludolph or the What’s that shout!<br>Voices without. Huzza! huzza! Long live the Emperor! |
| Other Voices | Fall back! Away there! |
| Otho | Say, what noise is that?<br>[ALBERT advancing from the bark of the Stage, whither he had<br>hastened on hearing the cheers of the soldiery. |
| Albert | It is young Gersa, the Hungarian prince,<br>Pick’d like a red stag from the fallow herd<br>Of prisoners. Poor prince, forlorn he steps,<br>Slow, and demure, and proud in his despair.<br>If I may judge by his so tragic bearing,<br>His eye not downcast, and his folded arm,<br>He doth this moment wish himself asleep<br>Among his fallen captains on yon plains. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter GERSA, in chains, and guarded, |
| Otho | Well said, Sir Albert. |
| Gersa | Not a word of greeting.<br>No welcome to a princely visitor,<br>Most mighty Otho? Will not my great host<br>Vouchsafe a syllable, before he bids<br>His gentlemen conduct me with all care<br>To some securest lodgings? cold perhaps! |
| Otho | What mood is this? Hath fortune touch’d thy brain? |
| Gersa | kings and princes of this fev’rous world,<br>What abject things, what mockeries must ye be,<br>What nerveless minions of safe palaces!<br>When here, a monarch, whose proud foot is used<br>To fallen princes’ necks, as to his stirrup,<br>Must needs exclaim that I am mad forsooth,<br>Because I cannot flatter with bent knees<br>My conqueror! |
| Otho | Gersa, I think you wrong me:<br>I think I have a better fame abroad. |
| Gersa | I prythee mock me not with gentle speech,<br>But, as a favour, bid me from thy presence;<br>Let me no longer be the wondering food<br>Of all these eyes; prythee command me hence! |
| Otho | Do not mistake me, Gersa. That you may not,<br>Come, fair Auranthe, try if your soft hands<br>Can manage those hard rivets to set free<br>So brave a prince and soldier. |
| Auranthe | Welcome task! |
| Gersa | I am wound up in deep astonishment!<br>Thank you, fair lady. Otho! emperor!<br>You rob me of myself; my dignity<br>Is now your infant; I am a weak child. |
| Otho | Give me your hand, and let this kindly grasp<br>Live in our memories. |
| Gersa | In mine it will.<br>I blush to think of my unchasten’d tongue;<br>But I was haunted by the monstrous ghost<br>Of all our slain battalions. Sire, reflect,<br>And pardon you will grant, that, at this hour,<br>The bruised remnants of our stricken camp<br>Are huddling undistinguish’d my dear friends,<br>With common thousands, into shallow graves. |
| Otho | Enough, most noble Gersa. You are free<br>To cheer the brave remainder of your host<br>By your own healing presence, and that too,<br>Not as their leader merely, but their king;<br>For, as I hear, the wily enemy,<br>Who eas’d the crownet from your infant brows,<br>Bloody Taraxa, is among the dead. |
| Gersa | Then I retire, so generous Otho please,<br>Bearing with me a weight of benefits<br>Too heavy to be borne. |
| Otho | It is not so;<br>Still understand me, King of Hungary,<br>Nor judge my open purposes awry.<br>Though I did hold you high in my esteem<br>For your self’s sake, I do not personate<br>The stage-play emperor to entrap applause,<br>To set the silly sort o’ the world agape,<br>And make the politic smile; no, I have heard<br>How in the Council you condemn ‘d this war,<br>Urging the perfidy of broken faith,<br>For that I am your friend. |
| Gersa | If ever, sire,<br>You are mine enemy, I dare here swear<br>‘Twill not be Gersa’s fault. Otho, farewell! |
| Otho | Will you return, Prince, to our banqueting? |
| Gersa | As to my father’s board I will return. |
| Otho | Conrad, with all due ceremony, give<br>The prince a regal escort to his camp;<br>Albert, go thou and bear him company.<br>Gersa, farewell! |
| Gersa | All happiness attend you! |
| Otho | Return with what good speed you may; for soon<br>We must consult upon our terms of peace.<br>[Exeunt GERSA and ALBERT with others.<br><br>And thus a marble column do I build<br>To prop my empire’s dome. Conrad, in thee<br>I have another stedfast one, to uphold<br>The portals of my state; and, for my own<br>Pre-eminence and safety, I will strive<br>To keep thy strength upon its pedestal.<br>For, without thee, this day I might have been<br>A show-monster about the streets of Prague,<br>In chains, as just now stood that noble prince:<br>And then to me no mercy had been shown,<br>For when the conquered lion is once dungeon’d,<br>Who lets him forth again? or dares to give<br>An old lion sugar-cates of mild reprieve?<br>Not to thine ear alone I make confession,<br>But to all here, as, by experience,<br>I know how the great basement of all power<br>Is frankness, and a true tongue to the world;<br>And how intriguing secrecy is proof<br>Of fear and weakness, and a hollow state.<br>Conrad, I owe thee much. |
| Conrad | To kiss that hand,<br>My emperor, is ample recompense,<br>For a mere act of duty. |
| Otho | Thou art wrong;<br>For what can any man on earth do more?<br>We will make trial of your house’s welcome,<br>My bright Auranthe! |
| Conrad | How is Friedburg honoured! |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter ETHELBERT and six Monks. |
| Ethelbert | The benison of heaven on your head,<br>Imperial Otho! |
| Otho | Who stays me? Speak! Quick! |
| Ethelbert | Pause but one moment, mighty conqueror<br>Upon the threshold of this house of joy. |
| Otho | Pray, do not prose, good Ethelbert, but speak<br>What is your purpose. |
| Ethelbert | The restoration of some captive maids,<br>Devoted to Heaven’s pious ministries,<br>Who, being driven from their religious cells,<br>And kept in thraldom by our enemy,<br>When late this province was a lawless spoil,<br>Still weep amid the wild Hungarian camp,<br>Though hemm’d around by thy victorious arms. |
| Otho | Demand the holy sisterhood in our name<br>From Gersa’s tents. Farewell, old Ethelbert. |
| Ethelbert | The saints will bless you for this pious care. |
| Otho | Daughter, your hand; Ludolph’s would fit it best. |
| Conrad | Ho ! let the music sound !<br><br>[Music. ETHELBERT raises his hands, as in benediction of OTHO. |
| *(didascalia)* | Exeunt severally. The scene closes on them. |

### SCENE III.

| Chi parla | Battuta |
|---|---|
| *(didascalia)* | The Country, with the Castle in the distance. |
| *(didascalia)* | Enter LUDOLPH and SIGIFRED. |
| Ludolph | You have my secret; let it not be breath ‘d. |
| Sigifred | Still give me leave to wonder that the Prince<br>Ludolph and the swift Arab are the same ;<br>Still to rejoice that ’twas a German arm<br>Death doing in a turban’d masquerade. |
| Ludolph | The Emperor must not know it, Sigifred. |
| Sigifred | I prythee, why? What happier hour of time<br>Could thy pleas’d star point down upon from heaven<br>With silver index, bidding thee make peace? |
| Ludolph | Still it must not be known, good Sigifred;<br>The star may point oblique. |
| Sigifred | If Otho knew<br>His son to be that unknown Mussulman<br>After whose spurring heels he sent me forth,<br>With one of his well-pleas’d Olympian oaths,<br>The charters of man’s greatness, at this hour<br>He would be watching round the castle walls,<br>And, like an anxious warder, strain his sight<br>For the first glimpse of such a son return ‘d<br>Ludolph, that blast of the Hungarians,<br>That Saracenic meteor of the fight,<br>That silent fury, whose fell Scymitar<br>Kept danger all aloof from Otho’s head,<br>And left him space for wonder. |
| Ludolph | Say no more.<br>Not as a swordsman would I pardon claim,<br>But as a son. The bronz’d centurion,<br>Long toil’d in foreign wars, and whose high deeds<br>Are shaded in a forest of tall spears,<br>Known only to his troop, hath greater plea<br>Of favour with my sire than I can have. |
| Sigifred | My lord, forgive me that I cannot see<br>How this proud temper with clear reason squares.<br>What made you then, with such an anxious love,<br>Hover around that life, whose bitter days<br>You vext with bad revolt? Was ‘t opium,<br>Or the mad-fumed wine? Nay, do not frown,<br>I rather would grieve with you than upbraid. |
| Ludolph | I do believe you. No, ’twas not to make<br>A father his son’s debtor, or to heal<br>His deep heart-sickness for a rebel child.<br>Twas done in memory of my boyish days,<br>Poor cancel for his kindness to my youth,<br>For all his calming of my childish griefs,<br>And all his smiles upon my merriment.<br>No, not a thousand foughten fields could sponge<br>Those days paternal from my memory,<br>Though now upon my head he heaps disgrace. |
| Sigifred | My Prince, you think too harshly |
| Ludolph | Can I so?<br>Hath he not gall’d my spirit to the quick?<br>And with a sullen rigour obstinate<br>Pour’d out a phial of wrath upon my faults?<br>Hunted me as the Tartar does the boar,<br>Driven me to the very edge o’ the world,<br>And almost put a price upon my head? |
| Sigifred | Remember how he spar’d the rebel lords. |
