THE FAMILY REUNION
Persons
AMY, DOWAGER LADY MONCHENSEY, IVY, VIOLET, and AGATHA, her younger sisters
COL. THE HON. GERALD PIPER, and THE HON. CHARLES PIPER, brothers of her deceased husband
MARY, daughter of a deceased cousin of Lady Monchensey
DENMAN, a parlourmaid
HARRY, LORD MONCHENSEY, Amy’s eldest son
DOWNING, his servant and chauffeur
DR. WARBURTON
SERGEANT WINCHELL
THE EUMENIDES
The scene is laid in a country house in the
North of England

| *(didascalia)* | [DENMAN enters to draw the curtains] |
| AMY | Not yet! I will ring for you. It is still quite light.<br><br><br>I have nothing to do but watch the days draw out,<br>Now that I sit in the house from October to June,<br>And the swallow comes too soon and the spring will be over<br>And the cuckoo will be gone before I am out again.<br>O Sun, that was once so warm, O Light that was taken for granted<br>When I was young and strong, and sun and light unsought for<br>And the night unfeared and the day expected<br>And clocks could be trusted, tomorrow assured<br>And time would not stop in the dark!<br>Put on the lights. But leave the curtains undrawn.<br>Make up the fire. Will the spring never come? I am cold. |
| AGATHA | Wishwood was always a cold place, Amy. |
| IVY | I have always told Amy she should go south in the winter.<br><br><br>Were I in Amy’s position, I would go south in the winter.<br>I would follow the sun, not wait for the sun to come here.<br>I would go south in the winter, if I could afford it,<br>Not freeze, as I do, in Bayswater, by a gas-fire counting shillings. |
| VIOLET | Go south! to the English circulating libraries,<br><br><br>To the military widows and the English chaplains,<br>To the chilly deck-chair and the strong cold tea —<br>The strong cold stewed bad Indian tea. |
| CHARLES | That’s not Amy’s style at all. We are country-bred people.<br><br><br>Amy has been too long used to our ways<br>Living with horses and dogs and guns<br>Ever to want to leave England in the winter.<br>But a single man like me is better off in London:<br>A man can be very cosy at his club<br>Even in an English winter. |
| GERALD | Well, as for me,<br><br><br>I’d just as soon be a subaltern again<br>To be back in the East. An incomparable climate<br>For a man who can exercise a little common prudence;<br>And your servants look after you very much better. |
| AMY | My servants are perfectly competent, Gerald.<br><br><br>I can still see to that. |
| VIOLET | Well, as for me,<br><br><br>I would never go south, no, definitely never,<br>Even could I do it as well as Amy:<br>England’s bad enough, I would never go south,<br>Simply to see the vulgarest people —<br>You can keep out of their way at home;<br>People with money from heaven knows where — |
| GERALD | Dividends from aeroplane shares. |
| VIOLET | They bathe all day and they dance all night<br><br><br>In the absolute minimum of clothes. |
| CHARLES | It’s the cocktail-drinking does the harm:<br><br><br>There’s nothing on earth so bad for the young.<br>All that a civilised person needs<br>Is a glass of dry sherry or two before dinner.<br>The modern young people don’t know what they’re drinking,<br>Modern young people don’t care what they’re eating;<br>They’ve lost their sense of taste and smell<br>Because of their cocktails and cigarettes. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter DENMAN with sherry and whisky. CHARLES takes sherry and GERALD whisky.] |
| CHARLES | That’s what it comes to. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Lights a cigarette] |
| IVY | The younger generation<br><br><br>Are undoubtedly decadent. |
| CHARLES | The younger generation<br><br><br>Are not what we were. Haven’t the stamina,<br>Haven’t the sense of responsibility. |
| GERALD | You’re being very hard on the younger generation.<br><br><br>I don’t come across them very much now, myself;<br>But I must say I’ve met some very decent specimens<br>And some first-class shots — better than you were,<br>Charles, as I remember. Besides, you’ve got to make allowances:<br>We haven’t left them such an easy world to live in.<br>Let the younger generation speak for itself:<br>It’s Mary’s generation. What does she think about it? |
| MARY | Really, Cousin Gerald, if you want information<br><br><br>About the younger generation, you must ask someone else.<br>I’m afraid that I don’t deserve the compliment:<br>I don’t belong to any generation. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Exit] |
| VIOLET | Really, Gerald, I must say you’re very tactless,<br><br><br>And I think that Charles might have been more considerate. |
| GERALD | I’m very sorry: but why was she upset?<br><br><br>I only meant to draw her into the conversation. |
| CHARLES | She’s a nice girl; but it’s a difficult age for her.<br><br><br>I suppose she must be getting on for thirty?<br>She ought to be married, that’s what it is. |
| AMY | So she should have been, if things had gone as I intended.<br><br><br>Harry’s return does not make things easy for her<br>At the moment: but life may still go right.<br>Meanwhile, let us drop the subject. The less said the better. |
| GERALD | That reminds me, Amy,<br><br><br>When are the boys all due to arrive? |
| AMY | I do not want the clock to stop in the dark.<br><br><br>If you want to know why I never leave Wishwood<br>That is the reason. I keep Wishwood alive<br>To keep the family alive, to keep them together,<br>To keep me alive, and I live to keep them.<br>You none of you understand how old you are<br>And death will come to you as a mild surprise,<br>A momentary shudder in a vacant room.<br>Only Agatha seems to discover some meaning in death<br>Which I cannot find.<br>— I am only certain of Arthur and John,<br>Arthur in London, John in Leicestershire:<br><br><br>They should both be here in good time for dinner.<br>Harry telephoned to me from Marseilles,<br>He would come by air to Paris, and so to London,<br>And hoped to arrive in the course of the evening. |
| VIOLET | Harry was always the most likely to be late. |
| AMY | This time, it will not be his fault.<br><br><br>We are very lucky to have Harry at all. |
| IVY | And when will you have your birthday cake, Amy,<br><br><br>And open your presents? |
| AMY | After dinner:<br><br><br>That is the best time. |
| IVY | It is the first time<br><br><br>You have not had your cake and your presents at tea. |
| AMY | This is a very particular occasion<br><br><br>As you ought to know. It will be the first time<br>For eight years that we have all been together. |
| AGATHA | It is going to be rather painful for Harry<br><br><br>After eight years and all that has happened<br>To come back to Wishwood. |
| GERALD | Why, painful? |
| VIOLET | Gerald! you know what Agatha means. |
| AGATHA | I mean painful, because everything is irrevocable,<br><br><br>Because the past is irremediable,<br>Because the future can only be built<br>Upon the real past. Wandering in the tropics<br>Or against the painted scene of the Mediterranean,<br>Harry must often have remembered Wishwood —<br>The nursery tea, the school holiday,<br>The daring feats on the old pony,<br>And thought to creep back through the little door.<br>He will find a new Wishwood. Adaptation is hard. |
| AMY | Nothing is changed, Agatha, at Wishwood.<br><br><br>Everything is kept as it was when he left it,<br>Except the old pony, and the mongrel setter<br>Which I had to have destroyed.<br>Nothing has been changed. I have seen to that. |
| AGATHA | Yes. I mean that at Wishwood he will find another Harry.<br><br><br>The man who returns will have to meet<br>The boy who left. Round by the stables,<br>In the coach-house, in the orchard,<br>In the plantation, down the corridor<br>That led to the nursery, round the corner<br>Of the new wing, he will have to face him —<br>And it will not be a very jolly corner.<br>When the loop in time comes — and it does not come for everybody —<br><br><br>The hidden is revealed, and the spectres show themselves. |
| GERALD | I don’t in the least know what you’re talking about.<br><br><br>You seem to be wanting to give us all the hump.<br>I must say, this isn’t cheerful for Amy’s birthday<br>Or for Harry’s homecoming. Make him feel at home, I say!<br>Make him feel that what has happened doesn’t matter.<br>He’s taken his medicine, I’ve no doubt.<br>Let him marry again and carry on at Wishwood. |
| AMY | Thank you, Gerald. Though Agatha means<br><br><br>As a rule, a good deal more than she cares to betray,<br>I am bound to say that I agree with you. |
| CHARLES | I never wrote to him when he lost his wife —<br><br><br>That was just about a year ago, wasn’t it?<br>Do you think that I ought to mention it now?<br>It seems to me too late. |
| AMY | Much too late.<br><br><br>If he wants to talk about it, that’s another matter;<br>But I don’t believe he will. He will wish to forget it.<br>I do not mince matters in front of the family:<br>You can call it nothing but a blessed relief.<br>VIOLET, I call it providential. |
| IVY | Yet it must have been shocking,<br><br><br>Especially to lose anybody in that way —<br><br><br>Swept off the deck in the middle of a storm,<br>And never even to recover the body. |
| CHARLES | ‘Well-known Peeress Vanishes from Liner’. |
| GERALD | Yes, it’s odd to think of her as permanently missing. |
| VIOLET | Had she been drinking? |
| AMY | I would never ask him. |
| IVY | These things are much better not enquired into.<br><br><br>She may have done it in a fit of temper. |
| GERALD | I never met her. |
| AMY | I am very glad you did not.<br><br><br>I am very glad that none of you ever met her.<br>It will make the situation very much easier<br>And is why I was so anxious you should all be here.<br>She never would have been one of the family,<br>She never wished to be one of the family,<br>She only wanted to keep him to herself<br>To satisfy her vanity. That’s why she dragged him<br>All over Europe and half round the world<br>To expensive hotels and undesirable society<br>Which she could choose herself. She never wanted<br>Harry’s relations or Harry’s old friends;<br>She never wanted to fit herself to Harry,<br>But only to bring Harry down to her own level.<br>A restless shivering painted shadow<br>In life, she is less than a shadow in death.<br>You might as well all of you know the truth<br>For the sake of the future. There can be no grief<br>And no regret and no remorse.<br>I would have prevented it if I could. For the sake of the future:<br>Harry is to take command at Wishwood<br>And I hope we can contrive his future happiness.<br>Do not discuss his absence. Please behave only<br>As if nothing had happened in the last eight years. |
| GERALD | That will be a little difficult. |
| VIOLET | Nonsense, Gerald!<br><br><br>You must see for yourself it’s the only thing to do. |
| AGATHA | Thus with most careful devotion<br><br><br>Thus with precise attention<br>To detail, interfering preparation<br>Of that which is already prepared<br>Men tighten the knot of confusion<br>Into perfect misunderstanding,<br>Reflecting a pocket-torch of observation<br>Upon each other’s opacity<br>Neglecting all the admonitions<br>From the world around the corner<br>The wind’s talk in the dry holly-tree<br>The inclination of the moon<br>The attraction of the dark passage<br>The paw under the door.<br>CHORUS (IVY, VIOLET, GERALD and CHARLES). Why do we feel embarrassed, impatient, fretful, ill at ease,<br><br><br>Assembled like amateur actors who have not been assigned their parts?<br><br><br>Like amateur actors in a dream when the curtain rises, to find themselves dressed for a different play, or having rehearsed the wrong parts,<br><br><br>Waiting for the rustling in the stalls, the titter in the dress circle, the laughter and catcalls in the gallery? |
| CHARLES | I might have been in St. James’s Street, in a comfortable chair rather nearer the fire. |
| IVY | I might have been visiting Cousin Lily at Sidmouth, if I had not had to come to this party. |
| GERALD | I might have been staying with Compton-Smith, down at his place in Dorset. |
| VIOLET | I should have been helping Lady Bumpus, at the Vicar’s American Tea. |
| CHORUS | Yet we are here at Amy’s command, to play an unread part in some monstrous farce, ridiculous in some nightmare pantomime. |
| AMY | What’s that? I thought I saw someone pass the window.<br><br><br>What time is it? |
| CHARLES | Nearly twenty to seven. |
| AMY | John should be here now, he has the shortest way to come.<br><br><br>John at least, if not Arthur. Hark, there is someone coming:<br>Yes, it must be John. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter HARRY] |
| AMY |                                      Harry! |
| *(didascalia)* | [HARRY stops suddenly at the door and stares at the window] |
| IVY | Welcome, Harry! |
| GERALD | Well done! |
| VIOLET | Welcome home to Wishwood! |
| CHARLES | Why, what’s the matter? |
| AMY | Harry, if you want the curtains drawn you should let me ring for Denman. |
| HARRY | How can you sit in this blaze of light for all the world to look at?<br><br><br>If you knew how you looked, when I saw you through the window!<br>Do you like to be stared at by eyes through a window? |
| AMY | You forget, Harry, that you are at Wishwood,<br><br><br>Not in town, where you have to close the blinds.<br>There is no one to see you but our servants who belong here.<br>And who all want to see you back, Harry. |
| HARRY | Look there, look there: do you see them? |
| GERALD | No, I don’t see anyone about. |
| HARRY | No, no, not there. Look there!<br><br><br>Can’t you see them? You don’t see them, but I see them,<br><br><br>And they see me. This is the first time that I have seen them.<br>In the Java Straits, in the Sunda Sea,<br>In the sweet sickly tropical night, I knew they were coming.<br>In Italy, from behind the nightingale’s thicket,<br>The eyes stared at me, and corrupted that song.<br>Behind the palm trees in the Grand Hotel<br>They were always there. But I did not see them.<br><br><br>Why should they wait until I came back to Wishwood?<br>There were a thousand places where I might have met them!<br>Why here? why here?<br>                                     Many happy returns of the day, mother.<br>Aunt Ivy, Aunt Violet, Uncle Gerald, Uncle Charles. Agatha. |
| AMY | We are very glad to have you back, Harry.<br><br><br>Now we shall all be together for dinner.<br>The servants have been looking forward to your coming:<br>Would you like to have them in after dinner<br>Or wait till tomorrow? I am sure you must be tired.<br>You will find everybody here, and everything the same.<br>Mr. Bevan — you remember — wants to call tomorrow<br>On some legal business, a question about taxes —<br>But I think you would rather wait till you are rested.<br>Your room is all ready for you. Nothing has been changed. |
| HARRY | Changed? nothing changed? how can you say that nothing is changed?<br><br><br>You all look so withered and young. |
| GERALD | We must have a ride tomorrow.<br><br><br>You’ll find you know the country as well as ever.<br>There wasn’t an inch of it you didn’t know.<br>But you’ll have to see about a couple of new hunters. |
| CHARLES | And I’ve a new wine merchant to recommend you;<br><br><br>Your cellar could do with a little attention. |
| IVY | And you’ll really have to find a successor to old Hawkins.<br><br><br>It’s really high time the old man was pensioned.<br>He’s let the rock garden go to rack and ruin,<br>And he’s nearly half blind. I’ve spoken to your mother<br>Time and time again: she’s done nothing about it<br>Because she preferred to wait for your coming. |
| VIOLET | And time and time again I have spoken to your mother<br>About the waste that goes on in the kitchen.<br>Mrs. Packell is too old to know what she is doing.<br>It really needs a man in charge of things at Wishwood. |
| AMY | You see your aunts and uncles are very helpful, Harry.<br><br><br>I have always found them forthcoming with advice<br>Which I have never taken. Now it is your business.<br>I have only struggled to keep Wishwood going<br>And to make no changes before your return.<br>Now it’s for you to manage. I am an old woman.<br>They can give me no further advice when I’m dead. |
| IVY | Oh, dear Amy!<br><br><br>No one wants you to die, I’m sure!<br>Now that Harry’s back, is the time to think of living. |
| HARRY | Time and time and time, and change, no change!<br><br><br>You all of you try to talk as if nothing had happened,<br>And yet you are talking of nothing else. Why not get to the point<br>Or if you want to pretend that I am another person —<br>A person that you have conspired to invent, please do so<br>In my absence. I shall be less embarrassing to you. Agatha? |
| AGATHA | I think, Harry, that having got so far —<br><br><br>If you want no pretences, let us have no pretences:<br>And you must try at once to make us understand,<br>And we must try to understand you. |
| HARRY | But how can I explain, how can I explain to you?<br><br><br>You will understand less after I have explained it.<br>All that I could hope to make you understand<br>Is only events: not what has happened.<br>And people to whom nothing has ever happened<br>Cannot understand the unimportance of events. |
| GERALD | Well, you can’t say that nothing has happened to me.<br><br><br>I started as a youngster on the North-West Frontier —<br>Been in tight corners most of my life<br>And some pretty nasty messes. |
| CHARLES | And there isn’t much would surprise me, Harry;<br><br><br>Or shock me, either. |
| HARRY | You are all people<br><br><br>To whom nothing has happened, at most a continual impact<br>Of external events. You have gone through life in sleep,<br>Never woken to the nightmare. I tell you, life would be unendurable<br>If you were wide awake. You do not know<br>The noxious smell untraceable in the drains,<br>Inaccessible to the plumbers, that has its hour of the night; you do not know<br><br><br>The unspoken voice of sorrow in the ancient bedroom<br>At three o’clock in the morning. I am not speaking<br>Of my own experience, but trying to give you<br>Comparisons in a more familiar medium. I am the old house<br>With the noxious smell and the sorrow before morning,<br>In which all past is present, all degradation<br>Is unredeemable. As for what happens —<br>Of the past you can only see what is past,<br>Not what is always present. That is what matters. |
| AGATHA | Nevertheless, Harry, best tell us as you can:<br><br><br>Talk in your own language, without stopping to debate<br>Whether it may be too far beyond our understanding. |
| HARRY | The sudden solitude in a crowded desert<br><br><br>In a thick smoke, many creatures moving<br>Without direction, for no direction<br>Leads anywhere but round and round in that vapour —<br>Without purpose, and without principle of conduct<br>In flickering intervals of light and darkness;<br>The partial anæsthesia of suffering without feeling<br>And partial observation of one’s own automatism<br>While the slow stain sinks deeper through the skin<br>Tainting the flesh and discolouring the bone —<br>This is what matters, but it is unspeakable,<br>Untranslatable: I talk in general terms<br>Because the particular has no language. One thinks to escape<br>By violence, but one is still alone<br>In an over-crowded desert, jostled by ghosts.<br>It was only reversing the senseless direction<br>For a momentary rest on the burning wheel<br>That cloudless night in the mid-Atlantic<br>When I pushed her over. |
| VIOLET | Pushed her? |
| HARRY | You would never imagine anyone could sink so quickly.<br><br><br>I had always supposed, wherever I went<br>That she would be with me; whatever I did<br>That she was unkillable. It was not like that.<br>Everything is true in a different sense.<br>I expected to find her when I went back to the cabin.<br>Later, I became excited, I think I made enquiries;<br>The purser and the steward were extremely sympathetic<br>And the doctor very attentive.<br>That night I slept heavily, alone. |
| AMY | Harry! |
| CHARLES | You mustn’t indulge such dangerous fancies.<br><br><br>It’s only doing harm to your mother and yourself.<br>Of course we know what really happened, we read it in the papers —<br>No need to revert to it. Remember, my boy,<br>I understand, your life together made it seem more horrible.<br>There’s a lot in my own past life that presses on my chest<br>When I wake, as I do now, early before morning.<br>I understand these feelings better than you know —<br>But you have no reason to reproach yourself.<br><br><br>Your conscience can be clear. |
| HARRY | It goes a good deal deeper<br><br><br>Than what people call their conscience; it is just the cancer<br>That eats away the self. I knew how you would take it.<br>First of all, you isolate the single event<br>As something so dreadful that it couldn’t have happened,<br>Because you could not bear it. So you must believe<br>That I suffer from delusions. It is not my conscience,<br>Not my mind, that is diseased, but the world I have to live in.<br>— I lay two days in contented drowsiness;<br>Then I recovered. I am afraid of sleep:<br>A condition in which one can be caught for the last time.<br>And also waking. She is nearer than ever.<br>The contamination has reached the marrow<br>And they are always near. Here, nearer than ever.<br><br><br>They are very close here. I had not expected that. |
| AMY | Harry, Harry, you are very tired<br><br><br>And overwrought. Coming so far<br>And making such haste, the change is too sudden for you.<br>You are unused to our foggy climate<br>And the northern country. When you see Wishwood<br>Again by day, all will be the same again.<br>I beg you to go now and rest before dinner.<br>Get Downing to draw you a hot bath,<br>And you will feel better. |
| AGATHA | There are certain points I do not yet understand:<br><br><br>They will be clear later. I am also convinced<br>That you only hold a fragment of the explanation.<br>It is only because of what you do not understand<br>That you feel the need to declare what you do.<br>There is more to understand: hold fast to that<br>As the way to freedom. |
| HARRY | I think I see what you mean,<br><br><br>Dimly — as you once explained the sobbing in the chimney<br>The evil in the dark closet, which they said was not there,<br>Which they explained away, but you explained them<br>Or at least, made me cease to be afraid of them.<br>I will go and have my bath. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Exit] |
| GERALD | God preserve us!<br><br><br>I never thought it would be as bad as this. |
| VIOLET | There is only one thing to be done:<br><br><br>Harry must see a doctor. |
| IVY | But I understand —<br><br><br>I have heard of such cases before — that people in his condition<br>Often betray the most immoderate resentment<br>At such a suggestion. They can be very cunning —<br>Their malady makes them so. They do not want to be cured<br>And they know what you are thinking. |
| CHARLES | He has probably let this notion grow in his mind,<br><br><br>Living among strangers, with no one to talk to.<br>I suspect it is simply that the wish to get rid of her<br>Makes him believe he did. He cannot trust his good fortune.<br>I believe that all he needs is someone to talk to,<br>To get it off his mind. I’ll have a talk to him tomorrow. |
