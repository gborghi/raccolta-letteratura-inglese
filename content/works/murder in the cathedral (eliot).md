### Part I

| Chi parla | Battuta |
|---|---|
| CHORUS | Here let us stand, close by the cathedral. Here let us wait.<br><br><br>Are we drawn by danger? Is it the knowledge of safety, that draws<br>        our feet<br>Towards the cathedral? What danger can be<br>For us, the poor, the poor women of Canterbury? what tribulation<br>With which we are not already familiar? There is no danger<br>For us, and there is no safety in the cathedral. Some presage of an<br>        act<br>Which our eyes are compelled to witness, has forced our feet<br>Towards the cathedral. We are forced to bear witness.<br>Since golden October declined into sombre November<br>And the apples were gathered and stored, and the land became<br>        brown sharp points of death in a waste of water and mud,<br>The New Year waits, breathes, waits, whispers in darkness.<br>While the labourer kicks off a muddy boot and stretches his hand<br>        to the fire,<br>The New Year waits, destiny waits for the coming.<br>Who has stretched out his hand to the fire and remembered the<br>        Saints at All Hallows,<br>Remembered the martyrs and saints who wait? and who shall<br>Stretch out his hand to the fire, and deny his master? who shall be<br>        warm<br>By the fire, and deny his master?<br>Seven years and the summer is over<br>Seven years since the Archbishop left us,<br>He who was always kind to his people.<br>But it would not be well if he should return.<br>King rules or barons rule;<br>We have suffered various oppression,<br>But mostly we are left to our own devices,<br>And we are content if we are left alone.<br>We try to keep our households in order;<br>The merchant, shy and cautious, tries to compile a little fortune,<br>And the labourer bends to his piece of earth, earth-colour, his own<br>        colour,<br>Preferring to pass unobserved.<br>Now I fear disturbance of the quiet seasons:<br>Winter shall come bringing death from the sea,<br>Ruinous spring shall beat at our doors,<br>Root and shoot shall eat our eyes and our ears,<br>Disastrous summer burn up the beds of our streams<br>And the poor shall wait for another decaying October.<br>Why should the summer bring consolation<br>For autumn fires and winter fogs?<br>What shall we do in the heat of summer<br>But wait in barren orchards for another October?<br>Some malady is coming upon us. We wait, we wait,<br>And the saints and martyrs wait, for those who shall be martyrs and<br>        saints.<br>Destiny waits in the hand of God, shaping the still unshapen:<br>I have seen these things in a shaft of sunlight.<br>Destiny waits in the hand of God, not in the hands of statesmen<br>Who do, some well, some ill, planning and guessing,<br>Having their aims which turn in their hands in the pattern of time.<br>Come, happy December, who shall observe you, who shall preserve<br>        you?<br>Shall the Son of Man be born again in the litter of scorn?<br>For us, the poor, there is no action,<br>But only to wait and to witness. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter PRIESTS] |
| FIRST PRIEST | Seven years and the summer is over.<br><br><br>Seven years since the Archbishop left us. |
| SECOND PRIEST | What does the Archbishop do, and our Sovereign<br><br><br>        Lord the Pope<br>With the stubborn King and the French King<br>In ceaseless intrigue, combinations,<br>In conference, meetings accepted, meetings refused,<br>Meetings unended or endless<br>At one place or another in France? |
| THIRD PRIEST | I see nothing quite conclusive in the art of temporal<br><br><br>        government,<br>But violence, duplicity and frequent malversation.<br>King rules or barons rule:<br>The strong man strongly and the weak man by caprice.<br>They have but one law, to seize the power and keep it,<br>And the steadfast can manipulate the greed and lust of others,<br>The feeble is devoured by his own. |
| FIRST PRIEST | Shall these things not end<br><br><br>Until the poor at the gate<br>Have forgotten their friend, their Father in God, have forgotten<br>That they had a friend? |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter MESSENGER] |
| MESSENGER | Servants of God, and watchers of the temple,<br><br><br>I am here to inform you, without circumlocution:<br>The Archbishop is in England, and is close outside the city.<br>I was sent before in haste<br>To give you notice of his coming, as much as was possible,<br>That you may prepare to meet him. |
| FIRST PRIEST | What, is the exile ended, is our Lord Archbishop<br><br><br>Reunited with the King? what reconciliation<br>Of two proud men? |
| THIRD PRIEST | What peace can be found<br><br><br>To grow between the hammer and the anvil? |
| SECOND PRIEST | Tell us,<br><br><br>Are the old disputes at an end, is the wall of pride cast down<br>That divided them? Is it peace or war? |
| FIRST PRIEST | Does he come<br><br><br>In full assurance, or only secure<br>In the power of Rome, the spiritual rule,<br>The assurance of right, and the love of the people? |
| MESSENGER | You are right to express a certain incredulity.<br><br><br>He comes in pride and sorrow, affirming all his claims,<br>Assured, beyond doubt, of the devotion of the people,<br>Who receive him with scenes of frenzied enthusiasm,<br>Lining the road and throwing down their capes,<br>Strewing the way with leaves and late flowers of the season.<br>The streets of the city will be packed to suffocation,<br>And I think that his horse will be deprived of its tail,<br>A single hair of which becomes a precious relic.<br>He is at one with the Pope, and with the King of France,<br>Who indeed would have liked to detain him in his kingdom:<br>But as for our King, that is another matter. |
| FIRST PRIEST | But again, is it war or peace? |
| MESSENGER | Peace, but not the kiss of peace.<br><br><br>A patched up affair, if you ask my opinion.<br>And if you ask me, I think the Lord Archbishop<br>Is not the man to cherish any illusions,<br>Or yet to diminish the least of his pretensions.<br>If you ask my opinion, I think that this peace<br>Is nothing like an end, or like a beginning.<br>It is common knowledge that when the Archbishop<br>Parted from the King, he said to the King,<br>My Lord, he said, I leave you as a man<br>Whom in this life I shall not see again.<br>I have this, I assure you, on the highest authority;<br>There are several opinions as to what he meant,<br>But no one considers it a happy prognostic. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Exit] |
| FIRST PRIEST | I fear for the Archbishop, I fear for the Church,<br><br><br>I know that the pride bred of sudden prosperity<br>Was but confirmed by bitter adversity.<br>I saw him as Chancellor, flattered by the King.<br>Liked or feared by courtiers, in their overbearing fashion,<br>Despised and despising, always isolated,<br>Never one among them, always insecure;<br>His pride always feeding upon his own virtues,<br>Pride drawing sustenance from impartiality,<br>Pride drawing sustenance from generosity,<br>Loathing power given by temporal devolution,<br>Wishing subjection to God alone.<br>Had the King been greater, or had he been weaker<br>Things had perhaps been different for Thomas. |
| SECOND PRIEST | Yet our lord is returned. Our lord has come back to his own again.<br><br><br>We have had enough of waiting, from December to dismal December.<br>The Archbishop shall be at our head, dispelling dismay and doubt.<br>He will tell us what we are to do, he will give us our orders, instruct us.<br>Our Lord is at one with the Pope, and also the King of France.<br>We can lean on a rock, we can feel a firm foothold<br>Against the perpetual wash of tides of balance of forces of barons and landholders.<br>The rock of God is beneath our feet. Let us meet the Archbishop with cordial thanksgiving:<br>Our lord, our Archbishop returns. And when the Archbishop returns<br>Our doubts are dispelled. Let us therefore rejoice,<br>I say rejoice, and show a glad face for his welcome.<br>I am the Archbishop’s man. Let us give the Archbishop welcome! |
| THIRD PRIEST | For good or ill, let the wheel turn.<br><br><br>The wheel has been still, these seven years, and no good.<br>For ill or good, let the wheel turn.<br>For who knows the end of good or evil?<br>Until the grinders cease<br>And the door shall be shut in the street,<br>And all the daughters of music shall be brought low. |
| CHORUS | Here is no continuing city, here is no abiding stay.<br><br><br>Ill the wind, ill the time, uncertain the profit, certain the danger.<br>O late late late, late is the time, late too late, and rotten the year;<br>Evil the wind, and bitter the sea, and grey the sky, grey grey grey.<br>O Thomas, return, Archbishop; return, return to France.<br>Return. Quickly. Quietly. Leave us to perish in quiet.<br>You come with applause, you come with rejoicing, but you come bringing death into Canterbury:<br>A doom on the house, a doom on yourself, a doom on the world.<br>We do not wish anything to happen.<br>Seven years we have lived quietly,<br>Succeeded in avoiding notice,<br>Living and partly living.<br>There have been oppression and luxury,<br>There have been poverty and licence,<br>There has been minor injustice.<br>Yet we have gone on living,<br>Living and partly living.<br>Sometimes the corn has failed us,<br>Sometimes the harvest is good,<br>One year is a year of rain,<br>Another a year of dryness,<br>One year the apples are abundant,<br>Another year the plums are lacking.<br>Yet we have gone on living,<br>Living and partly living.<br>We have kept the feasts, heard the masses,<br>We have brewed beer and cider,<br>Gathered wood against the winter,<br>Talked at the corner of the fire,<br>Talked at the corners of streets,<br>Talked not always in whispers,<br>Living and partly living.<br>We have seen births, deaths and marriages,<br>We have had various scandals,<br>We have been afflicted with taxes,<br>We have had laughter and gossip,<br>Several girls have disappeared<br>Unaccountably, and some not able to.<br>We have all had our private terrors,<br>Our particular shadows, our secret fears.<br>But now a great fear is upon us, a fear not of one but of many,<br>A fear like birth and death, when we see birth and death alone<br>In a void apart. We<br>Are afraid in a fear which we cannot know, which we cannot face,<br>        which none understands,<br>And our hearts are torn from us, our brains unskinned like the<br>        layers of an onion, our selves are lost lost<br>In a final fear which none understands. O Thomas Archbishop,<br>O Thomas our Lord, leave us and leave us be, in our humble and<br>        tarnished frame of existence, leave us; do not ask us<br>To stand to the doom on the house, the doom on the Archbishop,<br>        the doom on the world.<br>Archbishop, secure and assured of your fate, unaffrayed among the<br>        shades, do you realise what you ask, do you realise what it<br>        means<br>To the small folk drawn into the pattern of fate, the small folk who<br>        live among small things.<br>The strain on the brain of the small folk who stand to the doom of<br>        the house, the doom of their lord, the doom of the world?<br>O Thomas, Archbishop, leave us, leave us, leave sullen Dover, and<br>        set sail for France. Thomas our Archbishop still<br>        our Archbishop even in France. Thomas Archbishop, set the<br>        white sail between the grey sky and the bitter sea, leave<br>        us, leave us for France. |
| SECOND PRIEST | What a way to talk at such a juncture!<br><br><br>You are foolish, immodest and babbling women.<br>Do you not know that the good Archbishop<br>Is likely to arrive at any moment?<br>The crowds in the streets will be cheering and cheering,<br>You go on croaking like frogs in the treetops:<br>But frogs at least can be cooked and eaten.<br>Whatever you are afraid of, in your craven apprehension,<br>Let me ask you at the least to put on pleasant faces,<br>And give a hearty welcome to our good Archbishop. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter THOMAS] |
| THOMAS | Peace. And let them be, in their exaltation.<br><br><br>They speak better than they know, and beyond your understanding.<br>They know and do not know, what it is to act or suffer.<br>They know and do not know, that action is suffering<br>And suffering is action. Neither does the agent suffer<br>Nor the patient act. But both are fixed<br>In an eternal action, an eternal patience<br>To which all must consent that it may be willed<br>And which all must suffer that they may will it,<br>That the pattern may subsist, for the pattern is the action<br>And the suffering, that the wheel may turn and still<br>Be forever still. |
| SECOND PRIEST | O my Lord, forgive me, I did not see you coming,<br><br><br>Engrossed by the chatter of these foolish women.<br>Forgive us, my Lord, you would have had a better welcome<br>If we had been sooner prepared for the event.<br>But your Lordship knows that seven years of waiting,<br>Seven years of prayer, seven years of emptiness,<br>Have better prepared our hearts for your coming,<br>Than seven days could make ready Canterbury.<br>However, I will have fires laid in all your rooms<br>To take the chill off our English December,<br>Your Lordship now being used to a better climate.<br>Your Lordship will find your rooms in order as you left them. |
| THOMAS | And will try to leave them in order as I find them.<br><br><br>I am more than grateful for all your kind attentions.<br>These are small matters. Little rest in Canterbury<br>With eager enemies restless about us.<br>Rebellious bishops, York, London, Salisbury,<br>Would have intercepted our letters,<br>Filled the coast with spies and sent to meet me<br>Some who hold me in bitterest hate.<br>By God’s grace aware of their prevision<br>I sent my letters on another day,<br>Had fair crossing, found at Sandwich<br>Broc, Warenne, and the Sheriff of Kent,<br>Those who had sworn to have my head from me<br>Only John, the Dean of Salisbury,<br>Fearing for the King’s name, warning against treason,<br>Made them hold their hands. So for the time<br>We are unmolested. |
| FIRST PRIEST | But do they follow after? |
| THOMAS | For a little time the hungry hawk<br><br><br>Will only soar and hover, circling lower,<br>Waiting excuse, pretence, opportunity.<br>End will be simple, sudden, God-given.<br>Meanwhile the substance of our first act<br>Will be shadows, and the strife with shadows.<br>Heavier the interval than the consummation.<br>All things prepare the event. Watch. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter FIRST TEMPTER] |
| FIRST TEMPTER | You see, my Lord, I do not wait upon ceremony:<br><br><br>Here I have come, forgetting all acrimony,<br>Hoping that your present gravity<br>Will find excuse for my humble levity<br>Remembering all the good time past.<br>Your Lordship won’t despise an old friend out of favour?<br>Old Tom, gay Tom, Becket of London,<br>Your Lordship won’t forget that evening on the river<br>When the King, and you and I were all friends together?<br>Friendship should be more than biting Time can sever.<br>What, my Lord, now that you recover<br>Favour with the King, shall we say that summer’s over<br>Or that the good time cannot last?<br>Fluting in the meadows, viols in the hall,<br>Laughter and apple-blossom floating on the water,<br>Singing at nightfall, whispering in chambers,<br>Fires devouring the winter season,<br>Eating up the darkness, with wit and wine and wisdom!<br>Now that the King and you are in amity,<br>Clergy and laity may return to gaiety,<br>Mirth and sportfulness need not walk warily. |
| THOMAS | You talk of seasons that are past. I remember<br><br><br>Not worth forgetting. |
| TEMPTER | And of the new season.<br><br><br>Spring has come in winter. Snow in the branches<br>Shall float as sweet as blossoms. Ice along the ditches<br>Mirror the sunlight. Love in the orchard<br>Send the sap shooting. Mirth matches melancholy. |
| THOMAS | We do not know very much of the future<br><br><br>Except that from generation to generation<br>The same things happen again and again.<br>Men learn little from others’ experience.<br>But in the life of one man, never<br>The same time returns. Sever<br>The cord, shed the scale. Only<br>The fool, fixed in his folly, may think<br>He can turn the wheel on which he turns. |
| TEMPTER | My Lord, a nod is as good as a wink.<br><br><br>A man will often love what he spurns.<br>For the good times past, that are come again<br>I am your man. |
| THOMAS | Not in this train<br><br><br>Look to your behaviour. You were safer<br>Think of penitence and follow your master. |
| TEMPTER | Not at this gait!<br><br><br>If you go so fast, others may go faster.<br>Your Lordship is too proud!<br>The safest beast is not the one that roars most loud,<br>This was not the way of the King our master!<br>You were not used to be so hard upon sinners<br>When they were your friends. Be easy, man!<br>The easy man lives to eat the best dinners.<br>Take a friend’s advice. Leave well alone,<br>Or your goose may be cooked and eaten to the bone. |
| THOMAS | You come twenty years too late. |
| TEMPTER | Then I leave you to your fate.<br>I leave you to the pleasures of your higher vices,<br>Which will have to be paid for at higher prices.<br>Farewell, my Lord, I do not wait upon ceremony,<br>I leave as I came, forgetting all acrimony,<br>Hoping that your present gravity<br>Will find excuse for my humble levity.<br>If you will remember me, my Lord, at your prayers,<br>I’ll remember you at kissing-time below the stairs. |
| THOMAS | Leave-well-alone, the springtime fancy,<br><br><br>So one thought goes whistling down the wind.<br>The impossible is still temptation.<br>The impossible, the undesirable,<br>Voices under sleep, waking a dead world,<br>So that the mind may not be whole in the present. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter SECOND TEMPTER] |
| SECOND TEMPTER | Your Lordship has forgotten me, perhaps. I will remind you.<br><br><br>We met at Clarendon, at Northampton,<br>And last at Montmirail, in Maine. Now that I have recalled them,<br>Let us but set these not too pleasant memories<br>In balance against other, earlier<br>And weightier ones: those of the Chancellorship.<br>See how the late ones rise! You, master of policy<br>Whom all acknowledged, should guide the state again. |
| THOMAS | Your meaning? |
| TEMPTER | The Chancellorship that you resigned<br><br><br>When you were made Archbishop — that was a mistake<br>On your part — still may be regained. Think, my Lord,<br>Power obtained grows to glory,<br>Life lasting, a permanent possession.<br>A templed tomb, monument of marble.<br>Rule over men reckon no madness. |
| THOMAS | To the man of God what gladness? |
| TEMPTER | Sadness<br><br><br>Only to those giving love to God alone.<br>Shall he who held the solid substance<br>Wander waking with deceitful shadows?<br>Power is present. Holiness hereafter. |
| THOMAS | Who then? |
| TEMPTER | The Chancellor, King and Chancellor.<br>King commands. Chancellor richly rules.<br>This is a sentence not taught in the schools.<br>To set down the great, protect the poor,<br>Beneath the throne of God can man do more?<br>Disarm the ruffian, strengthen the laws,<br>Rule for the good of the better cause,<br>Dispensing justice make all even,<br>Is thrive on earth, and perhaps in heaven. |
| THOMAS | What means? |
| TEMPTER | Real power<br><br><br>Is purchased at price of a certain submission.<br>Your spiritual power is earthly perdition.<br>Power is present, for him who will wield. |
| THOMAS | Who shall have it? |
| TEMPTER | He who will come. |
| THOMAS | What shall be the month? |
| TEMPTER | The last from the first. |
| THOMAS | What shall we give for it? |
| TEMPTER | Pretence of priestly power. |
| THOMAS | Why should we give it? |
| TEMPTER | For the power and the glory. |
| THOMAS | No! |
| TEMPTER | Yes! Or bravery will be broken,<br><br><br>Cabined in Canterbury, realmless ruler,<br>Self-bound servant of a powerless Pope,<br>The old stag, circled with hounds. |
| THOMAS | No! |
| TEMPTER | Yes! men must manœuvre. Monarchs also,<br><br><br>Waging war abroad, need fast friends at home.<br>Private policy is public profit;<br>Dignity still shall be dressed with decorum. |
| THOMAS | You forget the bishops<br><br><br>Whom I have laid under excommunication. |
| TEMPTER | Hungry hatred<br><br><br>Will not strive against intelligent self-interest. |
| THOMAS | You forget the barons. Who will not forget<br><br><br>Constant curbing of petty privilege. |
| TEMPTER | Against the barons<br><br><br>Is King’s cause, churl’s cause, Chancellor’s cause. |
| THOMAS | No! shall I, who keep the keys<br>Of heaven and hell, supreme alone in England,<br>Who bind and loose, with power from the Pope,<br>Descend to desire a punier power?<br>Delegate to deal the doom of damnation,<br>To condemn kings, not serve among their servants,<br>Is my open office. No! Go. |
| TEMPTER | Then I leave you to your fate.<br><br><br>Your sin soars sunward, covering kings’ falcons. |
| THOMAS | Temporal power, to build a good world,<br><br><br>To keep order, as the world knows order.<br>Those who put their faith in worldly order<br>Not controlled by the order of God,<br>In confident ignorance, but arrest disorder,<br>Make it fast, breed fatal disease,<br>Degrade what they exalt. Power with the King —<br>I was the King, his arm, his better reason.<br><br><br>But what was once exaltation<br>Would now be only mean descent. |
| *(didascalia)* | [Enter THIRD TEMPTER] |
| THIRD TEMPTER | I am an unexpected visitor. |
| THOMAS | I expected you. |
| TEMPTER | But not in this guise, or for my present purpose. |
| THOMAS | No purpose brings surprise. |
| TEMPTER | Well, my Lord,<br><br><br>I am no trifler, and no politician.<br>To idle or intrigue at court<br>I have no skill. I am no courtier.<br>I know a horse, a dog, a wench;<br>I know how to hold my estates in order,<br>A country-keeping lord who minds his own business.<br>It is we country lords who know the country<br>And we who know what the country needs.<br>It is our country. We care for the country.<br>We are the backbone of the nation.<br>We, not the plotting parasites<br>About the King. Excuse my bluntness:<br>I am a rough straightforward Englishman. |
| THOMAS | Proceed straight forward. |
| TEMPTER | Purpose is plain.<br><br><br>Endurance of friendship does not depend<br>Upon ourselves, but upon circumstance.<br>But circumstance is not undetermined.<br>Unreal friendship may turn to real<br>But real friendship, once ended, cannot be mended.<br>Sooner shall enmity turn to alliance.<br>The enmity that never knew friendship<br>Can sooner know accord. |
| THOMAS | For a countryman<br><br><br>You wrap your meaning in as dark generality<br>As any courtier. |
| TEMPTER | This is the simple fact!<br><br><br>You have no hope of reconciliation<br>With Henry the King. You look only<br>To blind assertion in isolation.<br>That is a mistake. |
| THOMAS | O Henry, O my King! |

I wish only that you should meditate in your hearts the deep meaning and mystery of our masses of Christmas Day. For whenever Mass is said, we re-enact the Passion and Death of Our Lord; and on this Christmas Day we do this in celebration of His Birth. So that at the same moment we rejoice in His coming for the salvation of men, and offer again to God His Body and Blood in sacrifice, oblation and satisfaction for the sins of the whole world. It was in this same night that has just passed, that a multitude of the heavenly host appeared before the shepherds at Bethlehem, saying ‘Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to men of good will’; at this same time of all the year that we celebrate at once the Birth of Our Lord and His Passion and Death upon the Cross. Beloved, as the World sees, this is to behave in a strange fashion. For who in the World will both mourn and rejoice at once and for the same reason? For either joy will be overborne by mourning, or mourning will be cast out by joy; so it is only in these our Christian mysteries that we can rejoice and mourn at once for the same reason. Now think for a moment about the meaning of this word ‘peace’. Does it seem strange to you that the angels should have announced Peace, when ceaselessly the world has been stricken with War and the fear of War? Does it seem to you that the angelic voices were mistaken, and that the promise was a disappointment and a cheat?<br><br><br>Reflect now, how Our Lord Himself spoke of Peace. He said to His disciples, ‘Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you.’ Did He mean peace as we think of it: the kingdom of England at peace with its neighbours, the barons at peace with the King, the householder counting over his peaceful gains, the swept hearth, his best wine for a friend at the table, his wife singing to the children? Those men His disciples knew no such things: they went forth to journey afar, to suffer by land and sea, to know torture, imprisonment, disappointment, to suffer death by martyrdom. What then did He mean? If you ask that, remember then that He said also, ‘Not as the world gives, give I unto you.’ So then, He gave to His disciples peace, but not peace as the world gives.<br><br><br>Consider also one thing of which you have probably never thought. Not only do we at the feast of Christmas celebrate at once Our Lord’s Birth and His Death: but on the next day we celebrate the martyrdom of His first martyr, the blessed Stephen. Is it an accident, do you think, that the day of the first martyr follows immediately the day of the Birth of Christ? By no means. Just as we rejoice and mourn at once, in the Birth and in the Passion of Our Lord; so also, in a smaller figure, we both rejoice and mourn in the death of martyrs. We mourn, for the sins of the world that has martyred them; we rejoice, that another soul is numbered among the Saints in Heaven, for the glory of God and for the salvation of men.<br><br><br>Beloved, we do not think of a martyr simply as a good Christian who has been killed because he is a Christian: for that would be solely to mourn. We do not think of him simply as a good Christian who has been elevated to the company of the Saints: for that would be simply to rejoice: and neither our mourning nor our rejoicing is as the world’s is. A Christian martyrdom is never an accident, for Saints are not made by accident. Still less is a Christian martyrdom the effect of a man’s will to become a Saint, as a man by willing and contriving may become a ruler of men. A martyrdom is always the design of God, for His love of men, to warn them and to lead them, to bring them back to His ways. It is never the design of man; for the true martyr is he who has become the instrument of God, who has lost his will in the will of God, and who no longer desires anything for himself, not even the glory of being a martyr. So thus as on earth the Church mourns and rejoices at once, in a fashion that the world cannot understand; so in Heaven the Saints are most high, having made themselves most low, and are seen, not as we see them, but in the light of the Godhead from which they draw their being.<br><br><br>I have spoken to you to-day, dear children of God, of the martyrs of the past, asking you to remember especially our martyr of Canterbury, the blessed Archbishop Elphege; because it is fitting, on Christ’s birth day, to remember what is that Peace which He brought; and because, dear children, I do not think I shall ever preach to you again; and because it is possible that in a short time you may have yet another martyr, and that one perhaps not the last. I would have you keep in your hearts these words that I say, and think of them at another time. In the Name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. Amen. |
